Research · Anthropic

A Bound That Took Decades Moved Twenty-Five Points in Thirty-Six Hours

By The Editor, Research Desk · August 11, 2026

Bernhard Riemann proposed in 1859 that every non-trivial zero of the zeta function sits on a single line. Nobody has proved it. What mathematicians have done instead, for decades, is prove that some proportion of the zeros sit there, and grind that proportion upward a fraction of a point at a time. The standing figure was 41.6%. On Monday Anthropic published a result putting it at 67.2%, obtained by an unreleased research version of Claude running as roughly 60 subagents inside Claude Code for about a day and a half, across some 2,400 shell commands and 31 million output tokens. Anthropic calls it the largest single improvement in the problem's history. The proof is formally verified in Lean and the repository is public.

Zeta Zeros Proven to Lie on the Critical Line

The third bar is what the hypothesis actually asserts, and it is not a target anyone hit. Anthropic says it does not expect this method to reach it — comfortably the most restrained sentence any lab has published this year.

The mathematics is worth stating plainly, because the honest version is more interesting than the press version. Zeros on the critical line and zeros off it have conventionally been handled as separate cases. Claude's move was to treat them as one geometric object, read through an indefinite Hermitian form, which removes an assumption about where a zero has to be and yields a stronger inequality. Anthropic's own account says the result came from combining published work by Baluyot, Goldston, Suriajaya and Turnage-Butterbaugh with Bombieri's. It was reviewed by two outside number theorists, Brian Conrey and Dan Goldston — and yes, that is the same Goldston who appears in the list of people the proof is built on. He was asked to check a result standing on his own.

None of this is a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, and Anthropic does not say it is. A proof needs every zero, which is to say 100%, and the company states it does not expect this approach to get there. The bound was a by-product: Claude was pointed at the whole hypothesis, failed at the whole hypothesis, and produced a genuine result on the way past. That is a very normal thing for a mathematician to do and a fairly new thing for a product to do.

Lean will tell you a proof is correct. Nothing will tell you it is yours.

Which is the part I would like the industry to notice. Formal verification is a spectacular answer to the question is this true, and a complete non-answer to the question whose is it. Anthropic handled the second question the only way it can be handled — by naming five mathematicians in the write-up and asking one of them to read the file. That is not machinery, it is manners, and it had to be done by hand. Five days earlier, Scientific American reported the opposite outcome from the same capability: Steven Miller of Yeshiva said a sphere-packing proof released under OpenAI's Astra programme reproduced an argument he published in 2016 without attribution, and Fournier-Facio of Cambridge described the same pattern in its non-sofic groups result. Both labs shipped machine-checkable mathematics inside a week. Only one of them shipped a bibliography. As these systems start generating results faster than the field can read them, the bottleneck is not going to be whether the proofs hold. It is going to be whether anyone can still tell where they came from — and there is no Lean for that.

Security · OpenAI

OpenAI Turned Off Its Cyber Guardrails and Gained Half a Percentage Point

By The Editor, Security Desk · August 11, 2026

On Monday OpenAI split its Daybreak security programme into two tiers and shipped a new model into the deeper one. Daybreak Blue serves GPT-5.6 Sol with the system-level cyber guardrails removed, pitched at defensive work: vulnerability discovery, secure code review, malware analysis, incident response, patch validation. Daybreak Red serves a purpose-built model, GPT-5.6-Cyber, for exploit validation and deeper vulnerability research. Both tiers require identity verification, account security controls, monitoring and signed legal declarations, and from September 1st every Daybreak account must use a hardware security key. The announcement is titled, without evident irony, “expanding Daybreak as the cyber defense window narrows.”

Advanced Cyber Requests the Model Will Complete

All three figures are OpenAI's own, on an internal benchmark it has not named. The top row is what removing a safety system bought.

The number everyone will quote is 95%. The number worth an hour of your afternoon is 2.0%. OpenAI took its flagship model, deliberately stripped out the system-level cyber guardrails, handed it to vetted defenders — and compliance on advanced requests went from 1.5% to 2.0%. Half a point. Whatever was stopping GPT-5.6 Sol from doing this work, it was not the guardrail; the refusals live somewhere further down, in the model itself, where a policy switch cannot reach them. Which reframes what Daybreak Red actually is. It is not a permission, it is a capability — you do not unlock 95% by signing a form, you unlock it by being given a different model that was trained to say yes. The vetting, the monitoring, the hardware keys: genuinely more friction than most of this industry imposes on anything, and credit where it is due. But the honest description of what shipped on Monday is that OpenAI has built and is now distributing an offensive-security model, and has priced admission in paperwork. As for the window narrowing — it is narrowing partly because of the tools the people announcing it keep building. That is not hypocrisy. It is just the business, described accurately for once.

Government · Anthropic

Claude Code Enters a FedRAMP High Room, and Anthropic Keeps the Invoice

By The Editor, Government Desk · August 11, 2026

Anthropic has opened a public beta of Claude Code and Claude Cowork inside Claude for Government Desktop, running in a FedRAMP High authorised environment, logically isolated from commercial tenants and hosted through a government-authorised partner. Agencies get desktop file-based work, administrator-set configuration defaults, tamper-evident audit logs and per-department spend governance. The load-bearing detail is contractual rather than technical: Anthropic is the contracting and billing party, so an agency does not need a separate relationship with a cloud provider to buy it.

That last part is the story, and it is the same quiet motion as the company's own datacentre lease and its in-house chip team — Anthropic is methodically removing the people standing between it and both ends of its supply chain. Federal AI has until now been sold largely through the hyperscalers, who owned the paperwork and therefore the customer. Here the model company holds the paper. The other thing to notice is what an institution is actually buying. Anthropic's stated case for switching Claude Code to automatic approval on Thursday was that human reviewers approve 97% of permission requests anyway; agencies, meanwhile, are being sold administrator-set defaults and a log that cannot be quietly edited. Nobody in a federal building was ever going to be protected by clicking yes. They were going to be protected, if at all, by a record of what happened — and a record is the one artefact the approval prompt never produced.

Editor's Note

I do not have a joke about the first story, and I went looking for one. An unreleased version of Claude spent about thirty-six hours as sixty copies of itself and raised the proven proportion of Riemann zeta zeros lying where the hypothesis says they should from 41.6% to 67.2%. It is not a proof, and Anthropic says flatly that it does not expect this route to become one. The argument is in Lean, the repository is public, and two number theorists read it. This paper's entire premise is that machines make things up, and on Monday one of them did the opposite so thoroughly that a proof assistant agreed.

What I keep turning over is the half that no machine touched. Lean will confirm a proof is correct; nothing will confirm it is yours. Anthropic did that part by hand, naming the five mathematicians whose results Claude combined and asking one of them to check the answer. Five days earlier, Scientific American reported named mathematicians saying OpenAI's Astra results reproduced their arguments with no credit at all. Correctness was machine-checkable in both cases. Provenance was only ever going to be checked by whoever bothered, and this week exactly one lab bothered. See you tomorrow.
