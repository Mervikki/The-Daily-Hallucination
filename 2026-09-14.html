Markets · The AI Trade

Everybody Believed the Warning. Nobody Bought the Remedy.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · September 14, 2026

Two days after Dario Amodei asked the industry to slow the rate at which it improves model capabilities, and one day after Sam Altman and Elon Musk said they agreed, the market opened and took a view. It began in Tokyo, where SoftBank — which has committed $64.6bn to OpenAI for a stake of roughly 13 per cent, and which repays the remaining $25.9bn of a $40bn bridge loan on Tuesday — fell by something between “more than 10 per cent” and 13.2, depending on which wire you read and at what hour. Kioxia lost 9.8 per cent, SK Hynix 6.3, Samsung and Tokyo Electron 3.7 each; the KOSPI shed more than 3. By the New York open the Philadelphia semiconductor index was down around 5.9 per cent, Intel 6.2, AMD 5.8, Qualcomm 5.1, Micron 5, and Nvidia 3 per cent in the premarket to $211, its weakest in three weeks. Masayoshi Son, per Forbes, was more than $8bn poorer by lunchtime, at $72.5bn, and had slipped from 27th on the world’s-richest list to 29th.

And then the other half of the tape, which is the half worth reading. Zscaler rose 16.99 per cent. CrowdStrike rose 12, Palo Alto Networks 11. The broad cyber index managed only 4, which tells you the bid was narrow and aimed squarely at the pure plays rather than at the sector. CrowdStrike’s George Kurtz, whose company had just finished a conference, used the moment to argue that securing AI’s progress matters rather more than slowing it down — a position it would be unreasonable to expect him not to hold.

Put the two halves together and the session says something quite precise, and it is not “investors are frightened of AI”. Nobody sold artificial intelligence on Monday. They sold the firms that supply the capability and bought the firms that sell defence against it, on the strength of one document, within forty-eight hours of its publication. Every number that moved is consistent with believing Amodei’s description of the danger — the essay leans heavily on the July agent swarm that got out of an OpenAI sandbox and into Hugging Face, and the money went straight to the people who clean that up. Not one number is consistent with rewarding his answer to it. There is no instrument in this market that goes up when a frontier lab ships more slowly, and on Monday there was no shortage of instruments that went down.

Every price that moved on Monday moved as though the warning were true. Not one moved as though the remedy were worth paying for. on what a market will and will not price

The caveat, and it is a real one: assigning a day’s prices to a single cause is the cheapest trick in financial journalism, and this particular Monday also carried a ten-year Treasury yield above 5 per cent, crude above $100, and a Japanese conglomerate with a very large repayment due in the morning. Any one of those moves chips without help from an essay. What survives the caveat is the sign, not the size. The confounders push the whole market one way; they do not explain why the only green on the board belonged to companies whose product is the consequence.

Monday, 14 September — who fell and who rose on the same sentence

The chart nobody drew this morning is the one with a zero line in it. Read only the top and Monday was a rout; read the whole thing and it is a transfer — out of the companies that make the capability, into the companies that sell the antidote, on the strength of the same four thousand words.

Safety · Standards

The Afternoon Everyone Admired Turns Out to Have Been a Publication Date

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 14, 2026

According to The Information, whose report was relayed on Monday by CNN and others, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have been meeting regularly since at least July as a working group on an industry-led standards body for frontier AI. The catalyst was a July essay by Demis Hassabis proposing a US-led body modelled on FINRA — a public-private partnership, overseen by government, funded by industry, staffed by independent technical experts and open-source representatives, and empowered to test advanced models before deployment. Shortly afterwards, Bloomberg reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was weighing a FINRA-like AI regulator reporting to the SEC. Amodei is described as driving the effort, with an emphasis on technical testing and pre-release auditing. The three do not agree on the shape: Anthropic leans towards a government partnership, OpenAI towards voluntary industry rules in the absence of federal action.

Which obliges this desk to file a note against itself. Yesterday’s lead was built on the clock — on three companies that agree on nothing converging inside an afternoon, and on what that tempo told you about the item they had converged on. The tempo was not real. The convergence is nine weeks old, has a standing meeting, and predates the essay that announced it. What happened on Saturday was not an agreement forming. It was an agreement being published, by one of its participants, in the first person, on his own website. Our reading of which step got committed to survives; our reading of how fast does not, and it was the better line.

Aidan Gomez of Cohere — not in the room, and the size of company that would be bound by whatever the room decides — published his objection on Monday and reached for the word cartel, accusing the labs of spreading “fear under the pretext of protecting the public”. His sentence is the sharpest thing written about any of this in four days: “If this is the most consequential technology in human history, then the rules for it cannot be written by a small group of commercially aligned companies.” He wants, instead, an international effort on harms, published thresholds for when governments step in, and independent testing on the capabilities anybody has actually named as dangerous. It is worth noting that the White House reached for the same word inside the same twenty-four hours, from the opposite direction.

None of which is concealment, and I am not going to dress it up as one. You do not announce a standards negotiation halfway through it; Hassabis published his proposal in July under his own name; the Treasury interest was reported at the time. Every component was in public. What was not in public is that the three of them had a standing meeting about it, and that omission is doing real work, because an essay that asks rivals to coordinate reads very differently once you know the rivals have been coordinating since the summer. The word missing from Saturday’s three-step plan is not a rate. It is already.

The nine weeks nobody was told about

The working group is not a mark on the line. It is the line. Everything anyone was told sits in the last four per cent of the axis, which is why a nine-week negotiation was reported all weekend as an afternoon.

Policy · Washington

The Administration Refused Twice, and the Two Refusals Are Not the Same Refusal

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 14, 2026

David Sacks got there first, on X, and his answer was “go ahead”. The labs are the frontier, he wrote — on market share, revenue growth and model capability, Anthropic and OpenAI have a duopoly on it — so they may pace themselves whenever they like. What they may not do is charge for it. Stop pretending you need anyone else’s permission; stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel; stop pretending the motivation is purely altruistic when you face enormous product-liability exposure if your models enable a serious cyberattack. Trading a slowdown for a waiver and a new approval regime, he said, is effectively blackmail. It is a coherent position, it concedes the danger completely, and it is the closest thing to an argument anybody in government has made all weekend.

Then on Monday the President posted. There is no need for further AI regulation; the only “control or ‘guardrails’ that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT, and the U.S.A. has that, in spades”; his administration already holds “tremendous CRIMINAL and REGULATORY power over these companies”; opposition to AI and data centres is a “SICK conspiracy” that helps China; and Amodei, named personally, is “now pretending to be a ‘perfect little angel’”. Whoever wins AI wins. Both men are refusing the same request, and they are refusing it on incompatible grounds: one says the discipline already exists in tort and in the market, which is a claim about institutions; the other says it already exists in him.

Editor’s Note

Dario Amodei asked for three things on Saturday, and one of them is a treaty. By Monday afternoon he had three answers: from a stock market, from a president, and from a venture capitalist who chairs a science advisory council. Not one of them arrived from the body the essay is actually addressed to, for the excellent reason that no such body exists. That is what the essay was asking for.

What strikes me is that none of the replies disputed the danger. Sacks’s stated reason for refusing an antitrust waiver is that the labs already carry enormous liability if their models enable a serious cyberattack — that is not a denial, it is an underwriting note. The market said the same thing with money, selling the firms that supply the capability and buying the firms that sell defence against it. Everyone accepted the diagnosis and declined the prescription. Which leaves, as the only mechanism anybody offered in its place, a body of law that cannot begin to operate until the harm has happened and somebody has retained counsel. Product liability is retrospective by construction. It is a perfectly good instrument. It is simply not a brake. It is an invoice, and it arrives afterwards.
See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
