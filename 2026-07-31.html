Markets · Big Tech

Amazon Had Its Best Quarter Ever. Most of the Profit Was a Number Anthropic Set.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · July 31, 2026

Amazon reported after Thursday's close and the headline number was enormous: net income of $62.6 billion, more than triple a year earlier, on revenue of $200.6 billion. Read past the headline and most of that profit did not come from selling anything. The quarter included a $53.4 billion pre-tax gain that was, in the main, an accounting revaluation of Amazon's roughly 21% stake in Anthropic — the private lab's rising valuation, marked to market and booked as Amazon's income. Amazon did not sell the stake, ship a product, or close a customer to earn it. Anthropic raised money at a higher price, and a line on Amazon's income statement moved.

The genuinely operational AI story was quieter and sat one line down. AWS grew 37% to $42.2 billion, its fastest in eighteen quarters and well ahead of the ~31% the Street expected; Amazon's homegrown-chip and AI units each cleared a $25 billion annualized run rate. Andy Jassy raised 2026 capital-spending guidance from $200 billion to $220 billion and said even that won't meet demand this year. That is the part of the report that describes a business — compute sold by the hour, chips fabbed, a capex bill with customers standing behind it. It is also the part nobody led with, because a 37% growth rate is a smaller-looking number than $53 billion.

A Paper Gain Bigger Than a Quarter of AWS

Amazon's $53.4 billion non-operating gain — largely the Anthropic revaluation — outran AWS's entire $42.2 billion of quarterly revenue. One is a mark; the other is a business.

Amazon's best quarter turned on a price Anthropic set at its last funding round — a number Amazon books but does not control.

The mark-to-market rule is doing something strange to the income statement. Amazon's reported profit now swings on Anthropic's valuation, which is set by Anthropic's next round and the investors who price it — not by anything Amazon operates. Written up, the stake hands Amazon a record; a flat or down round would hand it a paper loss it also didn't cause. The boom has reached the point where a bookseller's most profitable quarter is, in the arithmetic, a footnote to a chatbot company's cap table.

Markets · Apple

Apple Set Records on Every Line and Fell 6% Anyway.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · July 31, 2026

Apple reported June-quarter revenue of $109.4 billion, up 16%, with earnings of $2.02 a share and quarterly records across iPhone, Mac and Services. iPhone sales rose about 22%; gross margin held above 50%, flattered by roughly two points of tariff refunds. The stock fell about 6% after hours anyway, on soft current-quarter guidance the company pinned on “supply constraints.” In a week when every rival's earnings call was an argument about AI spending and AI returns, the most valuable of them turned in a clean beat, said almost nothing on the subject — and got marked down for the guidance, not the silence.

AI Economics · OpenAI

OpenAI Cut GPT-5.6 Prices by up to 80% — Three Weeks After Launch.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · July 31, 2026

Three weeks after shipping the GPT-5.6 family, OpenAI cut the price of its cheapest tier, Luna, by 80% — to $0.20 per million input tokens and $1.20 output — and the mid tier, Terra, by 20%. The flagship, Sol, did not move: still $5 in, $30 out. OpenAI credited the reductions to efficiency gains from GPT-5.6's own development, including, in the company's telling, the model rewriting and optimizing its own production inference code. It also added a “Fast mode” that runs Sol up to 2.5× faster for twice the price.

The shape of the cut is the tell. The commodity tiers — the ones a startup swaps out the moment a cheaper Chinese open-weight clears the bar — got the knife; the flagship, which has no real substitute yet, kept its price. This is what “China pricing mode” looks like from the incumbent side: defend the frontier's margin, race to the floor everywhere the frontier isn't. And the efficiency story, taken at face value, is a model cutting the cost of running models — the flywheel everyone promised, arriving first as a discount on the bottom of the menu.

Policy · Washington

The Labs Racing Hardest Just Signed a Letter Asking to Be Slowed Down.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · July 31, 2026

OpenAI and Anthropic — as organizations — formally endorsed “Pacing the Frontier,” an open letter signed by more than 1,100 employees across the major labs warning of “a real risk” that AI advances faster than anyone can “understand or control,” and calling for international tools to deliberately slow development. Sam Altman, notably, did not personally sign, and has separately said humans are already “at the singularity.” The endorsement lands the same week OpenAI cut prices to accelerate adoption, and a day before the Aug. 1 deadline for the White House's voluntary frontier-model framework.

About that deadline: Aug. 1 is when the framework mandated by June's executive order is due to be designed, not when anyone must comply — the order expressly bars a mandatory licensing regime, and the whole thing remains voluntary. As of Thursday the administration was reported “close to finalizing” a scheme giving federal agencies up to thirty days of pre-release access to covered models. So the two loudest voices asking to be paced are also among the ones writing the pace, cutting the prices, and shipping the models that optimize themselves. A slowdown everyone endorses and no one schedules is not a brake. It is a press release with a conscience.

The rest of the day rhymed. The two labs whose valuations now move other companies' earnings — Anthropic and OpenAI — spent the week backing a letter that asks the world to slow AI down, one of them while cutting prices 80% to speed it up, a day before a framework deadline they helped write. The sector's numbers keep pointing at each other instead of at the world: a rival's funding round becomes your profit, a race becomes a petition to be paced. Underneath all of it, something is still being sold by the hour. See you tomorrow.
