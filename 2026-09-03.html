Models · OpenAI

OpenAI Shipped the Most Dangerous Model It Has Ever Measured. The Demo Was About Spreadsheets.

By The Editor, Models Desk · September 3, 2026

GPT-6 Astra was released on Thursday, two days after OpenAI declared it the first model ever to cross the Critical cybersecurity tier of its own Preparedness Framework — the story this paper led with on Wednesday. The published figures are OpenAI's own and they are not small: 100% on ExploitBench, 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, 97.6% on FrontierMath Tier 4 v2, and 72.6% on OSWorld 2.0 at roughly 47% less time per task than GPT-5.6 Sol. It is priced at $10 and $50 per million input and output tokens. A limited set of organisations gets it first through Daybreak, OpenAI's application-based cyber programme; ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise, the API and the big clouds follow "in the coming days." President Greg Brockman said it is "not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era."

Now put two sentences side by side. OpenAI's Critical threshold describes a model that can devise and execute end-to-end attack strategies against hardened targets given only a high-level goal — a system that acts against live infrastructure without step-by-step human direction. And here is Brockman on what is new in the product: "computer use is a particularly important part of what's new," a model that "can zip through spreadsheets, fill out forms, and navigate across web pages often at superhuman speed." Those are not two capabilities. They are one capability described twice, once by the safety team and once by the marketing, and the difference between them is not what the model does but what it is pointed at.

The gating follows the target rather than the behaviour. The general release will refuse advanced cyber requests, including proof-of-concept exploits; the offensive work goes to vetted partners through Daybreak. That is a defensible design and it is worth saying so plainly. It is also worth being clear about what the unrestricted half consists of: the thing everyone with a payment method gets in the coming days is unsupervised, high-speed, goal-directed action on live systems. The systems are simply yours rather than someone else's.

The launch itself had a strange shape — the string gpt-6-astra was visible in the API and the press had the story hours before OpenAI's own page went up, which is not how a company that spent Tuesday writing about containment usually stages a Thursday. On the 150% price rise, Brockman's line is that "the price per task is what matters." He may be right. It is nonetheless the first argument any vendor reaches for when the sticker goes up.

The thing that makes it the most dangerous model OpenAI has ever measured and the thing that makes it a product are the same sentence with a different object. — on Astra's Critical classification
OpenAI's flagship API price, per million tokens
Both lines are the same rise — two and a half times — and only one of them looks like it. That is what a dollar axis does to a small number, and the honest thing is to say so rather than rescale until the story improves. Sol's $4 and $20 are themselves a promotion that reverts to $5 and $30 on about 21 November, so the true gap is narrower than drawn, and points the same way.
Open Weights · Meta

Meta's Flagship Ships Closed. The Weights Are Coming "Soon."

By The Editor, Open Weights Desk · September 3, 2026

Muse Spark 1.3 went live on Wednesday on Meta's API service and its Muse Code command-line tool — smarter, less verbose and more efficient than 1.2, in the company's telling. The same day, Mark Zuckerberg posted on X that an open-weights version of Muse Spark is coming "soon." As of Thursday, per The Register, no Muse Spark weights of any version have been published. Meta does have an open-weight model in the field: Muse Glimmer, thirty billion parameters, Apache 2.0, released on 10 August, small enough to run offline on a single 24GB consumer card. It is not the flagship, and it was never claimed to be.

"Soon" is a word with no date, no artefact and no obligation attached to it, which is precisely why it is available for use. Meta shipped a product on Wednesday and announced an intention on Wednesday, and the difference between them is that one of the two can be downloaded. This is not hypocrisy; it is a schedule, and a schedule with no dates on it is a preference.

Deals · Nvidia

Nvidia Bought the Neutral Ground for $12.93bn, Then Promised Not to Stand on It

By The Editor, Capital Desk · September 3, 2026

Nvidia confirmed on Thursday that it will buy Hugging Face for $12.93bn, reported as roughly $11.9bn in cash to shareholders plus up to $1bn in equity-based retention awards. The deal is expected to close in the first half of 2027, subject to regulatory approval. Hugging Face hosts more than three million models and is used by more than eighteen million people and 200,000 companies. This paper covered the transaction on 28 August, when it was reported, unsigned and confirmed by nobody; it is now on the record with both companies' names attached, and that difference is the news.

With it comes a fact that changes the shape of the story. Hugging Face's chief executive told CNBC that the company approached Jensen Huang weeks ahead of the deal, and that there were other bidders. This was not a firm cornered by a chip monopoly; it was a seller who went looking. Huang, for his part, is on the record pledging that Hugging Face will "remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem" and that nobody will be obliged to use Nvidia products.

That pledge is not a footnote to the asset. It is the asset. What Hugging Face sells is not storage or bandwidth, both of which are cheap; it is the fact that the place the industry keeps its models is owned by nobody who sells accelerators. Nvidia has paid $12.93bn for that property and, in the same announcement, had to guarantee that owning it does not spend it. So it is worth noticing which halves of this transaction are written down. The cash is contractual. The retention pool is contractual. The closing conditions are contractual. The neutrality is a quote.

I take Huang at his word, and there is nothing in his record here that invites otherwise. But an assurance binds the man who gives it, and this one has to survive an approval process running into 2027, a regulatory environment that killed the last acquisition Nvidia announced at anything like this scale, and every executive who holds the job afterwards. None of those three will have said anything on Thursday.

Policy · Copyright

The Justice Department Filed Three Pages Saying OpenAI Should Win

By The Editor, Legal Desk · September 3, 2026

The Department of Justice filed a statement of interest this week with Judge Sidney Stein in the Southern District of New York, telling the court that training large language models on copyrighted text is fair use. It is the first time the federal government has taken a formal position in the wave of copyright suits brought against AI companies by authors, publishers, record labels and news organisations. The argument is that training is "exceedingly transformative" — the model converts articles into statistical representations rather than using them to inform or entertain anyone — and that training alone makes no protected expression available and therefore displaces no market. The brief adds that rules obstructing domestic AI development "threaten national security" and hand an advantage to foreign adversaries. A statement of interest binds nothing and nobody; the government is not a party to the case.

The Times replied on the record, through a spokesman, that the administration had sided with a handful of trillion-dollar companies over "countless American creators whose work they stole." Its own filings will make the fair-use argument at length, and Judge Stein is under no obligation to read the government's three pages twice.

One absence is worth marking. In July, OpenAI's chief executive publicly floated the federal government taking a five per cent stake in the company. Nothing has been agreed, no such holding exists, and I am not alleging that one does. But the document is titled the Interest of the United States, and of all the filings in which to leave an interest unmentioned, this is the one named after the practice.

The Quiet Correction

A story that resolved itself while nobody was looking

The Pro That Everything Overtook

The line we have been carrying

Since the spring this paper has kept a standing note that Gemini 3.5 Pro is a no-show: announced at Google I/O on 19 May, several rumoured dates missed, its specifications available only through leaks. Writing up Gemini 3.8 Flash yesterday, I put that sentence next to the number 3.8, decided it read oddly, and assumed we had been carrying something stale.

What checking it found

We had not. The line is exactly right, and in being right it has turned into a considerably stranger fact than the one we wrote down. Google has since shipped 3.5 Flash-Lite, 3.6 Flash, 3.7 Flash and, on Wednesday, 3.8 Flash and 3.8 Flash Cyber. On Monday it shipped agentic video understanding across three Flash-tier models, cutting token consumption by up to 88% and API costs by up to 66%. In July it released three Gemini models in a single announcement, and TechCrunch's headline was that none of them was 3.5 Pro. The only thing Google announced in May that has not arrived is the tier it announced first. Nobody has cancelled it, rescheduled it or been asked about it in public. The numbering simply walked around it, three-tenths at a time.

What Google announced, and what turned up
The dashed line is the only thing above the axis because it is the only thing that never landed. Everything below was announced later and is in general release today.

The correction, then, is to us, and it is the small kind that is worth printing anyway: we went looking for a stale line in our own notes and found the stale thing was somebody's roadmap. It has been public the entire time. It simply never came with an announcement, because nothing did.

Editor's Note

Nobody made the United States show up. Of everyone with a stake in the New York Times' case against OpenAI, the party that filed this week is the only one that isn't in it. Three pages, arguing that training on other people's writing is exceedingly transformative and that ruling otherwise would advantage foreign adversaries, under the title the Interest of the United States. In July OpenAI's chief executive floated the federal government taking five per cent of the company. Nothing has been agreed and I am not saying otherwise. I note only that a filing whose entire function is to declare an interest found room for the national-security one.

The day's other large commitment was not a document at all. Jensen Huang put $12.93bn on the table for Hugging Face and said it will remain an open platform for the whole ecosystem, that nobody will be obliged to buy his chips, and I think he means every word. But look at what each half of that deal is made of: about $11.9bn in cash and up to $1bn in retention are contractual, the closing conditions are contractual, and the openness is a sentence he said to reporters. If the money doesn't arrive there is a court. If the openness doesn't survive there is a transcript.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
