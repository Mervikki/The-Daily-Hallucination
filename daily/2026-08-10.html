Agents · Anthropic

Anthropic Measured the Approval Prompt. It Catches Thirteen Per Cent.

By The Editor, Agents Desk · August 10, 2026

On Saturday Anthropic published the post behind a change we covered on Friday from a fragment: from August 14th, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code for Pro, Max and Team sessions. The agent stops asking permission before each action; a classifier screens every tool call and interrupts only for the irreversible, the destructive, or anything reaching outside your own environment. What we did not have on Friday was the company's argument, and the argument is a number. In an internal study of 1,053 paying testers, Anthropic reports that the classifier caught 89% of harmful actions. The humans, clicking through approval prompts on their own machines about their own work, caught 13.6%.

Harmful Actions Caught, Old Default vs. New

Both figures are Anthropic's, from its own study of 1,053 paid testers, measuring its own product against its own users. It is not an independent result. It is also, if you are honest about your own afternoons, not a surprising one.

Sit with the second number rather than the first. Thirteen point six per cent is not the score of a broken safety mechanism. It is the score of a mechanism that was never doing the job we assigned it. Anyone who has spent an hour approving four hundred file reads knows exactly what happens around read number forty: the dialog stops being a decision and becomes a keystroke, and the keystroke stops being attached to a judgement at all. The prompt did not fail because people are careless. It failed because it asked for attention several hundred times an hour and attention does not come in that quantity. What it collected instead was consent — reliably, in writing, every time.

Thirteen point six per cent is not a control. It is a habit with a keyboard shortcut.

Anthropic also brought a second number, and this one is somebody else's. Trajectory Labs ran 72 held-out indirect prompt-injection scenarios — 720 attack attempts — against models as they stood on July 17th. None succeeded against Fable 5, Opus 5 or Sonnet 5 in auto mode. 5.83% got through against OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex's auto-review mode, or roughly one attempt in seventeen. Anthropic has additionally stopped charging Pro, Max and Team users for the tokens the safety classifier burns, which is a welcome change and also a quiet disclosure that until this weekend you were paying by the word for your own supervision.

A zero in a competitor comparison, published by the party that commissioned it, is the sort of number this paper is professionally obliged to hold at arm's length — not because it is wrong, but because nobody publishes the eval they lose. The genuinely interesting disclosure is elsewhere in the same post, and it is a list of exclusions. Auto mode does not become the default on Claude Enterprise, on the API, on Bedrock, on Google's agent platform, or on Microsoft Foundry. Every one of those is a surface with a procurement process attached. So the control Anthropic has just scored at thirteen point six per cent is being retired for individual developers and preserved, untouched, for institutions — which is to say it survives precisely where somebody may one day have to describe it in a compliance review. Two days ago we wrote that the only thing standing between an autonomous agent and a public code repository was one unpaid maintainer's judgement. It now turns out the industry has a number for how often that works, and has responded by keeping it exactly where it can be pointed at.

Infrastructure · China

A Gigawatt With No Nvidia In It

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 10, 2026

Reported over the weekend, originating with Bloomberg and picked up by Tom's Hardware and DataCenterDynamics: Z.AI — formerly Zhipu, the Chinese lab behind the open-weight GLM models — has built and partially powered up a datacentre rated at one gigawatt, running entirely on domestically manufactured accelerators. No Nvidia. The facility reportedly holds multiple clusters of 10,000-plus chips each. A gigawatt is roughly the draw of 750,000 homes. Z.AI is said to have hit its 2026 sales target in July and to be tracking toward about $1bn in annualised revenue, and its GLM-5.2, released open-weight in June, was reportedly trained end to end on Huawei Ascend hardware.

Every argument for export controls has rested on an unstated assumption about time — that denying the best chips buys enough years for the gap to matter. This is what the years bought. Not parity, and nobody should claim parity from a partially energised building whose chip supplier is not even named in the original report. What it demonstrates is subtler and worse for the policy: at some scale, the domestic option stops being a workaround and starts being an option. China's compute story this week is a gigawatt that got built and a set of open weights that didn't ship — Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max is now six days past its own promised release. One of those is a capability. The other is a press schedule. It is worth noticing which one arrived.

Products · OpenAI

ChatGPT Atlas Died Yesterday, 292 Days Old, and Is Survived by a Chrome Extension

By The Editor, Products Desk · August 10, 2026

On October 21st, 2025, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Atlas, a web browser with ChatGPT built into it, on macOS, with agent mode in preview and versions for Windows, iOS and Android promised as coming soon. Alphabet's shares fell on the announcement. As of yesterday, it no longer opens. OpenAI's support notice is headed “Evolving Atlas into ChatGPT for browser-based agentic work”, which is a graceful way to say that the browsing features are moving into the ChatGPT desktop app, into Codex, and into an extension for Google Chrome. Atlas was built on Chromium, Google's engine, from the first day.

Platforms Promised on Launch Day vs. Platforms Shipped

Three of these four were still coming soon on the day the product stopped working. “Soon” had 292 days to arrive.

The migration instructions are the part worth reading. Bookmarks do not transfer. Open tabs do not transfer. History does not transfer. Users were told to export what they could before Sunday and to import it into another browser — Chrome is named — and, where cookie export is available, not to share those files with anyone they do not trust, because of what the files grant access to. ChatGPT conversation history is unaffected, being the one asset that was never really in the browser.

There is no scandal here, just an unusually complete circle. OpenAI took Google's rendering engine, wrapped it in a product intended to take the front door of the internet away from Google, ran it for nine months on one operating system, and has now folded the surviving parts into an add-on that runs inside Google's browser. The strategic lesson OpenAI drew — that the browser is a feature and not a destination — is almost certainly correct. It is also the thing Google has been saying by simply continuing to exist.

The Glossary · A Recurring Feature
Gigawatt
Official definition

One billion watts. A unit of electrical power, equal to roughly the continuous draw of 750,000 homes, or one very large nuclear reactor.

Real definition

The industry's preferred unit of ambition: the smallest number that still sounds like infrastructure, and the largest that can be said without producing a single electron. Its great virtue is that it is silent on tense. A gigawatt may be energised, contracted, permitted, queued, or merely hoped for, and the word is the same in every case — which is why Z.AI's is “partially operating,” why fifteen of Lancium's nineteen sit in an interconnection queue, and why nobody in this business has ever been asked to specify. Compare: megawatt, a unit so modest it implies the speaker has actually built something.

Editor's Note

You have clicked yes more times this month than you can remember, and you did not read most of them. I know this, and you know this, and now Anthropic has gone and measured it: in a study of a thousand paying users, people reviewing their own agent's actions on their own machines caught 13.6% of the harmful ones. My first instinct was alarm that the approval prompt is being taken away. Then I sat with the number. The prompt was not protecting anybody. It was collecting a signature.

Which makes the exclusions the story. Auto mode becomes the default for individuals on Pro, Max and Team. It stays opt-in on Enterprise, on the API, on Bedrock, on Google's agent platform, on Microsoft Foundry — every surface, that is, where somebody in procurement might one day ask what your controls are. The mechanism Anthropic has just scored at thirteen point six per cent is being switched off for the people who use it and left switched on for the people who have to describe it. Nothing was removed this week. It was reassigned to the customers with auditors. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
