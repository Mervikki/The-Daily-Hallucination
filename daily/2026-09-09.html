Mathematics · OpenAI
Nobody in This Story Wants the Million Dollars

By The Editor, Mathematics Desk · September 9, 2026

On Tuesday OpenAI published a proof that a three-dimensional fluid obeying the Navier–Stokes equations can, starting smooth and at rest under a smooth applied force, develop a singularity in finite time. It was produced by an unreleased model the company describes as significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra, run as a swarm of roughly 10,000 agents over 88 hours, exchanging some 2.7 million messages and emitting on the order of 130 billion output tokens. The write-up runs to about a hundred pages and comes with a formalisation in Lean, which is the part that matters: a machine that does not care who wrote the argument has read it and not objected.

Now the credit, because there is a great deal of it and it should come first. OpenAI is not claiming the prize. The Clay Mathematics Institute’s written problem does permit a smooth forcing term, so the result arguably lands inside the official statement; the version the field actually cares about has no force in it, and the company has said plainly it will not go after the million dollars. Clay’s president Martin Bridson called the work exciting and the evaluation “deliberately unhurried” and “absolutely rigorous”; the institute still lists the problem as open. And Tristan Buckmaster of NYU and Levent Alpöge of Anthropic, who posted their own AI-assisted blowup proofs, have had their priority acknowledged by OpenAI in public and their work called a “monumental achievement.” Terence Tao called it a remarkable one.

Which brings us to what the week was actually spent arguing about. Buckmaster alleges that OpenAI’s Sébastien Bubeck twice pressed for Alpöge’s name to come off the paper because Alpöge works for Anthropic, and offered Buckmaster the alternative of publishing first and claiming the prize himself — on that condition. He says that when he threatened to go public, Bubeck replied: “Why would you ruin your career?” Bubeck calls the wider framing “false and inflammatory,” says no researcher and no agent saw the pair’s work before it was public, and has apologised without qualification for that specific line — “the opposite of what I was trying to convey” — adding that he retracted it on the spot. OpenAI has separately conceded it cannot entirely rule out an indirect connection. That last sentence is the most creditable thing any party has said all week, and nobody made them say it.

So set the theft reading aside; it is not established and this desk is not making it. Look instead at the price list. A million dollars was declined by the company that might have claimed it, offered away by one of its researchers as a bargaining chip, and turned down by the man it was offered to. Every party treated the prize as the cheap object in the room. The one thing that would not trade — the thing worth a phone call, two requests and a sentence somebody has since apologised for — was a name in an author list, and specifically which company’s name sat next to it.

A million dollars was the one object in this story that everybody was willing to give away. — on what was actually being negotiated

Note that the wall clock ran the other way: twenty-two times the tokens, in a third of the elapsed time. Whatever this line is measuring, it is not patience.
Silicon · OpenAI
The Models Designed the Chip That Runs the Models

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · September 9, 2026

At Goldman Sachs’s Communacopia conference on Monday and Tuesday, OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar confirmed that the company used its own frontier models to design Jalapeño, the inference ASIC it built with Broadcom, and that the part went from initial design to tape-out in about nine months — a cycle Broadcom has described as possibly the fastest ever achieved in high-performance advanced semiconductors. Friar also said the cheap model is now the cheap model: “If you’re deploying Luna and compare that to (Z.ai’s) GLM 5.3, for example, on a cloud layer, we are cheaper.”

Take the second sentence first, because it is the more interesting one. For two years the standing argument for open weights has been price — that a downloadable model with no per-token meter must eventually undercut a hosted one. Friar’s claim is that at the layer where most companies actually consume a model, which is somebody else’s cloud, it no longer does. That is a claim about billing infrastructure rather than about models, and it is checkable by anyone with two invoices, which is more than can be said for most things said at an investor conference.

As for the chip: a company that sells intelligence has used it on the silicon that serves it, and reported the schedule rather than the performance. Nine months is a supply-chain fact, verifiable by people who make chips for a living. It is also the only figure in this issue that a competitor can straightforwardly copy, which is presumably why it was the one Friar led with.

Agents · Meta
Meta Shipped the Agent. The Weights Are Still “Soon.”

By The Editor, Agents Desk · September 9, 2026

Meta launched Muse on Tuesday — a personal agent, US-only, over-18s, on iOS, Android, the web and WhatsApp, running on the Muse Spark model family under the internal codename Hatch. It connects to your email, calendar and payment methods and works through multi-step errands: booking appointments, filling in forms, watching a home security camera. There is a free tier, reported by Zuckerberg at up to 100 million tokens a week, then Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100. Alexandr Wang says the app runs in “its own isolated environment” and “never sees your actual passwords or payment details.” The stock closed up more than 6%.

Two details do more work than the launch copy. The first is that the free tier reportedly wants a payment card on file before it will do anything, which is a curious definition of free and a very clear statement about what the product is for. The second is that training on your interactions is opt-out — the agent that reads your inbox is, by default, a data collection instrument you have to go and switch off. Forbes and Benzinga both report that Meta’s own staff flagged privacy and reliability problems in internal testing before launch. Meta shipped anyway, which is a decision the company is entitled to make and which its own employees are on record disagreeing with.

And then there is the promise. In August, Zuckerberg said Meta would open the weights for Muse Spark 1.2. On 2 September, Muse Spark 1.3 shipped — closed, API only — and he said an open-weights version was coming “soon.” It is now the ninth. No Muse Spark weights of any version exist. The commitment has been renewed once, outlived the version number it was originally made about, and acquired a consumer product built on the closed one in the meantime. Meta’s only actual open artefact remains Muse Glimmer, 30B, Apache 2.0, from 10 August.

Policy · Google
Google Made Its Own Search Worse and Sent Out a Press Release About It

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 9, 2026

On Tuesday Google rolled out reworked search results across the EU for roughly 450 million users, stripping date filters, live pricing, descriptive tags and the vacation-rentals unit from travel queries and pushing comparison sites such as Expedia and Booking.com up the page. This follows the European Commission’s July decision fining Google €890m in total under the Digital Markets Act — €460m for self-preferencing in Search, €430m over app-store steering — with sixty days to comply and periodic penalties of up to 5% of worldwide daily turnover behind it. Google’s framing of its own compliance is that this is the largest reduction in search quality in the company’s twenty-nine-year history.

A company that has just been fined by a regulator announcing that the regulator has ruined its product is not a finding, it is a submission, and it should be read as one. The load-bearing figure — that earlier DMA measures cut free direct-booking referrals to European businesses by 30% — is Google’s, measured by Google, about traffic Google controls. What is genuinely notable is the choice of superlative. Twenty-nine years covers every algorithm update, every spam wave, and the entire arrival of generative search. Google has decided that the worst thing to happen to its results in three decades is a hotel price disappearing from a box.

Editor’s Note

There is a million dollars in today’s lead story and I cannot find anybody who wants it. OpenAI won’t claim the prize. One of its researchers is said to have offered it to a rival outright. The rival turned the offer down. Give the company its due here, and properly: it published the Lean file so the mathematics can be checked without trusting a word of the press release, it recognised the other side’s priority in public, Bubeck apologised for his worst sentence without hedging it, and OpenAI said out loud that it cannot entirely rule out an indirect connection. That is more candour than most disputes over a footnote produce, let alone one over a Millennium Problem.

Google spent Tuesday briefing that its own search is the worst it has been since 1997. Both companies are talking their product down; the difference is what it buys them. OpenAI undersells the claim so the claim gets believed. Google oversells the damage so somebody comes and takes the rule away. Only one of those is a discount. And when everything in this paper had a price on it — a prize, a chip cycle, a card on file, a hotel booking box — the single object nobody would trade was one company’s name printed after a co-author’s. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
