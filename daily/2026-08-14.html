Open Weights · Alibaba

Alibaba Shipped the Weights It Promised. It Also Shipped a Non-Compete.

By The Editor, Open Weights Desk · August 14, 2026

Alibaba published the weights for Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B on Hugging Face and ModelScope on Thursday — 2.4 trillion total parameters, roughly 95 billion active per token, a million tokens of context. This paper has noted their absence for a fortnight, so let us be exact about the arrival: they are here, they are real, and this is the first Max-tier model Alibaba has ever released as a download rather than an endpoint. Nobody else at the frontier has given away an object of this size. That deserves to be said before anything else is.

Now the licence, which is not Apache 2.0 and is not open source. Previous Qwen releases were Apache; this one arrives under a bespoke "Qwen3.8-Max License," and its operative clause is a category test rather than a size test. If you operate as a Model-as-a-Service provider or an AI work assistant, and your aggregate revenue crosses $50m in any consecutive twelve months, you must negotiate a separate commercial agreement with Alibaba Cloud. The rate is not published, because per reporting it has not been settled — negotiations are described as ongoing. Two further things stayed home: the released weights are text-only while the API takes images and video, and Qwen3.8-27B, the small one that would actually run on the hardware most readers own, missed its date for the second time.

Who Is Physically Able To Accept This Gift

The fifty is our arithmetic, not Alibaba's: 2.4 trillion parameters at four-bit, before you allow for context. Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a shopping list.

Put those two facts against each other and the licence stops looking like a tax on the wealthy. Almost nobody can run this file. A hospital, a university, a national library or a mid-sized bank can download it free at any revenue and will discover it needs a rack. The population that can actually stand up 2.4 trillion parameters and serve them at scale is, very nearly, the population of companies in the business of serving models to other people — which is precisely the category the clause names. Alibaba has not means-tested its generosity. It has drawn the boundary around the only people for whom the gift is usable.

Open weights, with a non-compete. You may run it. You may not be Alibaba Cloud with it.

None of which makes the release fake. A file you can inspect, fine-tune, air-gap and keep is worth more than an endpoint, and the threshold is genuinely high — the overwhelming majority of organisations on earth will never touch it. But the Open Source Definition spends its sixth clause forbidding exactly this: discrimination against a field of endeavour. Alibaba is free to write whatever licence it likes and has been admirably direct about writing one. The word to retire is "open-sourced," which appeared in most of Thursday's coverage and is now doing work the licence file declines to do.

Models · DeepSeek

DeepSeek Shipped Its Best Model, Then Deleted the Announcement

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 14, 2026

On Wednesday the deepseek-v4-pro endpoint quietly began resolving to a new build, 0813. A short statement appeared on DeepSeek's site crediting "significantly enhanced agent capabilities"; by Thursday afternoon it had been taken down. Anyone with that endpoint name in production received a different model and, if they blinked, no notice at all. Pricing on OpenRouter is $0.435 per million input tokens and $0.87 per million output, with a million-token context — and a warning on the API pricing page that a significant increase is coming, carrying neither a date nor a magnitude.

The scores depend entirely on which one you read. DeepSeek's own table claims Terminal-Bench 2.1 up from 72.1 to 87.9 and DeepSWE up from 12.8 to 62.7, a near-fivefold jump that nobody outside the company can check, because the 0813 weights are not published — April's V4 series went out under MIT, this checkpoint has not. Artificial Analysis, meanwhile, puts its composite intelligence index at 53: level with a model Zhipu shipped in June, four points behind the middle tier of GPT-5.6. The South China Morning Post's summary of all this was that the model struggles on benchmarks.

Except at one thing. A security researcher ran it against a CVE-rediscovery benchmark and reported that at pass@3 it recovered 87.5% of the vulnerabilities — ahead of Opus 5 and Qwen 3.8 at 81.3%, the best result he had measured. The catch is in the same post: only 65.6% of what it reported was real. So the cheapest frontier-adjacent model on the market is now also the most effective vulnerability finder anyone has published a number for, and roughly a third of its findings are noise. Both halves of that land in the same place, which is an open-source maintainer's inbox, at eighty-seven cents a million tokens.

Silicon · OpenAI

OpenAI Put a Frontier Model on Someone Else's Silicon and Talked About the Speedometer

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · August 14, 2026

OpenAI and Cerebras announced on Thursday that GPT-5.6 Sol is being served in an "Ultrafast" mode on Cerebras wafer-scale hardware, at up to 750 output tokens per second and up to fourteen times the standard tier's rate. Both companies say the model is identical in capability to Sol as served today; no third party has evaluated that. Access is a limited preview for selected customers, "expanding as capacity grows." There is no contract value, no system count, no term, no exclusivity language, and no price for the new tier.

The press releases are about latency, which is the least surprising thing in them. Cerebras is fast; that is the entire company. The fact worth a paragraph is that OpenAI is now running a current frontier model in production on hardware Nvidia did not make, and that neither party said so in as many words. Read the two announcements end to end and you will find enthusiastic detail about tokens per second and complete silence about the substrate being a substitute.

The reticence is the tell, and it is entirely rational. You do not announce a second supplier; you announce a feature that happens to require one. Nvidia has spent this month arranging half a trillion dollars of lending against its own chips, on the assumption that they remain the thing everyone must buy. A customer of that size quietly proving otherwise, in a press release about speed, is the more consequential document — and it is the one written to be skimmed.

Finance · Datacentres

A Hundred Billion Dollars, No Financials, and a Queue Forming Behind It

By The Editor, Finance Desk · August 14, 2026

Vantage Data Centers is weighing a public listing at a valuation of around $100bn, raising roughly $10bn, according to Reuters' sources — which would make it the largest datacentre flotation ever attempted. Silver Lake and DigitalBridge are behind it. No process has formally launched and Vantage has not commented. Switch has already hired banks for a raise of up to $10bn at about $50bn of equity, possibly listing in the fourth quarter; CyrusOne is reported to be preparing something for 2027.

What They Want To Raise vs. What They Say They Are Worth

Every number on this axis is attributed to people familiar with the matter. Not one company has confirmed a figure, and the third row is drawn honestly.

What is absent from all of it is any revenue, any EBITDA, any contracted-capacity or lease-backlog figure. A $100bn valuation is circulating without a single disclosed financial behind it, which is normal for pre-process reporting and worth noticing anyway. These are landlords. Their tenants are the companies currently financing chips against future demand for the buildings the landlords are floating. Somewhere in that loop a number has to be real.

Editor's Note

The most informative sentence published in AI on Wednesday was deleted on Thursday. DeepSeek put up a short line about "significantly enhanced agent capabilities," then took it down while the model stayed up and stayed swapped in under the old name. I have been trying to decide whether that is unusual and I have concluded it is only unusually literal. Everything shipped in this issue is finished. None of the terms are.

Look at what you cannot find out this morning. What Alibaba's revenue share will cost: not settled. What DeepSeek's warned price increase will be: no number, no date. What Ultrafast costs, or on how many wafers: nothing. What Vantage earns, at a hundred billion dollars: not disclosed. The models are now the easy part to give away — enormous, downloadable, permanently yours. It is the invoice that stays in draft. And when the industry finally does write the terms down, the one clause we have in front of us today says you may do anything at all with two and a half trillion parameters except go into business with them. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor

Corrections & Clarifications

We have not read the Qwen3.8-Max licence. The $50m figure, the Model-as-a-Service and AI-work-assistant categories and the unsettled revenue-share rate reach us through the South China Morning Post and Reuters-derived coverage; if the file scopes any of it differently, that is our correction and it is a large one. DeepSeek's Terminal-Bench and DeepSWE jumps are DeepSeek's own and unverifiable while the 0813 weights stay unpublished; the composite index of 53 and the API prices are third-party, and the CVE figures are one named researcher's own benchmark run, not a paper. Cerebras' 750 tokens per second is both parties' number. Every datacentre valuation here is attributed to unnamed sources.

This publication's annual revenue is comfortably below fifty million dollars, which is the first time that has ever been an advantage.
