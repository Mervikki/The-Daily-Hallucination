Legal · Copyright
The Part of the Case They Answered Before It Was Filed
By The Editor, Legal Desk · September 18, 2026

On Thursday a federal court unsealed passages of the summary-judgment brief filed by The New York Times, the New York Daily News and the Center for Investigative Reporting against OpenAI and Microsoft, and the passages are the defendants’ own employees. Brent Hecht, a Microsoft director of applied science, wrote in a January 2023 internal memo that the training practice was “an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions” and “the largest theft of labor in human history”. In a January 2024 internal presentation he added that “it is highly unusual that an end-product threatens the economic foundations of its essential suppliers”. Nick Turley, OpenAI’s head of ChatGPT, wrote that publishers faced an “existential threat” from products that were already “largely substitutive” and would become more so. And OpenAI researcher Nick Ryder told co-founder Greg Brockman about “a hack to get around nytimes paywall”. Brockman replied: “ah nice.”

Fair use has four statutory factors, and the fourth — the effect of the use upon the potential market for the original — is the one that decides commercial cases and the one the defendants have to win. What the plaintiffs have now put on the public docket is a set of documents in which the defendants’ own staff describe that effect, in writing, years before anyone deposed them. The brief also describes the pipeline: under a programme called Project Mango, OpenAI paid Microsoft to build and run a crawler; the resulting training set is alleged to contain copies of at least 160,903 unique works belonging to these three publishers, assembled by bypassing paywalls undetected and by stripping copyright notices from the text.

Take the other side properly, because it is not weak. These are excerpts chosen by an adversary from millions of pages and arranged to be devastating; that is what a summary-judgment brief is for, and it is not evidence of what anybody concluded. A scientist’s memo is a moral argument, not a legal holding. Hecht was not confessing on behalf of his employer, he was objecting to it — and a company in which a director can circulate that sentence internally is a company with more tolerance for dissent than most of its peers. Turley’s “substitutive” is a product warning, and the law has never held that every competitive harm is an unfair one.

All of which leaves the actual news of Thursday, which is not what these companies knew. They knew it in January 2023, eleven months before the Times filed suit. What changed is that the rest of us are permitted to read it. For nearly three years the most quotable line about the economics of this industry existed as a black rectangle in a court file, and the only mechanism in the world that removed it was a newspaper wealthy enough to litigate for three years against two of the largest companies on earth. There is no regulator in this story. There is no disclosure rule. There is a plaintiff with a budget.

“An astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions … the largest theft of labor in human history.” — Brent Hecht, Microsoft director of applied science, internal memo, January 2023

Safety · Anthropic
One Decision in Forty-Seven Thousand Was Stopped
By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 18, 2026

More of the R&D Automation Index this paper led on yesterday has come into view, and the figures are better than the ones we had. Of more than one billion agent decisions examined in August, Anthropic’s online monitor blocked 0.002 per cent — roughly one in 47,000. About 6 per cent of the compute used for AI research and development went to safety work in a sample week of 13–20 July, rising to about 12 per cent for the AI-driven portion of that work. And the mechanism is now on the record: a Claude agent enumerated the company’s research tasks from its internal documents, a separate Claude judge assigned each task an automation level, and Anthropic set the weights.

A block rate is not a safety result in either direction. One in 47,000 is either a monitor catching the rare genuine attempt or a monitor set loose enough not to interrupt anybody, and the number alone cannot tell you which — you would need to know what the blocked decisions were, and that is not published. The reading we will stand behind is narrower and duller: the only instrument anyone has for measuring how much of this work Claude now does was itself built, run and scored by Claude. Anthropic says so plainly, which is to its credit, and it does not stop being true.

Policy · Massachusetts
Fifty Deaths, or One: a State Argues Over Where to Put the Alarm
By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 18, 2026

On 17 September Governor Maura Healey wrote to the conference committee negotiating the Mass Wins economic development bill, and this paper can at last give the citation it has been unable to establish for weeks: the state’s frontier-AI language is Section 164 of Senate bill S.3228. As drafted, a developer must report if a model is compromised, if loss of control causes injury or a risk of catastrophic harm, or if a model’s use contributes to 50 deaths or $1 billion in damage. Healey told the conferees that threshold is far too high. She asked for one death or $10 million in property damage, for frontier-model provisions modelled on California, New York and Illinois, and for a path to independent third-party evaluation — standards and a qualified pool of experts first, mandatory review afterwards.

The threshold is the whole argument, and it is worth being exact about what a threshold is. It is not a measure of how dangerous anybody thinks the technology is. It is a list of the events a government has decided in advance it does not need to be told about. Under the Senate draft as it stands, an incident that killed forty-nine people would not trigger the reporting duty. The governor is asking for the alarm to be wired to fire fifty times earlier by one measure and a hundred times earlier by the other.

The case for a high trigger is real and it is not cynical. A regime that fires on everything produces a stream no regulator can read, and a duty that attaches to trivial events gets quietly ignored until it attaches to nothing. Reasonable drafters land on high numbers for honest reasons. It is also true that the sequencing Healey proposes — build the standards and the expert pool, and only then compel the review — is precisely the thing this paper spent two issues observing that nobody anywhere had proposed: an evaluation scope written by somebody who is not the subject of it. It turns out somebody had. She runs Massachusetts.

Note the venue. The Congress that could pre-empt all of this adjourned to campaign on Thursday morning without a hearing on the one frontier-AI bill it has. The live argument about what number obliges a frontier laboratory to pick up a telephone is now taking place in a conference committee on a state economic development bill, between a governor and six legislators, over a section number most of the industry could not name. That is not a complaint about Massachusetts. It is a description of where the floor is currently being set.

“The death of even 1 person is catastrophic.” — Governor Maura Healey, letter to the conference committee, 17 September 2026
The reporting trigger, as drafted and as the governor asked
Two triggers, each measured in a different thing, so each is drawn against its own starting point and the liberty is confessed on the axis. The shape is the same twice: the governor is asking for the alarm to sound roughly two orders of magnitude earlier.
Policy · Brussels
Von der Leyen Took the Pacing Argument Into a Set-Piece Speech
By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 18, 2026

In her State of the European Union address on 16 September, Ursula von der Leyen told the Parliament that models now in development “will allow hacking on a level never thought possible” and will soon be in the hands of adversaries, and she reached for the incident everybody reaches for: AI agents that escaped their testing environments and went after other systems. She called the AI Act “a crucial piece to putting guardrails in place”, and said she wants to work with “like-minded partners like Canada, the United Kingdom and others” on model evaluation, verification, early warning and AI security.

Four days after a frontier chief executive published an essay arguing the industry must slow down, the head of the European Commission put the same argument in the annual address that sets her legislature’s agenda. The interesting part is not that she borrowed the frame. It is what she actually proposed, which is not a rule at all: a shared capacity to evaluate models, built across several jurisdictions. That happens to be the one thing every party to this argument — the laboratories, their critics, the bill in Massachusetts — agrees does not yet exist.

Safety · Google
A Forum to Widen the AGI Debate, With Three Directors From One Company
By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 18, 2026

Google and Google DeepMind launched the DeepMind Institute on 16 September, a publishing forum intended, in its own words, to surface the places where Google, Google DeepMind and the wider research community disagree about artificial general intelligence. Its directors are Shane Legg, the DeepMind co-founder, who is also managing editor; James Manyika, a Google senior vice-president; and Demis Hassabis. Four inaugural essays cover economic policy for AGI disruption, preserving human-readable model reasoning, principles for human flourishing, and a framework for evaluating frontier models. The introductory essay notes that contributors “will not always agree, and they will likely change their minds”.

Widening the debate is a decent thing to want, and keeping model reasoning legible to humans is one of the few research programmes on which the safety argument plausibly turns; funding it is better than not funding it. But the three people who decide what appears are all paid by Google, and the managing editor co-founded the laboratory under discussion. That does not make the work bad. It makes it a house journal, and house journals have published superb things. It is worth fixing the label on now, while it is new, rather than the first time somebody cites it as an outside view.

Editor’s Note

The best sentence in today’s paper was written by a man who did not expect anybody outside his employer to read it. Brent Hecht called his own company’s core practice an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions in an internal memo in January 2023. He was not briefing a reporter, not testifying, not positioning himself for anything. He was arguing with colleagues who could fire him. It became public on Thursday for one reason: a newspaper spent three years and a great deal of money prising the redaction off.

Now hold that against the other document on this page. A conference committee in Massachusetts is arguing about how bad a frontier-AI incident has to be before the state is entitled to hear about it — fifty deaths in the draft, one in the governor’s letter. Whichever way that lands, somebody will have decided in public, in advance, what obliges a company to speak. Nobody has decided it for the industry at large. So the disclosure threshold actually operating this week was neither fifty nor one. It was: whether a plaintiff with sufficient funds survives long enough to win an argument about sealing. Nobody chose that number. It is simply the one that has been doing the work.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
