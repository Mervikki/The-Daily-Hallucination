Labour · Amazon

Amazon Is Closing the Marketplace That Trained the Industry, and the Reason Is That It Was Cheap

By The Editor, Labour Desk · August 26, 2026

Amazon confirmed on Tuesday that Mechanical Turk shuts down on 30 September, twenty-one years after it opened. It had already closed to new customers at the end of July. Coverage of the notice says SageMaker Ground Truth and Amazon Augmented AI go the same day, which would mean Amazon is leaving the business of putting a human in the loop altogether. The stated reason, as reported, is an internal assessment of the company's programs and services — a sentence with the temperature of a parking notice. MTurk launched in 2005, sold its unit of work as a Human Intelligence Task, typically paid a few cents for one, and at its peak is reported to have had more than 500,000 workers. Jeff Bezos called it artificial artificial intelligence, which was a joke at the time.

The obvious reading is that the models finally ate the work. That reading is pointing the wrong way. Demand for human judgement has never been higher; the labs are buying more of it than at any moment in history, and paying vastly more for it. What died is the particular proposition MTurk sold — that human judgement is a commodity you buy anonymously, in units of pennies, from somebody whose name you never learn. The firms that took the market sell the opposite. Mercor was in talks in July, per Forbes, to raise $500 million at a $20 billion valuation, ten months after raising at ten; it says it crossed $2 billion in annualised gross billings in June, double where it stood in February. It recruits doctors, lawyers, bankers and PhDs. Its own published rate card averages $114 an hour.

The mechanism is the part nobody enjoys saying out loud. In 2023, researchers at EPFL estimated that between 33 and 46 per cent of crowd workers on Mechanical Turk had used a language model to complete a text-production task. Of course they had: the pay was pennies and the shortcut was free. But that finding quietly ends the business. Once the cheapest way to fake human judgement costs nothing, unverified human judgement cannot be bought cheaply, because it cannot be distinguished from the fake. So the industry stopped buying judgement and started buying credentials. What a lab purchases from Mercor is not a better answer. It is a checkable claim about whose answer it is — a licence number, a specialism, a CV. The credential is the line item.

The industry did not stop buying human judgement. It stopped being able to buy it from strangers. — on the closure of Mechanical Turk

Markets · Anthropic

Anthropic Will Tell Investors Its Market Is Roughly the United States

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 26, 2026

The Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday, citing people familiar, that Anthropic will pitch public-offering investors a total addressable market of more than $30 trillion, derived from the full scope of work that could be performed by AI models. Reported in the same breath: a raise of as much as $100 billion, a valuation around $2 trillion, prospectus documents expected shortly and a debut possible in September or early October. The Journal is blocked to this desk; we have all of it through Fortune, Quartz and others, every one of which credits the Journal rather than matching it. For a fortnight this paper refused to print the $2 trillion figure on the grounds that we could not tie it to any named reporter's own text. It now has one. We are recording the change rather than pretending we always had it.

For scale: American annual GDP was cited this week at $32.5 trillion. SpaceX pitched $28.5 trillion ahead of its own offering, and the Journal's own check notes that the 191 technology companies in the S&P 1500 booked roughly $2.4 trillion of revenue between them last year — so the claim is about twelve times the combined annual revenue of the listed American technology industry. Aswath Damodaran of NYU told the Journal that SpaceX's number was "reaching the end of what's plausible and pushing beyond." Anthropic's is a trillion and a half past it.

Nobody serious mistakes a TAM for a forecast; it is the size of the pool, not the swim, and every prospectus has one. What is worth noticing is what has happened to the genre. SpaceX set the record at $28.5 trillion. Anthropic arrives at slightly more than $30 trillion. No one sizes a market from first principles and lands five per cent above the previous record by coincidence. This is not an estimate of the world; it is a position taken relative to the last company that made one. And the underlying claim — that the addressable market is the full scope of work — means the pool being measured is every hour anybody is paid for, which is a sentence about the labour market wearing the costume of a slide.

Every other figure in a prospectus describes the company. This one describes the world, and the world has not been consulted. — on the $30 trillion addressable market

Earnings · Nvidia

Nvidia Kept Sixty-Two Cents of Every Dollar and the Shares Went Down

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 26, 2026

Nvidia reported its second quarter of fiscal 2027 on Wednesday, for the three months ended 26 July: revenue $96.2 billion, up 106 per cent on the year and 18 per cent on the quarter. Data Center alone was $89.0 billion, up 117 per cent. GAAP net income was $59.7 billion; diluted earnings $2.46 a share; gross margin 75.0 per cent on both a GAAP and a non-GAAP basis. Third-quarter guidance is $108.0 billion give or take two per cent, and the guidance explicitly assumes no data-centre compute revenue from China whatsoever. CNBC reports the company also pointed to fiscal-2028 growth of around 70 per cent. The shares slipped in after-hours trading regardless.

There is no chart on this item because there is nothing to put beside it. Fifty-nine point seven billion dollars of net income on ninety-six point two billion of revenue is sixty-two cents in the dollar, at a scale where most large companies cannot hold ten. The genuinely interesting sentence is the zero-China assumption: Nvidia has deleted an entire market from its own forecast and still guided up twelve per cent, which is either unusual conservatism or a quiet concession about where the export question has finally settled. Either way the company has now managed to make the largest number in the industry boring, and the market answered with a shrug.

Silicon · OpenAI

OpenAI's First Chip Was Benchmarked Against the Nvidia Part It Does Not Compete With

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · August 26, 2026

OpenAI published first performance results on Tuesday for Jalapeño, the inference accelerator it is building with Broadcom. Its claims: 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt and 1.7 to 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency than systems built on Nvidia's GB200 and GB300, rising to 2.1 to 4.1 times on interactive and agentic workloads. The headline composite is that on DeepSeek R1 the chip delivered 104.3 times the throughput per kilowatt of a GB300 system while matching that system's lowest reported time between output tokens. Deployment is inside OpenAI's own infrastructure before the end of the year, wider in 2027; second and third generations are already in development.

Within a day the research firm SemiAnalysis had called the comparison "somewhat incomplete and unfair," for a specific and unanswerable reason: Jalapeño uses HBM4 memory and the Blackwell parts it was measured against do not. The like-for-like rival is Rubin, which also uses HBM4, and Rubin is not on the card. This is how vendor benchmarks have always been built and it is worth saying plainly anyway, because the 104.3 will outlive the caveat by several months and probably several funding rounds.

The better detail is what the chip was tested on: GPT-OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1 670B and Kimi K2.5 1T. OpenAI's own frontier models are nowhere on the list, and they cannot be — a benchmark nobody outside the building can reproduce is not a benchmark, it is a press release with a decimal point. Which means the only reason any outsider can even attempt to check a claim about closed American silicon is that two of the three test models are Chinese open weights anybody can download and run. The open-weight ecosystem this industry spends its safety budget describing as a hazard is, this week, the only working measuring instrument on the premises.

Product · Anthropic

Claude Remembers You in More Rooms Now, and Anthropic Will Pay Strangers to Say Whether That Is Good

By The Editor, Product Desk · August 26, 2026

Two announcements landed on Tuesday. The memory systems behind Claude chat and Claude Cowork have been unified, so what the model learns on one surface carries to the other; memories are now written topic by topic during a conversation rather than summarised at the end, meaning state syncs while a chat is still open, and users can read, edit and delete what has been retained, by topic. Separately, Anthropic opened a $5 million grant programme funding independent research into AI's effect on user wellbeing — money, model access and technical support, with grantees working independently and publishing open-source evaluations. Applications close on 21 September; full proposals are invited by 5 October. The framing cites unsettled standards for users seeking companionship and for conversations involving a mental-health crisis. Bain announced a global partnership the same day.

The grant programme is the better item and deserves saying so warmly: five million dollars is real money, open-source evaluations are a genuine commitment, and conceding in public that there is no agreed standard for what a model should do when the person typing is in crisis is a harder thing to write down than any benchmark. It is also worth noticing the order of operations. The memory that follows you between applications shipped on Tuesday. The research into whether being followed between applications is good for the person being followed accepts applications until 21 September, and reports whenever it reports.

Editor's Note

A marketplace closed this week for being too cheap. That is not one of the usual ways for a market to fail. Mechanical Turk sold human judgement at a few cents a unit from people whose names nobody needed, and it shuts on 30 September while the firms selling the same input at a hundred and fourteen dollars an hour raise money at twenty billion. Amazon did not lose on price. It lost because the price was the problem: once a worker earning pennies can hold a language model, cheap anonymous judgement becomes indistinguishable from the free fake kind, and the only remedy anybody has found is to stop buying judgement and start buying identity.

So what the labs now purchase is not a better answer. It is a verified claim about who produced it — a licence number, a specialism, a face. For twenty-one years the offer was that nobody had to know who you were. The replacement pays roughly forty times better and requires that everybody does. I have read a great deal this week about what that shift is worth to the people buying, and not one line about whether the people doing the work consider it a raise or a background check. Nobody has asked them. They are, as they have been throughout, the input.

See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
