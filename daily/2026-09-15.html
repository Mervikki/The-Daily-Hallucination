Policy · Beijing

Beijing Refused the Author and Named the Venue

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 15, 2026

The reply came inside a day and in three registers. Foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun, on Monday: “Fearmongering, confrontation and vicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance and serve the interests of no one.” The Global Times, published by People's Daily, called Dario Amodei's essay a Cold War playbook whose real agenda is “to attempt to curb China's AI development through technological barriers and regulatory monopolies, uphold Washington's monopolistic hegemony in cutting-edge technology, and exclude China from the global AI governance system.” And China Daily, in an editorial that dismissed the slowdown push as self-serving, produced the best line anyone has written about the working group this paper reported yesterday: the coordination among the three companies “sounds rather like a club whose membership rules have been drafted before the guest list is announced.” A global AI-safety framework that excludes China, it added, “is not quite global.”

Every headline that reached this desk used a verb of attack — hits back, slams, dismisses, rejects — and most of the text earns it. But the same editorial also says that China and the United States “should cooperate where neither can manage the consequences alone”, and points at a planned bilateral AI-safety dialogue and future high-level exchanges as offering “opportunities for practical engagement”. That paragraph is, in substance, step three. Amodei's third step asks for graded agreements with authoritarian states rising toward a joint speed limit on AI progress. The counterparty has now pointed at a channel that already exists.

What Beijing refused is the author, not the agenda, and it is not hard to see why. The essay that proposes talks also asks Washington to tighten chip export controls, enforce anti-distillation rules and harden model weights against theft, on the stated grounds that a Chinese lead “would pose grave danger for the United States and the world” and that CCP-associated projects would be positioned to “militarily dominate democracies”. Those clauses are not marginalia. They are the constraint the whole plan runs on: democracies may slow by no more than their lead. A document cannot be an invitation and a containment strategy at once without the invitee working out which paragraph is load-bearing.

The obvious objection, and it holds: Beijing's answer costs Beijing nothing. Endorsing dialogue while the other side's president spends the same weekend refusing to regulate anything is the cheapest position on the board, and China Daily named no rate, no threshold and no evaluator — which puts it exactly level with every American laboratory. Amodei's hawkish clauses are also a coherent position honestly held; asking your own country to slow down while asking it not to lose is harder than either half on its own. What survives is narrower and duller than the headlines. The one party whose consent his third step actually requires has replied in public, and pointed at a room.

“The proposed coordination among the three companies sounds rather like a club whose membership rules have been drafted before the guest list is announced.” — China Daily, editorial

The three steps, by how many parties have to agree

The only step anybody has completed is the only step that needed nobody else's signature. Step three needs the fewest counterparties of the three — and it is the one whose counterparty replied this week by newspaper.

Policy · Washington

Vermont Filled In the Blank

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 15, 2026

Nearly three hundred people spent Tuesday in a Washington ballroom at an all-day event called the Pro-Human Assembly, hosted by the Future of Life Institute and organised around a declaration that humans remain in charge of AI, that people keep agency and liberty, and that AI companies answer for what their products do. Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon on one programme; Richard Blumenthal and Chip Roy; Greg Casar; Ashley Judd and Joseph Gordon-Levitt; the Archbishop of San Francisco and the National Association of Evangelicals; and parents of children who died by suicide after talking to chatbots. Bannon, in a statement to Axios: “The Oligarchs are finally being forced to admit they've lied from the beginning about the dangers of acceleration with zero guardrails.”

The legislative content is the part worth reading, and it is not new. Sanders and Casar announced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act on 3 September; what Sanders added on Tuesday is that they will introduce it next week, a fortnight and change after the press release. It would permanently prohibit developing or deploying superintelligent AI, pause advanced development until a federal regulator writes safety rules, stand up a cabinet-level agency empowered to strip dangerous capabilities and, where necessary, destroy a superintelligence, and direct the government to pursue international agreements stopping anyone else from building one. Penalties: the corporate death penalty for firms, up to twenty years for individuals, explicitly modelled on the statutes covering unlawful nuclear weapons work.

For a month this desk has been noting that nobody in the industry will put a value on a capability threshold. Amodei's checkpoints take the form if capability X, then certifications Y and Z, and no value for X is ever supplied. OpenAI's Chris Lehane asked Congress for alignment-evaluation gates without saying where a gate sits. Massachusetts screens by revenue. Here, at last, is a document that fills in X: a system that “exhibits or can easily be modified to exhibit” capabilities that “match or exceed human cognitive performance and capabilities across a broad range of domains or tasks”. The consequence attached to crossing it is not a certification. It is twenty years.

Filling in the blank is not the same as filling it in well, and this definition is poor. “Can easily be modified to exhibit” does an enormous amount of unexamined work; “a broad range of domains” has no test attached to it; and Gary Marcus, who is nobody's idea of a lab loyalist, has published his opposition. A threshold that loose is not a threshold, it is a prohibition with a preamble. But the laboratories' refusal to name a number and a senator's willingness to name an unusable one are evidence of the same underlying fact, which is that nobody has a measure. Only one of the two parties looks embarrassed about it.

Every lab that asked for capability checkpoints left the capability blank. The bill that fills it in attaches twenty years to the answer.

Who came to the Pro-Human Assembly

Five constituencies that agree on almost nothing else, in one room, for a day. The only announced speaker who had personally helped train a frontier model — Jacob Coxon, lately of OpenAI and Anthropic — told the organisers on Monday he would not be coming.

Business · Anthropic

Two Finance Products in Two Days, and Only One of Them Has an Architecture

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 15, 2026

On Monday, at the Future Proof festival in Huntington Beach, Anthropic launched Claude for Financial Advisors, wiring Claude into the software wealth managers already run: BlackRock's Advisor Center for model portfolios, portfolio construction and institutional analytics; Charles Schwab's Advisor Services for balances, positions, transactions, cost basis, alerts and money movement; then Addepar, Envestnet, iCapital, Orion, Wealthbox, Wealth.com and Zocks. The jobs are research, portfolio review, meeting preparation, documentation, prospect intake and compliance screening. Investment recommendations and client communications remain subject to human approval, and client data, per the launch coverage, stays on the custodians' servers rather than moving.

On Tuesday, testers pulling apart the Claude iOS app found an unshipped tab called Money. “Understand your money with Claude,” the onboarding screen says, above an option to link your bank accounts — ongoing access to the feed, rather than the statement you upload once and forget. There is no announcement, no date, no web equivalent and no published policy, for the excellent reason that it has not launched. Unshipped strings are how software works and nobody has done anything wrong.

It is still a contrast in postures, arriving twenty-four hours apart. The customers who have compliance departments get an architecture in which their clients' data does not move and a human signs the consequential outputs. The retail build, as leaked, opens with a button that connects a bank. Both may end up equally well governed. Only one of them has been asked.

Markets

The Confounder Got Bigger

By The Editor, Markets Desk · September 15, 2026

Tuesday's tape does this paper no favours. The S&P 500 closed down 0.45 per cent at 7,585.73, the Nasdaq Composite down 0.78 at 25,981.57, the Dow off 328 points at 52,093.11 — a second consecutive losing session, but not the same session twice. The ten-year Treasury yield printed 5.041 per cent, its highest since 2007, with the Federal Reserve's decision due Wednesday and Brent above $105. Several of the semiconductors worst hit on Monday went the other way: AMD up around 2 per cent, Qualcomm around 4. BofA's Vivek Arya spent the day raising his 2030 chip-market estimate from $2.7tn to $3.2tn. SoftBank's $25.9bn bridge repayment was due today; we found no confirmation it settled.

Yesterday this desk argued that Monday's split tape priced Amodei's diagnosis and declined his remedy. We named the yield and the crude as confounders in the same breath, and today the yield is the story and the chips are bouncing. That does not retire the reading — the sign split was real, and a four-per-cent move in a broad cyber index against sixteen in a pure play is not what a rates story looks like. It does mean the honest version is one day old and one day thin. What would settle it is whether Zscaler, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto held Monday's gains on Tuesday, and we could not find out: not one outlet we can reach printed a Tuesday figure for any of the three.

Editor's Note

Nine accounts of Beijing's reply crossed this desk today and every headline on them used a verb of attack. The paragraph in which China Daily says the two countries should cooperate where neither can manage the consequences alone, and points at a bilateral AI-safety dialogue that is already planned, was in none of them. None of this is a mark against the essay. Its first step — an outside team with employee-like access, permitted to publish things Anthropic would rather it did not — is the most expensive thing any laboratory has volunteered this year, Anthropic is doing it alone, and the man signed it with his own name on his own domain. It is a mark against the reading.

Sort Tuesday's two answers by whether the party giving one can act on it. Beijing pointed at a room its own government can convene on a Tuesday afternoon and adjourn by Wednesday. Sanders pointed at a bill he will introduce next week, in a Congress that has still not answered the letter twenty-nine of its own members sent in August asking merely for testimony, carrying a twenty-year sentence and a definition that would not survive a markup. One reply costs nothing and can be delivered; the other costs everything and cannot. And in that ballroom, among three hundred people arguing about a machine, there was exactly one person on the programme who had helped build one. He told the organisers on Monday that he would not be coming. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
