Research · OpenAI

An Intern Is Defined by Supervision. OpenAI Counted Everything Else.

By The Editor, Research Desk · September 7, 2026

On Saturday OpenAI published an account of what its own coding agents are doing to its own research organisation, and declared that it had hit the “automated research intern” milestone it set itself last autumn. The headline figure: as of mid-August the research org was drawing on 3.1 agent-workdays of effort for every workday of human labour, an agent-workday being measured against a standard eight-hour shift. Underneath it, the spend. At the start of the year the median researcher used agents sparingly; by mid-August the median researcher was running more than $600 a day of inference at API prices, and the 90th percentile was running more than $7,000 a day. Experiments per active experimenter hit an all-time high in August, against a series that starts in January 2025.

Now the part that deserves the credit, and it deserves quite a lot of it. The same post publishes the number that complicates its own headline: over the last six months, more than half of successful four-to-eight-hour tasks involved one or more human interventions, and agents “still require significant human steering to be successful, especially as task complexity rises.” It says the measurements are preliminary and that the overall pace of research will not keep up with the specific metrics. A company shipping a productivity claim with the caveat that undercuts it attached, unprompted, in the same document, is rare enough that it should be said plainly rather than buried in a clause.

Which brings us to the word. Jakub Pachocki told MIT Technology Review in March how he tells the two goals apart: “The way I would distinguish a research intern from a full automated researcher is the span of time that we would have it work mostly autonomously.” So the milestone, by the company’s own definition, is a claim about autonomy. Look at what the post actually measures. Agent-workdays are inference divided by a notional shift — a consumption figure. Dollars per researcher per day is a bill. Experiments per experimenter is volume. Every one of those goes up when you buy more tokens. The single number that speaks to the definition appears once, has no trend line attached, and says a person had to come and look more often than not.

None of which makes 3.1 a fudge. It is a genuinely useful internal metric and I would like every lab to publish one. It is just not a measure of the thing the milestone is named after, and the company knows it, which is why the intervention rate is in there at all.

Agent-workdays measure what was purchased. An intern is measured by how often somebody has to come and look. — on the one number in the post with no history attached

Policy · Massachusetts

Nobody in Massachusetts Is Arguing About Whether to Be Evaluated

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 7, 2026

The Massachusetts Senate passed an economic development bill in late July carrying AI guardrails aimed at frontier developers — companies above roughly $500m of AI-derived revenue or $1bn of R&D spend. It requires them to publish a safety framework addressed to catastrophic risk, hands enforcement to the state attorney general, and in the Senate’s version requires independent evaluators to assess models every four months. It is in bicameral negotiation now. Anthropic backs it and would accept four to six months; OpenAI and Google want something closer to the Illinois model, an annual third-party audit. Anthropic’s Cesar Fernandez: “we ultimately don’t think the industry should grade its own homework.” OpenAI’s Donnie Fowler, on the patchwork risk: inconsistency “doesn’t mean safer.” Both companies have hired Boston lobbyists.

Strip out the press releases and there is less daylight here than the coverage suggests. Both firms already publish a safety framework voluntarily. Both already commission outside evaluations. Neither is arguing that independent assessment is a bad idea. What is actually in dispute is a frequency and a defendant: three evaluations a year or one, and whether a document a company writes about itself becomes a thing a state attorney general can sue over. The cadence is the argument on the record. The attorney general is the argument underneath it.

Silicon · DeepSeek

DeepSeek Ordered 160,000 Training Chips and Does Not Plan to Train on Them

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · September 7, 2026

Bloomberg reported on Friday that DeepSeek plans to order 160,000 Huawei Ascend 950DT accelerators for a gigawatt-scale site at Ulanqab, in Inner Mongolia, about 350km north-west of Beijing — a location picked for an average annual temperature around 4°C and cheap power, which is to say for the two line items nobody puts in a press release. Partial operation is targeted for late 2027 or early 2028. Secondary coverage derives a face value near ¥17.8bn, roughly $2.6bn, from a per-chip price of ¥111,000. Neither DeepSeek nor Huawei has confirmed any of it.

Two facts in the reporting are doing more work than the headline number. The first: Huawei designed and marketed the 950DT for training, the harder and more demanding of the two jobs, and DeepSeek does not currently plan to use it for that. It is buying a training chip to run inference. The second: component shortages, advanced memory chief among them, are expected to hold 2026 production of the part to somewhere between 200,000 and 300,000 units. One order, from one customer, is therefore between about half and four-fifths of everything the chip is expected to exist in this year.

There are two readings and neither is flattering to somebody. Either DeepSeek does not think the 950DT is good enough to train on, which is a verdict on Huawei that Huawei will not enjoy. Or it thinks the binding constraint has moved to serving queries rather than making models — which is what Dell’s management said out loud a fortnight ago and what Coatue is reportedly building a vehicle around. Third possibility, of course: it is the only thing available, and a chip you can actually take delivery of beats a better one you cannot.

A chip built for the harder job, bought in bulk for the easier one, by a customer with nowhere else to shop. — on 160,000 accelerators bound for Inner Mongolia

Google Assistant

It is survived by Gemini, which is cleverer, more articulate, and has never once been asked to convert ounces to grams by somebody whose hands were covered in flour.

Assistant spent ten years answering the same four questions. What is the weather. Set a timer for eight minutes. Turn off the kitchen light. Play the other one. Its ambitions were narrow, widely mocked, and met almost entirely — which puts it ahead of most of the software this paper writes about. Removal from Android and Wear OS began on Friday and will run for several weeks depending on device and region. Google notes that once your device has switched, you cannot switch back, which is the most candid sentence in any deprecation notice we have read this year.

It lives on, for now, in cars with Google built-in, in Google Home devices, and in televisions. A decade of work, and the estate is a set of appliances.

Editor’s Note

OpenAI says it has built a research intern. I am going to take the word, because nobody made the company use it. An intern is not defined by throughput. Anybody who has ever had one knows what defines an intern, and it is how often you have to walk over and look. OpenAI publishes that figure — more than half of the successful long tasks needed somebody to step in — and measures everything else in agent-workdays and dollars per person per day, which are units of consumption. The one number that matches the word is the only one in the post with no trend line under it.

Massachusetts is arguing about the same thing with the tense changed. A Senate bill would make large developers hire independent evaluators every four months; OpenAI prefers the Illinois cadence, once a year, and says a patchwork of state rules makes nobody safer, which is true and is also not an answer to how often. Jakub Pachocki’s reported view is that confidence in monitoring, rather than raw capability, will increasingly set the pace of this industry. Massachusetts has proposed a number for that. One of these is a principle and one of them is a figure you can be sued over, and it is very easy to agree with a principle.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
