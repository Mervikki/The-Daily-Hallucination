Frontier Models · Anthropic

This Time, Anthropic Didn't Grade Its Own Homework.

By The Editor, Models Desk · July 27, 2026

On Thursday Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5 the way every lab ships a model — with a page of benchmarks, all of them its own. Over the weekend the outside graders arrived. The ARC Prize Foundation scored Opus 5 at 30.16% on ARC-AGI-3, roughly four times the previous public-leaderboard best; Artificial Analysis independently measured GDPval-AA v2 at 1,861 — the exact figure Anthropic had claimed on launch day. At $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output, half of what the company charges for its Fable 5 flagship, it is the uncommon release whose numbers survived contact with someone who didn't write them.

Keep the enthusiasm in proportion. ARC-AGI-3 is a deliberately brutal reasoning test, and “four times better” describes a model that still fails it roughly seven times in ten. The interesting thing was never going to be the number itself — it's the provenance. A lab controls every figure it prints on a launch slide and none of the figures a stranger measures a week later, and those two sets of numbers are, most weeks, politely different. Here they matched. That is rarer than it ought to be, and worth marking on the days it happens.

The Number Anthropic Didn't Set: ARC-AGI-3

ARC-AGI-3 figures per the ARC Prize Foundation; the prior public-leaderboard best is approximate (Opus 5 lands roughly 4× higher). A single independent evaluator, not a broad panel — but not the vendor, either.

Remember what “Opus” used to be at this company: the tier you paid a premium for. It is now the value tier, priced at half the flagship, and this week the outside record caught up with the marketing and agreed the value tier had very nearly caught the flagship. Anthropic spent Wednesday of last week in Washington calling a cheap model that matches an expensive one a crime; it spent this weekend having strangers confirm it had shipped one on purpose. Both can be true at once, which is roughly the state of the whole industry.

A lab controls every number on its launch slide and none of the numbers a stranger measures a week later. This week, for once, the two agreed.

The fair caveats: these are two named evaluators, not a jury, and “independent” in AI benchmarking means “a different party ran a similar test,” not gospel. But set Opus 5 beside the model one column over — the one whose makers published a leaderboard rank and quietly left the hallucination rate off the chart — and the contrast is the entire story of the week. One model handed its numbers to someone else and they held. The other made sure no one else got to choose which number counted.

Open Models · Kimi K3

The Largest Open Model Ever Left One Number Off the Slide.

By The Editor, Open Models Desk · July 27, 2026

Moonshot's Kimi K3 — the 2.8-trillion-parameter model whose open weights were scheduled to land today, under a Modified MIT license with a carve-out for anyone past 100 million users or $20 million in revenue — spent last week selling a single figure: 57.1 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, fourth overall, a rung above a frontier flagship or two. As of press time the download had not been independently confirmed live from where we sit, which changes nothing about the number that matters.

Because there is a number a magazine with this masthead goes looking for first, and it was not on the chart. Independent testing of the hosted preview put K3's hallucination rate at roughly 51% on fact-sensitive prompts — it invents, on that measure, about as often as it doesn't — and Moonshot did not include it in the materials it did choose to publish. A leaderboard rank tells you how a model does on its best axis, on its best day. The hallucination rate tells you the interest rate on believing it.

Models · Google

Google Shipped Three Gemini Models. The One Everyone Wanted Wasn't Among Them.

By The Editor, Models Desk · July 27, 2026

On July 21 Google released Gemini 3.6 Flash, 3.5 Flash-Lite, and 3.5 Flash Cyber. It did not release Gemini 3.5 Pro — the flagship leaked, dated, and re-dated since spring, and reportedly torn down and rebuilt from the base model after it missed Google's own quality bar on hallucination and reliability (Bloomberg, July 16). Polymarket's window for a Pro launch now sits around July 31, where its previous windows also sat.

There is a particular shape to a company that can ship the trim around a thing but not the thing itself. The Flash line is the frame, and the frame is now complete; it's the picture that's missing. Google has missed enough of its own Pro dates that the schedule stopped being news a while ago — the slippage is the product update, and it ships on time.

Personal Agents · Nous

The Open Agent Nobody Can Review Just Priced Itself at $1.5 Billion.

By The Editor, Personal Agents Desk · July 27, 2026

Nous Research, maker of the open-source Hermes agent, is reported to be closing roughly $75 million at a $1.5 billion valuation, led by Robot Ventures with Union Square Ventures in — a ~50% step-up from a round that closed under three months ago, bringing the total raised to about $145 million.

Set that beside the deadline everyone else is watching. Washington is finalizing a framework to give federal agencies a 30-day pre-release look at frontier models by Aug. 1 — a review that binds the labs shipping behind an API and, by construction, can't touch a model whose weights leave the building as a download. Hermes is exactly that kind of model, and its backers just put a billion and a half on the one thing the reviewers were never going to be allowed to read.

Editor's Note

This week the outside graders showed up, and you could sort the frontier by how each model took a grade it didn't write. Anthropic handed Opus 5 to two independent evaluators and its own launch numbers held — half the price of its flagship, and now with a score the marketing department didn't get to pick. Moonshot handed Kimi K3 to a leaderboard, won a good rank, and left the hallucination rate — the number that says how much of the output you can actually trust — off the chart entirely. Google's flagship couldn't get to the exam room at all.

None of that is a rhyme; it's a sort. The same event — somebody other than the maker running the numbers — flattered the first model, embarrassed the second, and skipped the third for want of a model to test. What it clarifies is small and durable: every lab publishes the figure it chose, measured on the axis it wins, and the one worth reading is almost always the one that didn't make the slide. Opus 5's happened to survive the audit. The trouble is how few models ever get audited at all. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
