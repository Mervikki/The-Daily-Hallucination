Markets · Stripe

The Industry's Only Neutral Scoreboard Now Has an Owner

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 21, 2026

Stripe said on Wednesday that it has agreed to acquire OpenRouter, and disclosed no terms. OpenRouter is the gateway a very large number of companies use to reach models they have not signed contracts with — its own description is routing and optimising token usage across more than four hundred models from over eighty providers. Bloomberg puts the price above $7 billion; the New York Times has it at roughly $7.5 billion, of which $1.5 billion goes to the founders and $6 billion to investors; a third account says more than $8 billion. Neither company has confirmed any of them.

Seven months, one gateway, one repricing

The left dot is a financing that closed. The right end is not a number at all — it is the spread between three newspapers, because Stripe published none. The six-times is our arithmetic on the midpoint.

Take the commercial logic seriously, because it is better than the headline suggests. Stripe's whole business is standing between a buyer and a seller and taking an accurate reading. OpenRouter is a company that already does exactly that, for tokens: it watches four hundred models get called, priced and billed, across vendors who would each prefer you saw only their own dashboard. Stripe did not buy a model company or a research lab. It bought the meter, and the meter was arguably the most defensible asset in the sector, because nobody has to trust it — they only have to trust that it has no reason to lie.

Alex Atallah's pitch was that OpenRouter was the Stripe for AI, and that it prevented lock-in. Stripe agreed with the first half.

Which is the awkward part, and it is awkward for us as much as for anybody. OpenRouter's public rankings are one of the very few usage numbers in this industry produced by a party with no stake in the result — this paper has a standing rule against printing agent-adoption figures unless they come from there, precisely because everything else on offer is a vendor's slide. That rule was never about OpenRouter being clever. It was about OpenRouter being disinterested. As of Wednesday it is a subsidiary of a company with a strategy in this market, which does not make the rankings wrong and does not make Stripe a villain. It just means the sentence "according to an independent source" has one fewer place to point.

Nobody has alleged that anything will change, and I do not expect a crude thumb on the scale; that would destroy the asset Stripe just paid for. The quieter risk is the ordinary one — that a metering business owned by a payments company starts optimising for what payments companies find interesting, and the public leaderboard becomes a marketing surface rather than a by-product. It will happen slowly enough that nobody will be able to date it.

Chips · Google

Google Will Be Paid in Marvell Stock for Buying Marvell Chips

By The Editor, Chips Desk · August 21, 2026

Marvell will co-develop Google's custom AI silicon — processors, storage management and the networking parts that sit around the TPUs — and as part of the arrangement Google receives warrants over roughly 59 million Marvell shares at $206.58 each, about $12.2 billion at the strike. Around 1.4 million of those vest in the first year. The rest vest in tranches, one for every $500 million of chips Google buys. Marvell rose about ten per cent on the day. Broadcom, which held this seat, fell five.

This paper spent late July on the observation that a supplier had begun underwriting its own customer's demand. Today's deal is that arrangement turned inside out: here the customer is compensated, in the supplier's own equity, for being the demand. Every $500 million Google spends buys it a little more of the company it is spending with, at a price fixed on Wednesday. It is a volume discount, and the discount is denominated in other people's confidence — Google only collects if the market keeps believing the deal is a good one. The talk of $120 billion of revenue through fiscal 2033 is a ceiling somebody drew, not a commitment anybody signed, and it should be read the way one reads a brochure.

Courts · Meta

Meta's Own Safety Veteran Was Read the Company Line, and Declined to Agree With It

By The Editor, Courts Desk · August 21, 2026

Arturo Béjar, who spent eight years working on safety at Meta, testified in Oakland on Wednesday that Mark Zuckerberg's public claim that the company puts safety ahead of profit is, in his words, "not an accurate statement." The trial before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers opened on Tuesday, is expected to run six or seven weeks, and carries reported exposure of up to $1.4 trillion along with the possibility of court-ordered product changes. Zuckerberg is on the states' witness list for three hours.

We cover this because of what is in the complaint rather than who is in the dock: infinite scroll, autoplay, the like button and the recommendation algorithms. It is the first proceeding of its size in which a ranking system is a named cause of harm rather than a background fact, and a jury is going to be asked to hold an objective function in its head for six weeks. Whatever the verdict, the transcript is going to be the most detailed public account of how a recommender was tuned that anybody has ever obtained without a subpoena — and it is being obtained with one.

Agents · Anthropic

Claude Can Drive a Browser Now. You Have to Bring the Browser.

By The Editor, Agents Desk · August 21, 2026

Anthropic's release notes on Wednesday carried the largest batch of general-availability announcements the platform has had in months, and almost nobody wrote about it. Computer use — the thing that gets demonstrated on stage — left beta as computer_toolset_20260801, gaining batch actions and zoom by default. Alongside it arrived something new: a browser tool, browser_toolset_20260801, which does not drive a whole desktop but works inside a viewport, reading the accessibility tree, the elements, the forms and the tabs. Files, Agent Skills and enterprise user management all reached general availability the same day. Managed agents can now be fenced to allowed or blocked domains, and self-hosted sandboxes can mount a memory store.

The engineering here deserves plain credit. Pointing a model at a virtual desktop and asking it to find the button by looking at pixels was always the demo, not the product; reading a page's accessibility tree is more reliable, cheaper, and — the part that matters commercially — auditable, because every action has a name instead of a coordinate. This is the version that survives contact with a compliance department. It is also, notably, five models wide on day one: Fable 5, Mythos 5, Opus 5, Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.8.

Anthropic writes the tool definitions. You supply the browser, the machine, the network address and the consequences.

Two details are worth pausing on. The first is the word client: this is a client toolset, which in plain English means Anthropic specifies what the tools do and you run them, on infrastructure you own, from an IP address that belongs to you, inside sessions you are logged into. Every awkward question about an agent filling in a form on a real website now has an answer, and the answer is your company. The second is a number the documentation states without comment — one toolset entry expands, by default, into twenty-seven member tools. There is a version of this industry in which "just add one line to your request" is how twenty-seven capabilities enter production without a review meeting, and we appear to be living in it.

One unrelated line from the same page, filed under credit where it is due: Sonnet 5's introductory pricing of $2 and $10 per million tokens was made permanent on 10 August, and the rise to $3 and $15 that had been scheduled for 1 September will not happen. Announced price increases usually arrive. This one was quietly cancelled, and nobody sent a press release about being cheaper than they promised to be.

Lifecycle · Anthropic

Two Dates on Anthropic's Own Page Stopped Meaning Anything Last Week

By The Editor, Lifecycle Desk · August 21, 2026

Anthropic's deprecation page makes a commitment: at least sixty days' notice before a publicly released model is retired. It also lists tentative retirement dates for models still running. Claude Sonnet 4.5 says "not sooner than September 29, 2026." Claude Haiku 4.5 says "not sooner than October 15, 2026." Neither has been deprecated. Neither, therefore, can be retired on those dates: the sixty-day clock has not started, so Sonnet 4.5's date became unreachable on 31 July, and Haiku 4.5's went the same way on 16 August, five days ago. Nobody has announced anything. The dates simply aged out of the realm of the possible while sitting on a public page.

How much notice Anthropic actually gives

Days from announcement to the model going dark, counted off Anthropic's own deprecation history. The commitment is a floor. For four consecutive retirements it has also been the ceiling.

This is not a scandal, and the arithmetic cuts kindly as often as not: a company that says "at least sixty days" and then delivers sixty, sixty-one, sixty-two and sixty-two is keeping its word to the day. But a floor that is never exceeded is a schedule, and a schedule is what customers actually plan against. The practical reading for anyone still running Sonnet 4.5 is that you have until at least late October, whatever the page says — and the more interesting reading is that the two nearest dates in Anthropic's published lifecycle are now decorative. Everything on that page is checkable by anyone with a calendar, which is more than can be said for most of the numbers in this issue.

The Quiet Correction · Revisiting Issues 99 and 101

The Outage That Closed On Time

What We Said

Three Claude outages in five days. Tuesday's, we wrote, "cleared without anybody saying when," and we drew the chart with a dashed edge to prove it. No cause was ever given.

What Turns Out To Be True

There were four. A 12 August incident we left out as unverified was flagged at 13:50 UTC and resolved at 18:07, with four of six services degraded — so the run was four in seven days, not three in five. Tuesday's impact window was published: 16:11 to 18:23 UTC, two hours and twelve minutes, an ordinary length for an ordinary failure. And a cause has been offered, attributed to Anthropic and pointing at demand outrunning capacity, with infrastructure "stretched to meet it, particularly at peak hours."

Nobody Announced Any Of This

There was no postmortem, no statement, no correction to correct. The numbers were on the status page the entire time. A paper that spent a whole lead reading a status page as an accidental architecture diagram never did get round to reading the status page.

Editor's Note

There is a standing instruction in this paper's style file, written after a fortnight in which we could not print a single trustworthy number about a personal-agent company: never quote anybody's usage figures unless they come from OpenRouter's public rankings, because everything else on offer is a press release with a chart on it. On Wednesday, OpenRouter agreed to be bought.

I do not expect Stripe to put a thumb on the scale; that would destroy the thing it just paid for. That isn't the point. This industry produces three kinds of number — the ones labs publish about themselves, the ones anonymous people hand to Bloomberg, and the ones counted by somebody with no stake in the answer. We have spent a hundred issues complaining about the first two. The third category was essentially one company, and it now has a shareholder.

Which is why the smallest story in today's paper is the one I would defend hardest. Anthropic promises at least sixty days before it switches a model off, and has delivered sixty, sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-two — so precisely that two dates on its own page have quietly become impossible, a thing any reader can establish with a calendar and nobody's permission. One of today's instruments stopped being independent this week. The other never was, and you can still catch it out. I know which I would rather be left with. See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
