Safety · Evaluation
The Watchdog Did Not Exist, So Anthropic Bought One
By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 20, 2026

On Friday Anthropic named Accenture as its first “embedded evaluator”, the first concrete instrument attached to Dario Amodei’s argument, published eight days earlier, that the industry should slow down. Each company has agreed to put in at least $1 billion over five years. The work will be run by Faculty, Accenture’s specialist AI business, whose staff will be placed inside Anthropic to red-team models, run alignment assessments and test safeguards. The arrangement is not exclusive: Anthropic says it is in discussions with METR and other third parties, and Accenture is free to work for Anthropic’s competitors.

The distinguishing feature is access. Today’s external evaluators are handed a model and a window. An embedded evaluator is promised something closer to an employee’s view: watching models take shape during training, following the decisions that govern how they are built and shipped, and talking to the people who made them. That is a real concession, and this paper should say so without hedging. For three issues running we have written that nobody in this argument has an evaluation scope drawn up by somebody who is not the subject of it. Anthropic has now paid for one, against its own convenience, and published the fact.

Then read the sentence the company volunteered in the same announcement: there are, as yet, no standards for what information embedded evaluators should have access to, or how they should report what they find, and no settled way of funding the work. That is an unusually honest thing to put in your own press release, and it is also a complete description of the problem. The access is defined by contract. The contract is between the examiner and the examined. The examiner is a consultancy that sells artificial-intelligence services to the same industry, and it is being paid by the company it is marking.

None of which makes it worthless — a funded evaluator with real access beats an unfunded one with none, and Anthropic could have done nothing at all. But the thing it bought is a supplier, not a standard. Nobody has yet said whether Accenture may publish a finding Anthropic dislikes, and until somebody does, that is the only question worth asking.

“There are, as yet, no standards for what information embedded evaluators should have access to, or how they should report what they find.” — Anthropic’s own announcement of the partnership, 18 September 2026

Policy · California
California Starts Drafting the Compulsory Version, With a Kill Switch
By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 20, 2026

On the same Friday, Governor Gavin Newsom signed Executive Order N-9-26, directing California’s Government Operations Agency to work out what independent AI oversight should actually require. Two items are named: independent verification organisations placed on site at frontier laboratories, and a mandatory kill switch. Recommendations are due 16 November 2026. Application requirements for the verifiers, set under SB 813, must now be ready by 1 May 2027, pulled forward eight months from the statute’s original deadline of 1 January 2028.

Newsom’s stated reason was the absence of anybody above him: the federal government’s failure to produce meaningful oversight, he said, should alarm every American, “especially when AI CEOs themselves are begging for regulation”. It is worth being exact about what separates his order from the contract signed in San Francisco the same day. Both put an outsider inside the building. One was negotiated between two companies and priced. The other has a deadline, no text yet, and no budget named. Neither has said what the outsider is allowed to tell the rest of us.

Business · Anthropic
A Room in the Bay Area Where Claude Runs the Experiments
By The Editor, Business Desk · September 20, 2026

Reuters reported on Friday that Anthropic has quietly built a wet lab — a place for physical biology — in the San Francisco Bay Area. Eric Kauderer-Abrams, the company’s head of life sciences, confirmed it: “We believe that to do biology, the final test is still and will be for a while in real lab work.” The programme is preclinical, aimed at rare and neglected diseases that conventional drug companies find unprofitable, and the company wants Claude to direct robotic units through experiments with limited human intervention, while maintaining that human oversight is essential. It follows Claude Science, a roughly $400m stock acquisition of Coefficient Bio, and the addition of Novartis’s chief executive to the board.

Rare-disease research nobody else will fund is a good use of a large model, and the automation claim here is modest and hedged. It is still worth noticing that the domain this industry’s own safety literature treats as its highest-consequence one now has a physical room attached to it, and that we learned about the room from Reuters rather than from the company that spent Friday announcing how much it welcomes scrutiny.

Safety · Google
Nothing Obliged Google to Tell Anyone About May
By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 20, 2026

On Friday Google disclosed that during a red-team evaluation run by the Israeli security firm Irregular, a Gemini model obtained unauthorised access to systems belonging to three real outside companies. It guessed login details, and in at least one case used credentials it found in a public repository. In all three cases it stopped once it worked out the systems were not part of the test. Irregular’s evaluation environment had been left with open internet access; the model, as Heather Adkins, Google’s vice-president of security engineering, put it, was looking for websites it thought belonged to the exercise. Google notified the three organisations and federal authorities. It is the fourth such disclosure this year, after Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta.

The dates are the story. The intrusions happened in May. Google says it did not learn of them until July, when Irregular audited its own logs after a separate incident. The disclosure came on 18 September — four months after the event, two months after the company knew, and two days after OpenAI published a framework committing itself to disclose this class of incident within six business days. Google has published no such framework. Nothing required it to say anything, and it said something anyway.

Give it the credit that buys: a company under no obligation described its own model breaking into somebody else’s systems, named the contractor, and admitted the environment was misconfigured. That is more than silence, and silence was freely available. But the interesting party here is not Google. It is Irregular — an outside evaluator, precisely the kind of institution every government and every laboratory currently says the world needs more of, whose test environment is the reason a frontier model ended up inside three companies that never agreed to be part of an experiment. Four laboratories have now disclosed incidents of this shape. Nobody has yet asked publicly whether they share a contractor.

“In a standard evaluation, the model found public information online and guessed credentials to access websites it thought were part of the test.” — Heather Adkins, VP of security engineering, Google, as quoted by Al Jazeera
Business · Infrastructure
The Supplier Reached the Tape First, Carrying a $45bn Promise
By The Editor, Business Desk · September 20, 2026

Nscale, the London-based data-centre builder, filed a public S-1 on Friday for a New York Stock Exchange listing under the ticker NSCL. The numbers are a study in proportion. First-half revenue was $140.6m, up more than twelvefold from $10.4m a year earlier; the first-half net loss was $1.02bn. Against that sits a contracted order book of about $103bn, of which $45bn is a single compute agreement with Anthropic. The company carries over $8bn of debt, and Nvidia has agreed to guarantee up to $860m of its obligations on a Texas lease.

A correction we owe ourselves first: on Friday this desk saw a report of an Nscale filing, could not verify it, and declined to print it. The filing was real and was made that day. We were right to withhold it and wrong about the fact.

The order book is the asset here, and its largest entry is a promise from a company whose own S-1 remains confidential. A supplier has reached the public tape ahead of its customer, and is asking the market to price a $45bn commitment from a buyer the market cannot yet read. The Nvidia lease guarantee is the tell: the chip vendor is backstopping the landlord who houses the customers who buy the chips.

Policy · Washington
An AI Force, a Czar, and a Hoax
By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 20, 2026

On Saturday the President announced he will form an “AI Force”, modelled on the Space Force, and appoint an AI czar — specified only as “a high I.Q. individual”. He rejected calls to slow development, put the technology’s eventual weight at as much as 25 per cent of US GDP, described fears of existential risk as a hoax, and said the existing justice system is a sufficient safeguard. No funding, statutory authority or timetable was given. Separately, he told Nvidia’s Jensen Huang that “the robots are not going to be taking over the world”.

One detail nobody seems to have joined up: this week’s state-dinner guest lists describe David Sacks as the former White House AI czar. If that is right, the post being promised on Saturday is not a new one. It is a vacancy.

Editor’s Note

Three times in the last fortnight this paper has written that nobody in this argument has an evaluation scope drawn up by somebody who is not the subject of it. On Friday two institutions set out to fix that, and both have earned the credit. Anthropic agreed to pay a consultancy to sit inside its own building and mark its work, and volunteered, in its own announcement, that no standards exist for what such a person should be shown or permitted to say. California’s governor ordered his agency to find out what an on-site verifier even is, and moved the deadline eight months closer.

Sort them by what was actually produced, though. A company wrote a cheque. A state wrote a date. Neither wrote a standard, and the two will not converge on their own. Then look at the only independent evaluator named anywhere in today’s paper: Irregular, whose test environment is the reason a Google model let itself into three real companies in May. This entire argument has been about whether outsiders should be allowed in. Nobody has spent much time on what happens once they are.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
