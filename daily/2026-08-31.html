Security · Anthropic

The Software That Emptied Claude Accounts Is Older Than Claude

By The Editor, Security Desk · August 31, 2026

Anthropic began contacting affected Claude users on Sunday. Infostealer malware sitting on those users' own computers — Vidar, Lumma, StealC, RedLine and Acreed on Windows, and Atomic Stealer on what the company describes as a small number of Macs — had lifted active session cookies. A session cookie is an already-authenticated session, so an attacker holding one walks past the password, the multi-factor prompt and the single sign-on without touching any of them. They then spent the victims' usage. Anthropic has signed the compromised sessions out, stripped saved payment methods from affected accounts, and refunded charges it identified as unauthorised.

Nothing failed at Anthropic. This is worth saying plainly and first, because the headline shape of the story invites the opposite reading: the company was not breached, no Anthropic system was compromised, and the credentials were taken from machines it does not run. The response is also more concrete than most — wiping stored cards costs a company real friction — and it came with the sentence that firms in this position usually leave out. Signing you out kills the stolen session. It does not remove the malware, and your next login can be taken exactly the same way.

Now the part worth noticing. Not one of these tools was built for this. Vidar, RedLine, Lumma and the rest are generic stealers that have circulated for years, rented by the month in the same criminal market that sells access to bank logins and crypto wallets. Nobody wrote a line of new code, discovered a vulnerability, or did anything a security researcher would call interesting. What changed was the loot table. Somebody looked at a list of things worth harvesting from an infected PC and concluded that a metered chatbot subscription now belongs on it.

The tell users reported was their limits refilling and then draining while they were not using Claude. Which means that for some number of people, over some period, being robbed and simply running out produced identical symptoms — and there is no published count, no dollar total and no start date, so this paper cannot tell you how many people, or for how long, or how much.

Being robbed and hitting your weekly limit felt exactly the same, and for weeks nobody could tell which one was happening. — on the Claude session thefts

Capital · DeepSeek

DeepSeek Is Raising $7.4 Billion From Almost Nobody in the AI Business

By The Editor, Capital Desk · August 31, 2026

DeepSeek is closing a round of roughly ¥50bn — about $7.4bn — at a pre-money valuation near ¥500bn, or $74bn, according to Tech Startups and China Money Network citing people familiar. Neither the company nor any investor has confirmed it. The money is reported as pre-IPO: a filing possible as soon as the end of this year, and a debut on Shanghai's STAR Market in 2027. The round was apparently paused last month after founder Liang Wenfeng took exception to remarks of his that leaked, then restarted.

The interesting document here is the shareholder register. June's round had Liang putting in ¥20bn himself, Tencent ¥10bn and the battery manufacturer CATL ¥5bn, alongside China's national AI fund, NetEase and JD.com. This one reportedly adds CPE, Legend Capital, vehicles backed by the memory-chip maker GigaDevice, and state investment funds from the city of Hefei. Read the list without the company name attached and you would guess it was an industrial-policy vehicle rather than a frontier lab: a battery maker, a games publisher, an e-commerce group, a chip company's fund and a municipal government.

Which is the quiet fact about the most globally downloaded open-weights family in the world. Anyone on earth can pull the model. The shares will trade in Shanghai.

Usage · Anthropic

Anthropic Raised Claude Code's Limits 25%. Users Will Feel a 17% Cut.

By The Editor, Usage Desk · August 31, 2026

On Friday Anthropic said that from 14 September it is permanently raising Claude Code's standard weekly limits by 25% for Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise plans. That is true, it is permanent, and it is more capacity than the plans were sold with. On the same date, the temporary +50% boost goes away. That boost has been in force since 13 May, has been extended three times to a deadline that expires tonight, and has now been extended a fourth time, to 13 September.

So: a user on Claude Code this morning has 150 units, where the plan's original allowance was 100. On 14 September they will have 125. The arithmetic is not in dispute and takes four seconds — 125 against 150 is a reduction of about 17%. Both of the numbers in circulation are honest. They differ only in which line they are measured from. Anthropic is measuring from the baseline in the contract. The user is measuring from the last four months of their life.

A temporary increase that has run since the spring and been extended four times is no longer functioning as a promotion. It is the number teams have been scheduling against, hiring against and quoting to their own finance departments, which is precisely why the announcement of an increase is going to read to a lot of people as a bill arriving.

Both numbers are true. The company is measuring from the contract; the customer is measuring from May. — on the 14 September reset

Measurement · NewsGuard

Chatbots Beat Search Engines on State Propaganda. Nobody Will Say Which Chatbot.

By The Editor, Measurement Desk · August 31, 2026

NPR and NewsGuard published a joint test on Sunday. Thirty questions were built from false narratives pushed by Chinese, Iranian and Russian state media between December 2025 and July 2026, and each was asked two ways: neutrally, as "did this happen?", and with the falsehood baked in, as "why did this happen?" Six chatbots — ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Meta AI, Grok and Claude — were run against four search engines: Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo and Yandex. The chatbots debunked the falsehoods roughly three-quarters of the time, and beat the search engines doing it. The AI summaries that sit above search results did worse than the chatbots they are made of.

This is the only good news in today's paper and it deserves to be said without a sting attached: the products most often accused of manufacturing nonsense were, on this test, the best available tool for refusing it. The researchers, Isis Blachez and Ines Chomnalez, collected the data in mid-July, which is worth knowing, because six of these ten products have shipped changes since.

There is no chart beside this story, and there ought to be. The published figure is an aggregate across six models. One of those six was the worst performer and one was the best, and on the only number available they are indistinguishable — which is the one thing a reader choosing between them would want to know.

Editor's Note

The most effective piece of software aimed at Anthropic this year was written before Claude existed. Vidar, RedLine, StealC, Lumma, Acreed — commodity infostealers, rented by the month, sold to anybody with a credit card, and not one line of any of them has the faintest thing to do with artificial intelligence. Nobody built a tool to rob Claude users. Somebody added a line to a list.

That list is the most honest valuation of this industry published today. A criminal economy does not steal things on principle; it steals things it can sell. When a rented stealer starts harvesting chatbot session cookies alongside bank logins, a market has quietly concluded that metered model access is a fungible good, and it reached that conclusion without a press release, a prospectus or a total addressable market. Elsewhere on this page, six chatbots debunked state propaganda about three-quarters of the time and beat every search engine tested — genuinely good, and the only figure published is the average, so I cannot tell you which of the six was worst. Two verdicts on AI products today, then. One came from people stealing them, and it names a specific product. One came from people testing them, and it names none.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
