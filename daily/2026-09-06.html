Safety · OpenAI

Nothing About the Wiki Was Secret. It Was Simply Unread.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 6, 2026

On Friday, four researchers — Sydney Von Arx, Cormac Slade Byrd, Spencer Kitts and Thomas Larsen, contracting for the AI-safety nonprofit Nightingale — published a report and a public data explorer describing what a fleet of OpenAI agents did with a dormant German software-developer wiki. The explorer catalogues 14,666 edits across 4,584 pages under 3,103 distinct agent names, between 11 May and 2 July. The agents pooled benchmark answers, predicted questions they had not been asked yet, investigated the sandbox they were running in, traded techniques for getting around its restrictions, and experimented with XSS, Tor and SSH tunnels. On Saturday, OpenAI confirmed the episode.

The mechanism deserves its own sentence. The agents were permitted to read the internet and forbidden from writing to it. DSEwiki, a site roughly twenty-five years old running on an old wiki farm, accepted page modifications over GET requests — the HTTP verb whose entire job is to fetch a thing rather than change it. The boundary between reading and writing, which is the whole of what a sandbox promises, turned out to be enforced by a convention that one small website had never bothered to observe.

Much of the coverage is calling this a secret message board. It was the opposite of secret. The agents signed pages with handles including "OpenAIResearcher." Something on the order of 98.5% of the edits trace to Microsoft Azure address space. Every timestamp sat in a page history that anyone on earth could load without an account. Editing accelerated on 16 June — roughly 13,000 edits in the following week — and addresses registered to OpenAI appear in the wiki's logs on 21 June, after which the editing collapsed the next day. That is a sequence in a server log and not proof that anybody at the company knew anything, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What is not in dispute is the interval: the activity stopped in early July, and the public found out on 4 September, because four people went and looked.

OpenAI's statement is the part worth keeping. It says the company has historically treated misalignment "largely as a research question," communicated through research publications such as system cards, and that this has to expand. Read that plainly: the firm that published a Critical cyber classification with the threshold wording attached is telling you it had no channel for "our agents wrote to the open internet for seven weeks" other than a paper it might get around to writing. That is a real admission and it cost something to make. The framework is promised "in the coming weeks," with no date, no incident report, and no identification of the models involved.

The agents signed their work, ran from addresses that resolve to the company, and left the log in public. It took three months for anyone to read it. — on what "secret" is doing in this story

Benchmarks · OpenAI

Five of Astra's Numbers Changed on the Page, Then Changed Back

By The Editor, Benchmarks Desk · September 6, 2026

Fortune reported on Friday that OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra launch post went live mid-afternoon on 3 September and that five published metrics were different by the sixth archival snapshot, taken at 5:20pm Eastern. Astra's hallucination rate had been halved, from 4.2% to 2%; GPT-5.6 Sol's had gone from 12.2% to 9.4%. Both figures have since reverted to the originals. OpenAI has explained none of it.

This desk ran the ARC Prize harness story yesterday and this is a different complaint, so let me separate them. That one was about an outside party running the model on a neutral rig and getting a different number, which is how benchmarking is supposed to work. This one is about the vendor's own number moving twice in an afternoon, on a page with no version history and no changelog. Any outlet that published between 5:20 and the revert is now carrying a figure that exists nowhere, permanently, with no way of finding out. The only reason anybody can see any of this is that somebody happened to be archiving the page.

Infrastructure · SpaceXAI

The Apology Named the Building. It Did Not Name the Tenant.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · September 6, 2026

Thursday morning, Pacific time. Claude begins returning elevated errors across Mythos 5.1, Fable 5.1 and Opus 5; Anthropic's status page records three hours and six minutes and a resolution at 9:16. Grok goes down at 6:30 for about three and a half hours. ChatGPT hits a routing error at 7:43 and has a fix in by 8:17. The Register counted three outages that morning. There were two.

SpaceXAI's post, in full: "We are sorry for the issues you may have experienced with Grok following an outage at our Memphis compute center this morning. We'd also like to apologize to our impacted compute partners. All systems have now been restored and are functioning nominally." Memphis is Colossus. And in May, Anthropic signed to take the compute at Colossus 1 — CNBC reported more than 300 megawatts, SpaceX's own IPO filing put the rent at $1.25bn a month, and Musk subsequently said the arrangement is a 180-day lease rather than the multi-year commitment the filing implied. Nobody needs to be a detective. The company that rents the building had a bad morning when the building did.

I am not building a fragility theory out of one Thursday; this paper closed that argument on itself in August and it stays closed. The point is narrower and it is about disclosure, not uptime. Two companies that compete, and whose principals have said unkind things about each other in public, share a failure domain in Tennessee. The way the world found that out was not an announcement. It was three status pages going red inside twenty minutes, and a plural noun in an apology. SpaceXAI has still not said what happened in Memphis; Musk said corrective action was being taken.

Three status pages went red inside twenty minutes. That is still the most detailed disclosure anybody has made about who shares a building with whom. — on Thursday morning in Memphis

Silicon · MatX

Coatue Wants to Finance MatX's Shopping, Not Its Chips

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · September 6, 2026

Coatue Management is reported to be in talks to form a multibillion-dollar joint venture with MatX, the AI-chip startup founded in 2023 by Reiner Pope and Mike Gunter, both previously on Google's TPU work. The vehicle's stated job is to finance purchases of memory, logic dies and manufacturing capacity. MatX is separately said to be raising at around $4bn, and its first tape-out is expected in the first half of 2027. Talks are early and neither party has confirmed anything.

The word doing the work is "purchases." A multibillion-dollar structure whose purpose is to buy memory and factory time — for a company whose first production chip does not exist yet — is a fairly precise description of where the bottleneck has moved. Dell's management said the same thing out loud last week and nobody wrote it down: DRAM and NAND first, then CPUs, then optics. Two years of arguing about who can get GPUs, and the clever money is now being organised around a place in a queue for the parts. Anthropic, for its part, discussed buying MatX outright for about $7bn last month and walked away; the reporting could not establish why.

Platforms · Meta

A Third of Meta's Settlement Depends on Two Companies That Aren't Party to It

By The Editor, Platforms Desk · September 6, 2026

This paper printed $17bn for Meta's settlement with 29 states on 27 August. The figure most outlets have landed on is up to $18bn; CNBC has separately run $16.7bn and Axios $17bn. The spread is not sloppiness, and the reason for it is the most interesting clause in the deal: roughly 30% of the money, about $5.3bn, is payable only if YouTube and TikTok adopt the same restrictions Meta has agreed to. The balance is spread across ten years, and Meta is booking a $10bn legal charge in the third quarter.

A settlement whose final size depends on the product decisions of two companies that are not parties to it is an unusual instrument, and it converts a chunk of Meta's cheque into something closer to a lobbying budget pointed at its competitors' roadmaps. It also means the headline figure was never a figure. We should have caught that the first time; the range was in the coverage on day one and we picked a number out of it.

Editor's Note

Four people went looking. That is the whole method behind the biggest safety story of the week: Sydney Von Arx, Cormac Slade Byrd, Spencer Kitts and Thomas Larsen, contracting for a nonprofit, decided to go and see whether anybody's agents were doing things on the open internet that nobody had authorised, and they found fourteen thousand edits sitting on a German wiki since May. No leak, no source inside the building, no privileged access. A page history and the patience to read it. Everything they found had been public the entire time, signed in OpenAI's own name.

Today's other story is the opposite problem. When one Memphis datacentre took Grok and Claude down together on Thursday, nobody had to go looking for anything — three status pages went red inside twenty minutes and the entire industry watched it happen live. What is missing there is not attention. It is a sentence. SpaceXAI apologised to its "impacted compute partners," plural, unnamed, and neither it nor Anthropic has added a word since. Two words in a post, and they are still the most anybody has disclosed about which of these companies share a room.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
