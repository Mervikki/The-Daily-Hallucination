Safety · Anthropic

Anthropic Says the System Worked. Google Indexed the Wallet Keys Anyway.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · July 28, 2026

Over the weekend of July 25–26, hundreds of “shared” Claude conversations and Artifacts turned up in Google's search index, findable by anyone who typed site:claude.ai/share — a trick surfaced, as these things are, by a Reddit thread. The exposed pages reportedly lacked noindex tags and included the full range of what people tell a chatbot in confidence: legal questions, proprietary engineering work, and in some cases names, addresses, and crypto wallet keys. By Sunday morning most of the results had quietly dropped out of Google. The links that had already been shared stayed live to anyone holding them.

Anthropic's response was that nothing had gone wrong: “We give people control over sharing their Claude conversations publicly,” the company said, and “we do not share chat directories or sitemaps with search engines” — the links “are not guessable or discoverable unless people choose to share them themselves.” All technically true, and all beside the point. A public share link is a public web page; Google indexes public web pages; that is the entire arrangement of the internet. The failure lives in the gap between what a user thinks “share” means — send this to one person — and what it does: publish to the open, crawlable web.

The Weekend a “Share” Button Met Google's Crawler

The window in which the pages were crawlable closed in roughly a day; the pages that were already opened did not close with it. “Hundreds” is the reporters' count, not an audited figure.

There is a shape to this failure worth naming, and it is the opposite of the one this masthead is named for. Nobody was lied to. Claude did not invent anyone's wallet keys — it remembered them, exactly, and a button labelled “share” did the rest. The costly failure this weekend was not fabrication; it was fidelity, pointed at an audience the user never pictured. The industry has spent two years building elaborate machinery to measure how often a model makes things up. It has spent rather less on what happens when the model gets everything right and the plumbing publishes it.

Nobody hallucinated. The model remembered everything accurately — and a button that said “share” handed it to Google.

Notice the reflex in the defense: you were in control, so this is yours to own. It is the move of the decade — relocate the failure onto the user's understanding of a word the company got to define. Fair enough that a click happened; you did press share. Hollow enough that no one who pressed it understood the word meant “submit to the index.” A company whose entire brand is anticipating failure modes shipped a share flow whose failure mode was a stranger's legal question becoming a search result. “Working as intended” describes the button. It does not describe the weekend.

Infrastructure · Nvidia

Nvidia May Guarantee $250 Billion So OpenAI Can Buy More Nvidia.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · July 28, 2026

According to a weekend report, Nvidia is in talks to provide a roughly $250 billion financial backstop — a guarantee leaning on its own credit — so OpenAI can raise the lease and construction debt for a 10-gigawatt data center in Piketon, Ohio, on a former uranium-enrichment site being developed by SoftBank's SB Energy. The campus could cost at least $500 billion to build. The backstop covers the buildings, not the silicon inside them; the chips are a separate conversation, reportedly worth up to another ~$350 billion in financing. Nothing is signed.

Say the structure out loud and it stops sounding like infrastructure and starts sounding like a circle. Nvidia lends its balance sheet to guarantee the debt that builds the datacenter that exists to run Nvidia chips that OpenAI buys with money it can raise because Nvidia guaranteed the debt. This is vendor financing at a scale that stops being a metaphor: when the biggest customer's ability to pay is being propped up by the supplier, “demand” and “subsidy” are the same line item read from two ends. The number is enormous and the deal is unsigned. Both facts deserve equal weight.

Open Models · Kimi K3

K3's Weights Are Live — and Its Hallucination Rate Beats the Flagship's.

By The Editor, Open Models Desk · July 28, 2026

Moonshot's Kimi K3 shipped its open weights on schedule July 27 — the 2.8-trillion-parameter file we spent the weekend describing, now confirmed live on Hugging Face, with a newly disclosed architecture built on Kimi Delta Attention across most of its layers and a native vision encoder. It is, on the record, the largest open-weight model yet released. Demand reportedly forced Moonshot to suspend new API subscriptions again.

Yesterday we made K3's ~51% hallucination rate the villain of the story — the number left off the slide. It deserves a second look, because the number cuts a way we didn't have on Sunday. On Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience test, that 51% sits below Claude Fable 5's 54.9%: on the same measure, Anthropic's flagship invents more often than the open challenger it beats elsewhere. K3 simply attempts more questions — getting more right and more wrong. Half is a grim number. It is also, this week, the flagship's number too.

The Number, Read Both Ways

Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience figures. The open challenger hallucinates slightly less than the flagship it trails on raw intelligence — the reading yesterday's issue only had one half of.

Policy · White House

Washington's Model Review Lands This Week, With the NSA Grading Cyber.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · July 28, 2026

The voluntary framework mandated by the June 2 executive order is due to be formalized by Aug. 1, as its 60-day clock runs out. The reported contours have sharpened: up to 30 days of pre-release federal access to “covered frontier models,” with CAISI at Commerce and the NSA running classified cyber-capability benchmarks, and labs and government jointly picking which “trusted partners” also get an early look.

The NSA line is the part worth pausing on. This is not a review about whether a chatbot says something rude — it is a classified measurement of how good a model is at breaking into things, run behind a bar the public will never see the far side of. “Voluntary” is doing its usual work: the mandatory version is drafted and waiting. What's new is who holds the red pen, and that it's cleared.

Editor's Note

We run under a name that assumes the dangerous failure is the invented one — the confident wrong answer, delivered with a straight face. This weekend argued the other side. Anthropic's model didn't fabricate anyone's crypto keys; it recalled them exactly, and a button marked “share” fed them to Google's crawler. Nobody was lied to. Everybody was published. The company's defense — that this worked as intended — is the tell: the intent was fine, and the intent is not what happened to people.

The same reversal showed up one column over. Yesterday we made K3's 51% hallucination rate the number Moonshot hid; on the same test, the flagship it's chasing scores worse — a digit we owe you now that we have it. Two days, one uncomfortable lesson for a paper called this: the failure that costs you isn't always the machine getting it wrong. This weekend it was the machine getting it exactly right, and a button that said share. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
