Science · Anthropic

Claude Found Something in Phage DNA. Nobody Knows What It Does.

By The Editor, Science Desk · September 23, 2026

Anthropic said this morning that Claude, running as roughly 950 agents for 21 hours across 210 million tokens, worked through a DNA sequence database looking for unusual reverse transcriptases — the enzymes that copy RNA back into DNA. It narrowed more than 200,000 sequences to 3,500 candidates and then to twenty, and one agent noticed that beside one of those genes sat an array of evenly spaced DNA repeats: three to twenty-one copies of a short sequence, with none of the cas genes that flank a CRISPR array. The company calls the system array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ART — an enzyme, a partner gene, and the array. Human involvement, it says, was the opening prompt and the laboratory work. Feng Zhang, of MIT and the Broad Institute, one of the people who made CRISPR work, read the preprint.

Take the finding at its real size, which is smaller than the headlines and still substantial. The reverse transcriptase itself was already known. What appears to be new is the array beside it and the partner protein — the recognition that three things sitting together constitute one system. And what does that system do? Anthropic does not know, and says so in the announcement, which is the rarest sentence any laboratory publishes about its own work.

Then the arithmetic. 210 million tokens at Opus 5.5's published rates comes to somewhere between eight hundred and four thousand dollars depending on the mix, and the company has not said which model it used. Six days ago it published an application form for life-science teams who want its biology classifiers loosened: research credentials, security standards, ethical research oversight, six-monthly renewal on the high-risk tier, and for its most capable model a small set of entities gated by a federal agency it declines to name. The best available evidence that those safeguards cost real science has now been produced by the one laboratory that never had to fill the form in.

The identification of RNA-repeat arrays associated with reverse transcriptases is genuinely intriguing and merits further investigation. — Feng Zhang, on a preprint whose central finding is that nobody yet knows what the thing is

What 950 agents and 21 hours produced

Every square is one agent. Anthropic's figures: 950 agents, 21 hours, 210 million tokens, 200,000 sequences screened, 3,500 candidates, twenty finalists, one array.

Which agent found it is not recorded, and the rust square's position here is arbitrary. The dollar cost above is our arithmetic, not the company's.

Safety · Anthropic

The Evaluators Were Named All Along. We Said They Weren't.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 23, 2026

This paper wrote yesterday that Anthropic had not named an evaluator for Claude Opus 5.5, published no finding and given no date. That was wrong in every part. The announcement page names Frontier Design and METR, and a system card dated 22 September carries their input, engaged under the company's Frontier Compliance Framework. METR's finding is that Opus 5.5 is an incremental improvement on Fable 5.1 and is unlikely to be able to fully automate AI research and development. Anthropic's own CoBench 2.1 puts the model at 55.8% against an 85% threshold for substituting for research staff. The documents were reachable this morning from the same desk that could not open them yesterday, which is a description of our sandbox and not of anybody's transparency.

The card is more interesting than its absence was. Opus 5.5 assisted with dual-use security work at the highest rate of any model in the evaluation, and refused outright malicious requests at the lowest rate of any model in it. Both numbers point the same way: this model is more willing. The card also contains this sentence: when safeguards intervened during benchmarking, “cybersecurity tasks were completed by Claude Opus 4.8, and biology and frontier LLM development tasks were completed by Claude Opus 5.” We cannot tell from it whether the substitute models were administering the tests or finishing the work, and it is the one line in the document that changes what a published score means.

Open Source · Alphabet

Google Gave Away the Robot Stack and Kept the Brain

By The Editor, Technology Desk · September 23, 2026

At ROSCon in Toronto, Intrinsic — the robotics software company Alphabet folded into Google in February — released Intrinsic Core under Apache 2.0: a preconfigured, ROS-compatible environment that runs on local hardware, with real-time control that adjusts a robot's path mid-move from sensor feedback, pose estimation built on Nvidia's FoundationPose, motion and grasp planning, simulation, calibration and drivers. Intrinsic says these are the capabilities it uses in real deployments rather than a demonstration build. A reference design for CNC machine tending on Universal Robots and FANUC hardware shipped alongside it, aimed at a market in which, by Intrinsic's own figure, 8% of American and European shops use any automation at all.

Forbes called it the Android of robotics, and the company will not mind, though it is worth remembering what that comparison actually describes: a permissively licensed base everybody adopted, on top of which the licensor kept the services that mattered. This is a genuinely useful release — control, planning and calibration are the unglamorous parts nobody open-sources, and the 8% figure is the real argument for giving them away. It is also the layer that makes a robot move, not the layer that decides where. Google has given away the spine. The model stays upstairs.

Policy · United Nations

They Built It, Then Asked the Council to Slow Them Down

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 23, 2026

The Security Council held its first high-level briefing devoted to frontier AI safety this afternoon, convened by France, which holds the September presidency, and chaired by its foreign minister, Jean-Noël Barrot. Four people briefed the fifteen members: Yoshua Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI; Sam Altman, who appeared in person; and Dario Amodei and Hugging Face's Clément Delangue, both by video. Altman told the Council that “this moment calls for extreme care”. Amodei pledged to slow down where needed. António Guterres said voluntary efforts will not be enough if they are isolated, unverifiable or unevenly applied. The day before, in the General Assembly hall upstairs, the president of the host country rejected “any attempt to construct a globalist scheme of control” over what he announced would henceforth be officially called Super Intelligence.

Amodei's proposal is the substantial thing that happened in that room, and it deserves to be taken at full strength. Three parts: embedded external evaluators with employee-like access; a narrow, government-mediated antitrust waiver so competitors may hold safety conversations without prosecution; and a global speed limit on recursive self-improvement, modelled explicitly on the SALT treaties, which capped Cold War missile counts without eliminating a single warhead. It is the first proposal out of this industry with a historical precedent attached, and the precedent is a good one. SALT held.

SALT held because of what came with it. Each side agreed to a number, each side could count the other's launchers from orbit, and the treaty said so in terms: national technical means of verification. There is no orbit from which you can count a training run. The thing to be limited here is a rate of capability improvement inside a private data centre, measured by nobody named, against a definition nobody has published. Amodei has taken the request to the one body that could build that machinery, which is the correct address. What he cannot ask it for is the satellite.

Voluntary efforts will not be sufficient if they are isolated, unverifiable or unevenly applied. — António Guterres, describing the entire existing safety regime, with most of its authors in the room or on its screens
Policy · China

Beijing Is Investigating the Two Labs It Sent to the Council

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 23, 2026

DeepSeek and Moonshot were both invited to make statements to the Security Council. Both are also, as of this week, under investigation by the Cyberspace Administration of China over data security: officials have visited both firms and questioned executives and staff, and no determination on penalties has been made. The trigger is a 154-page report published by Anthropic, which says Moonshot sent it more than 23 million user inputs between May and July, and DeepSeek 12.1 million in a fortnight in July — routing prompts to Claude and training on the answers. The prompts, Anthropic says, carried names, email addresses and company data in at least a dozen languages. In one case described, a user affiliated with the People's Liberation Army asked Moonshot's Kimi to examine surveillance footage from police cameras in Chengdu, and Moonshot passed the request and the footage to Claude.

Notice how this was found out. Not by an auditor, a regulator or a treaty. Anthropic read its own inbound traffic, recognised what was in it, and published. Whatever else that is, it is the only verification event in this industry this week that actually caught somebody, and it worked because the companies being caught were customers. Beijing's stated complaint is data security, and it is a reasonable one. Read from a different chair, it is also a complaint that its two national champions were buying from the Americans.

Business · Models

OpenAI Halved Its Prices Before Anthropic's Cut Had Cleared

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 23, 2026

GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna arrived yesterday at $2 and $10 per million input and output tokens for Sol, and ten cents and fifty cents for Luna — roughly half the previous generation's rates, and in Luna's case rather more than half. Both sit below GPT-6 Astra; OpenAI says Sol reaches “Astra-level reliability” at a fraction of Astra's cost, and puts Sol at 68.8% on DeepSWE v1.1, 1.1 points behind Claude Fable 5, at around eighty per cent less per task. Yesterday this paper led on Anthropic cutting Opus from $5 and $25 to $4 and $20, and called that cut unusually large. It was, until the afternoon.

Flagship output price, before this week and now

Dollars per million output tokens. Two flagship repricings landed inside about a day of each other, in opposite corners of the industry.

Anthropic's figures come from its published pricing page. OpenAI's reach us as trade outlets report them; openai.com was unreachable from this desk.

What is being competed away is the price of the frontier, which until this month was the industry's one dependable moat. Two flagship repricings inside a day is not a coincidence and it is not generosity; it is what happens when two firms work out simultaneously that their customers' switching cost is a config file. The number to watch is Luna's. Fifty cents per million output tokens is not a discount on the frontier. It is a bet that most of what people actually do with these models was never frontier work in the first place.

Editor's Note

The rarest sentence any laboratory publishes about its own work turned up this morning, and it was about an enzyme. Anthropic said Claude had found a system in the DNA of bacteriophages — an unusual reverse transcriptase, a partner gene, an array of repeats that looks something like CRISPR — and then said it does not know what the system does. Feng Zhang, who helped make CRISPR work, read the preprint and said it merits further investigation. That is what careful looks like, and it was worth the 210 million tokens.

Now hold it against the other two things that happened today. At the Security Council, Dario Amodei asked for a speed limit on recursive self-improvement modelled on SALT — the best-argued proposal this industry has produced, and one that worked in its original form because each side could count the other's missiles from orbit. And in Beijing, regulators began walking into DeepSeek's and Moonshot's offices, because Anthropic had read its own inbound API traffic and published a hundred and fifty-four pages about what was in it. One of those is a request for verification machinery that does not exist. The other is verification that already happened, by accident, because the firms being inspected were paying customers. The only inspector currently working in this industry sends an invoice at the end of the month.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
