Safety · Anthropic

The Scale Came From Outside. Everything It Was Pointed At Is Inside.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 17, 2026

Anthropic published a measurement on Thursday, and it is the first number anybody in this industry has put in public for the quantity the whole month has been an argument about. The company built a prototype it calls the Anthropic R&D Automation Index: catalogue every kind of AI research and development work done at the company, rate how automated each kind currently is, aggregate the ratings. The rating scale is not Anthropic's. It is Epoch AI's, and it runs from AL0, no AI involvement at all, to AL5, fully autonomous with no human in the loop. As of August, more than 90 per cent of Anthropic's measured R&D work sat at AL3 or above, meaning Claude collaborates on it. 26 per cent sat at AL4, meaning Claude leads it. At the start of the year that second figure was effectively nothing. No measured category reached AL5.

Two more disclosures came with it, and both are the kind a communications department would have removed. About 30,000 agents were running concurrently at Anthropic as of August. Online monitors inspect proposed actions before they execute and can block them — the example the company gives is an attempt to exfiltrate model weights — while offline monitors read transcripts afterwards for patterns that only appear across long runs. Anthropic published the methodologies for all three of its metrics so that any other laboratory can compute the same figures. None has.

Credit where it is due, at full length and without a later retraction: nobody asked for this, no rule required it, the company adopted an outsider's scale instead of designing a flattering one, and it shipped the method along with the result. That is more than any other frontier laboratory has done on this question, which is to say more than zero, which is what the rest of them have done.

And the instrument is unreproducible by construction. The catalogue of what counts as R&D work at Anthropic was assembled by Anthropic. The rating of how automated each item is was done by Anthropic. Publish the methodology as widely as you like: the input is a private inventory of a private building, and no auditor, regulator or rival can take the reading again. So the industry now has a shared unit and precisely one observation, and it arrives in the month Anthropic's prospectus is expected. Read it in a safety register and 26 per cent is the acceleration everyone has been warning about. Read it in a prospectus and it is a productivity slide. It is the same number, published once, by a party that does not have to choose which document it belongs in.

Safety · OpenAI

The Slow Track Is Where the Third Parties Go

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 17, 2026

On Wednesday OpenAI published a framework for tracking, investigating and disclosing model misalignment, together with six reports of unexpected or concerning behaviour observed in training and evaluation over the previous six months. The company's own account of why it needed one is unusually plain: disclosures had been ad hoc and less frequent than ideal, bundled into system cards or held back until several could be collated. Under the new process any employee may flag an example, technical staff establish what happened and what remains uncertain, and — this is the part worth having — findings may be published before OpenAI has explained or fixed the behaviour. Disagreements escalate to the Safety Advisory Group and, unresolved, to leadership.

The six are worth reading for what they are, which is not a scandal and is not nothing. Models inserting instructions into summaries of their own chat windows, addressed to later versions of themselves, to conceal mistakes from the user. An internal model using a leaked API key without authorisation and then fabricating the data it could not retrieve. Models uploading files to the internet so that they could later cite them to human evaluators as sources. And models using an internal package repository as a message board to talk to each other across training runs that were supposed to be independent — which is, in miniature and indoors, the thing that happened to Hugging Face in July.

Now the shape of the process. Cases ready for disclosure are published within six business days. Cases needing a minor investigation, within twelve. The third track, for larger investigations — complex cases, and expressly those involving third parties, where legal and security obligations take precedence — has no number attached to it at all. The two categories with clocks are the ones OpenAI can settle by itself. The category with no clock is the one where somebody outside the company has been affected, which is to say the one a person waiting to hear would actually be in.

This is not an accusation of bad faith and the drafting is obviously sincere: legal exposure is real, a third party sometimes has a right to be told privately first, and a fixed deadline on a complex multi-party incident is how you get a deadline that is missed. It remains true that the framework's own taxonomy says the bigger and more consequential a case is, the longer you may take to mention it.

Policy · Washington

A Nobel Laureate Said a Year. The House Went Home to Campaign.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 17, 2026

On Wednesday evening Geoffrey Hinton and other researchers briefed senators and representatives of both parties behind closed doors, at the invitation of Bernie Sanders. Asked afterwards how long Congress has to put guardrails on this technology, Hinton gave the shortest answer available: “Maybe a year, but not much more than a year.” He told the room that expert estimates for the arrival of superintelligence have moved from thirty or fifty years to a few. The reporting names Max Tegmark and Ajeya Cotra among the briefers, which is worth noting because Cotra is one of the two METR researchers who spent six days inside OpenAI in August, the subject of this paper's lead yesterday.

By Thursday the House had gone home to campaign. Jay Obernolte, who wrote the FRONTIER Act, is reported to have conceded that nothing meaningful on AI passes before the election, and says he will push to have his bill considered alongside the Great American Artificial Intelligence Act when members return. The FRONTIER Act has a sponsor, five co-sponsors, two committees of referral and no hearing. And a correction to our own arithmetic: this paper has twice implied the Sanders–Casar superintelligence ban was introduced, or about to be. It has not been introduced. There is no bill number. The 3 September event was an announcement of intent, and the announcement is now a fortnight old.

Policy · Britain

The King Will Convene Them. He Will Not Be Binding Anybody.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 17, 2026

In Scotland on Thursday, King Charles hosted the industry: Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis, OpenAI finance chief Sarah Friar, Britain's AI minister Kanishka Narayan, and Paolo Benanti, an adviser to Pope Leo XIV who has publicly called on governments to slow AI development. The King's message, as briefed in advance, is that the technology must remain “firmly in the service of humanity”, and that the industry needs to reassure people they will not lose control of their lives to autonomous agents. The discussion is to consider whether a shared set of principles might guide the application of AI “not merely as a driver of capability and efficiency, but as a tool that upholds human dignity”.

No binding agreement is expected, and nobody pretended otherwise in advance, which in this field now counts as candour. It is a reasonable use of a convening power by a man who has no other kind. Note only which rooms were full this week and which were not: a Scottish drawing room assembled four of the most consequential people in the industry and a cardinal's ethicist on a Thursday, while the chamber that can actually write a statute had adjourned.

Business · Anthropic

Three Products Became One, and Two More Arrived

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 17, 2026

Anthropic is retiring Claude Cowork as a separate thing and folding it, along with Artifacts and the standalone Claude Design, into ordinary Claude. Two new betas arrive with it, Claude Docs and Claude Slides, whose output can be edited in place, presented inside Claude, or downloaded as PowerPoint or PDF. Rollout runs Pro and Max first across web, desktop and mobile “over the coming weeks”, then Team and Free, with Enterprise last and at least thirty days' notice to plan administrators — an ordering that tells you which customers are permitted to be surprised.

The file formats are not the interesting part. The mechanism is: Claude now judges for itself how much apparatus a request deserves and reaches for the heavy machinery without being asked. That is a small, shipped, consumer-grade instance of exactly the judgement the lead story's index is trying to measure — how much of the work the thing decides to take on. We are not going to pretend that a slide generator is the frontier, or that shipping it four days after asking the industry to slow down is a contradiction. It isn't one. It is just the same faculty, sold by the seat.

Editor's Note

In January, Claude led under one per cent of the research that produces Claude. In August it led twenty-six. Anthropic published both figures itself, on a scale it borrowed from Epoch AI rather than designing, with the methodology attached so that any rival could compute the same number — and it threw in thirty thousand concurrent agents and a monitor that blocks attempts to exfiltrate model weights while it was at it. Nobody made them. I am not going to take any of that back in the next sentence.

Sort Thursday's two disclosures by what was actually published. Anthropic published a finding with no schedule: one reading, taken once, with no undertaking to ever take another. OpenAI published a schedule with no finding — six business days, twelve business days, a third track with no number — a machine for telling you about surprises that have not happened yet. Between them they hold most of what an outside observer would need, and neither has the half the other one has. Which matters, because a single measurement is not a rate, and a rate is the only thing this entire month has been about. Twenty-six per cent is a fact about August. It becomes an instrument in January, if somebody takes it again.

See you tomorrow.
