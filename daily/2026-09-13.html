Safety · Anthropic

Three Rivals Agreed Before Sunday, and Not One of Them Named a Speed

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 13, 2026

On Saturday Dario Amodei published We Must Pace the Frontier — around 3,800 words, on his own website rather than his company’s, opening on the proposition that the industry must slow the rate at which it improves model capabilities. The plan has three steps: embed independent evaluators inside the frontier labs; get the labs in democratic countries to agree capability-based checkpoints, backed by federal regulation; then negotiate graded agreements with authoritarian states, rising to a limit on the rate of progress itself. Anthropic is committing to the first step unilaterally, and the terms are the most generous any lab has put in writing: permanent, employee-like access for an outside team such as METR — desks, badges, laptops, permissions comparable to the internal risk teams — covering alignment during training rather than finished models, with the right to publish findings free of Anthropic’s editorial control. The redaction power is narrow, cannot be used because a finding is unfavourable, and reviewers may say publicly when a redaction removed something that mattered to their conclusions. Nine days ago the same organisation had eight weeks and an intention.

By evening Sam Altman had agreed — “I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier” — said the question had been under discussion inside OpenAI for weeks, and committed his own company to the same evaluator access, with more to share soon. Elon Musk contributed three words: “Dario is right.”

Now the part worth sitting with, which is the clock. This industry has spent two years unable to agree on the definition of a safe release, and on Saturday three companies that agree on nothing, including one pair who have spent years suing each other, converged inside an afternoon. What they converged on is step one, and step one is not about pace. It is about who is allowed to watch. The pacing lives in steps two and three: the checkpoint regime that needs your competitors, and the international agreement that needs the People’s Republic of China. Amodei said so himself on Sunday, on CBS, calling China the “toughest dilemma” and adding that a joint speed limit will be “very difficult because the incentives to pull ahead and the military advantage that you get from that are so large.”

The evaluator pledge is not cheap and I am not going to pretend it is. The clause that costs is the one nobody was obliged to write: an outside team that can publish, and that cannot be redacted for being unflattering, is a standing licence to embarrass your employer on the record. No frontier lab has ever handed that out. But the essay is titled after a verb about rate, and the thresholds in it are specified as a form — if capability X, then certifications Y and Z — rather than as values. Nobody named X. Nobody named a current rate, a target rate, or a date. What got unanimous assent in an afternoon was the item denominated in access, not in speed.

Three companies that agree on nothing else agreed on this inside an afternoon. That is a fact about the item, not about the three companies. — on what a fast consensus measures

Which of the three steps anybody has actually taken

Six of the nine cells were never going to be filled this weekend, and drawing them empty is the honest way to show a three-part plan of which one part happened. The hollow square is Mr Musk, who agreed with the essay and undertook nothing. The whole of the news sits in the left-hand column.

Business · OpenAI

Altman Put the Listing Off and Named Safety as the Reason

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 13, 2026

In an interview published by Fortune on Saturday, Sam Altman ruled out an OpenAI listing this year. “I actually think that, given everything happening with safety, right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public, and we don’t feel pressure on that,” he said; asked directly about the year, he gave the flattest answer available — “not 2026” — which pushes the most anticipated flotation in the history of the sector into 2027 at the earliest. In the same conversation he described a ten per cent chance of AI causing human extinction by the end of the decade as “unacceptable”.

Take that at face value for a moment, because it deserves it: a chief executive declining to sell shares in his own company, on the record, because of the state of the safety work, is not a sentence that gets drafted by a bank. It is also the second lab this month to publish an act of self-restraint in a week when nobody asked for one.

And then the calendar. The company reportedly weeks away from putting a prospectus in front of the public is Anthropic, whose confidential draft has been sitting with the regulator since June and whose filing has been expected this month, with a listing aimed at the days before the midterms. So of this weekend’s two firms, the one that deferred a commercial event on safety grounds is the one that did not write the essay about slowing down, and the one that wrote the essay is the one still walking towards the exchange. None of that is hypocrisy — a share sale has nothing to do with a release schedule, Anthropic’s timetable is reported rather than confirmed, and a company that raised at several hundred billion dollars in March can afford to wave a listing away in a manner that a company burning more than it earns cannot. It is simply worth noticing which of the two was inconvenienced.

Models · Open Weights

Sakana’s New Flagship Is Not a Model, and That Is the Claim

By The Editor, Models Desk · September 13, 2026

Sakana AI shipped Fugu Max v1.0 and Fugu Ultra v2.0 this week. Neither is a foundation model. Both are routers — language models trained to dispatch a task across a fixed pool of open-weight and specialist systems, and to call instances of themselves recursively, an approach the company traces to two ICLR papers of its own. Fugu Max lists at $2 and $6 per million input and output tokens; Ultra v2 at $5 and $30, with a premium band above 272K of context. Sakana claims best or joint-best on five of eight benchmarks for Ultra and says Max widens the cost-performance frontier on seven of ten.

The number the company wants quoted is 48.3 on Chartography, a visual-reasoning test, against 27.3 for Claude Opus 5 and 29.5 for Fable 5 — and, it says, neither Anthropic model is in the pool Fugu is allowed to call. Every one of those figures is Sakana’s own, measured by Sakana, on a leaderboard nobody outside has replicated, which is where this paper leaves it. The interesting part is not the score but the shape of the product: a company arguing that the frontier is now a dispatch problem, and that the way to beat a large model is to own the switchboard rather than the models. As for the benchmark being the one about reading charts, a newspaper that draws its own will be declining to comment further.

The Quiet Correction

The Promotion That Was the Product

By The Editor · September 13, 2026

Claude Code weekly limits · 13 May – 13 September 2026

On 29 August this paper did an arithmetic that nobody had published and then filed a note against itself warning that the arithmetic might be wrong. Today is the day it stops being arguable. The temporary 50 per cent uplift to Claude Code’s weekly limits, which has been in force since 13 May and has been extended four times by our count, runs out tonight. Tomorrow a permanent increase of 25 per cent takes its place.

Two true numbers and the line neither of them touches

Both readings of this chart are true. 125 really is a quarter more than 100, and it really is a sixth less than what a subscriber holds this morning. The dashed line is the one the announcement is written against; the rust line is the one anybody has lived on for the last four months.

What resolved itself quietly is not the arithmetic, which several outlets have since run. It is the word temporary. A measure introduced on 13 May and extended through July, then through August, then through today has been in force for 123 days — a third of a year, and longer than most of the model versions it was used to run. At that duration the promotion is not an interruption to the standard. It is the standard, and what arrives tomorrow is a reduction from it. I am not claiming vindication, because being right and nobody having got round to disagreeing look identical from this desk. I am noting that the increase was posted to a company account with a date on it, and the decrease was never stated by anybody, because from where the announcement is standing there is not one.

Editor’s Note

The most consequential safety document this industry has produced in a year was published on a personal website. Not anthropic.com — darioamodei.com, a man’s own domain, under his own name. It was agreed to on a social network, by a chief executive posting as himself, and seconded by a third man in three words. In the same forty-eight hours the entities that would have to honour any of it — the corporations, the ones with counsel and disclosure obligations and a registrar — published nothing at all.

I don’t think that is sinister. It is how this industry has always worked, and a personal essay is how you say something before your lawyers have made it smaller. But it does tell you what kind of instrument we have been handed. A commitment written by a person and posted to his own site binds exactly as long as that person is in the building, and this paper spent Friday reporting three researchers leaving two laboratories in three days. The badges and the laptops are real and generous. The signature on them is a man’s, not a company’s, and men leave. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
