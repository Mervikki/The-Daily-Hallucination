Open Weights · Z.ai

Z.ai Is Holding the Weights Back for Safety. It Put the Model on Sale on Friday.

By The Editor, Open Weights Desk · August 16, 2026

Z.ai released GLM-5.3 on Friday. It runs on the same base as GLM-5.2 — around 744 billion parameters, mixture-of-experts — and the company says every gain came from extended post-training rather than a fresh pre-train, which is itself the interesting engineering claim. The coding results are what you would expect from a lab in this position: roughly 50% better than GLM-5.2 on Z.ai's own internal coding-agent benchmark, and the strongest open-weights system the company says it has measured. The unusual sentence is further down the announcement. Z.ai says the model's offensive-security capability grew faster than the training was designed to produce, with the largest gains at the exploitation end of the chain, and that it is therefore holding the weights back roughly two weeks for safety evaluation and hardening. It is the first time the company has delayed a release on cybersecurity grounds.

The numbers it published to support that are its own. On CyberGym, Z.ai puts GLM-5.3 at 84.5%, up from GLM-5.2's 77.2%. On ExploitBench it reports 54.4% against 24.4% — a capability that more than doubled while the base model stayed still. Z.ai also published where it believes the competition sits on the same test: Anthropic's Claude Mythos 5 at 83.8%, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol at 83.6%. Separately it says its models have found 2,436 vulnerabilities across 269 open-source projects since GLM-5.2, 1,097 of them rated critical or high severity.

Where GLM-5.3's Gains Actually Landed

Every dot here is Z.ai's own and no independent evaluation is possible until the weights ship. Z.ai puts Mythos 5 at 83.8 and Sol at 83.6 on CyberGym — a claimed lead of less than a point, narrower than the rust dot is wide.

Take the delay at its best first, because the best case is a good one. Weights are not an endpoint. Once a file is on Hugging Face it cannot be rate-limited, revoked, logged or refused, and whatever safety behaviour is trained into it can be fine-tuned back out by anyone with a rack and a weekend. An API is a control surface; a download is not. A lab that finds a capability it did not set out to build, says so in public, and takes a fortnight before making it permanently ownable is behaving better than a lab that says nothing. Anthropic held Mythos Preview back in April over the same class of capability and simply never shipped it. Z.ai did not have to publish the CyberGym line at all, and nobody would have known.

Now notice what is not being held. GLM-5.3 has been live on Z.ai's API since Friday and was pushed to existing GLM Coding Plan subscribers on release. The capability the company describes as having outgrown its own training plan is commercially available this morning, to anyone with a card, wherever Z.ai sells. What waits two weeks is the copy you get to keep. That may genuinely be the right order of operations — the download is the irreversible act and the endpoint is not — but it does mean the safety evaluation is running after general availability rather than before it, and "we are hardening the weights" turns out to be a sentence about distribution rather than about the model.

The safety hold and the verification hold are the same hold.

Which leaves an awkward fortnight. The number that makes GLM-5.3 alarming is the one nobody outside Z.ai can check, and it is unverifiable for exactly the reason it is alarming: the file is not out. For the next two weeks the only account of how good this model is at breaking software belongs to the company that is currently renting it to you by the token. That is not an accusation — it is just the shape of the thing, and it is worth saying plainly before the weights land and everyone starts quoting 84.5% as though somebody checked.

Finance · Nvidia

A Number Nobody Ever Said Out Loud Just Fell by $130 Billion

By The Editor, Finance Desk · August 16, 2026

The Wall Street Journal reported on Friday that Nvidia has scaled back the financing guarantee it is prepared to extend to OpenAI for the ten-gigawatt campus planned on Department of Energy land in Pike County, Ohio. The figure reported in late July was $250bn. The figure reported now is under $120bn, and it covers the first phase of the build rather than the whole thing. The reason, attributed to people familiar rather than to anyone at Nvidia, is investor discomfort about how much risk the company is carrying on other people's construction projects.

The Guarantee, as Reported Three Weeks Apart

Both endpoints are anonymously-sourced press reports three weeks apart. Nvidia was never proposing to spend either sum: it was proposing to guarantee financing that lenders would arrange.

That distinction is worth holding onto, because the imprecision is the story. A backstop is not a cheque. What entered the record in late July was a report of an intention to guarantee somebody else's lending, and what has replaced it is a report of a smaller intention. In between, the larger number was quoted everywhere — including in this paper — as though it were a commitment with a signature under it. Nobody now has to issue a correction, because nobody ever made a statement. The rest of Pike County travels the same way: a separate chip-purchase arrangement reported at up to $350bn, a total build cost reported above $500bn, and a deal that the same reporting says could be signed as early as this weekend, which is to say it has not been signed.

The tell is how little happened. A commitment the size of a small national economy was cut by more than half, and the coverage that bothered to look at the market mostly noted that Nvidia's shares barely moved. That is not complacency. It is accuracy. You cannot reprice what was never priced — and an industry that has spent this year financing chips against buildings against demand for the chips should probably notice how much of its own scaffolding is made of numbers that no one has ever had to stand behind.

Business · OpenAI

OpenAI's Revenue Doubled. Its Sales Chief Left. Only One of Those Has a Name on It.

By The Editor, Business Desk · August 16, 2026

Bloomberg reported this week that OpenAI's annualised revenue run rate has passed $40bn — roughly twice where it stood at the end of 2025 — with the growth attributed to coding products, consumer subscriptions and the advertising business the company switched on in February. Read the sourcing carefully and a pattern falls out. The one element anybody put their name to is a rate of change: co-founder and president Greg Brockman told staff internally that the run rate grew more than 20% month-over-month in July. The $40bn itself arrives unattributed. The company signed the derivative and let somebody else carry the total.

In the same few days, Fortune reported that chief revenue officer Denise Dresser is being replaced by Dali Rajic, previously president and chief operating officer of the security firm Wiz, less than a year into the job; OpenAI has not confirmed it. That follows chief operating officer Brad Lightcap's exit, announced days earlier after four years, and Fidji Simo stepping back in July from the applications role she was recruited into, citing health. Three senior departures in about a month, at a company whose revenue is reportedly compounding at better than 20% a month.

Fast-growing companies lose executives, and none of these three individually means anything. What is worth a paragraph is the mismatch in register. The revenue figure is a leak: precise, flattering, internal, unauditable, and surfacing in a week when a flattering internal number is useful. The departures are simply a matter of record — you cannot leak a vacancy, because the chair is either occupied or it is not. If you want to know how OpenAI is doing this morning, there is exactly one category of information about it that somebody has to stand behind, and it happens to be the category about who is leaving.

Editor's Note

Some time in the next fortnight, on a day Z.ai has not named, a file will appear on Hugging Face carrying the highest offensive-security score anyone has published. That is the safety plan: two weeks, and then permanent. I am not being sarcastic — a download cannot be rate-limited or switched off, and taking a fortnight before making a capability ownable forever is more care than most releases get, and more than most labs would have volunteered. But the model has been on sale since Friday. What is being held back is not the capability. It is the copy.

The rest of the issue is about numbers with nobody behind them. Nvidia's Ohio guarantee fell from $250bn to under $120bn between two anonymous reports, and not one correction is owed by anybody, because not one statement was ever made. OpenAI's revenue crossed $40bn on the same terms; the only named source in the story gave a percentage. So a file gets a fortnight of safety work before anybody is allowed to keep it, and a hundred and thirty billion dollars moves in an afternoon on the word of people who cannot be named. Somewhere in this industry there is a careful process. It is attached to the software. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
