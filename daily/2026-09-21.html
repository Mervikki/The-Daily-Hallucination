Safety · Anthropic

The Model That Was Too Dangerous for Biology Now Takes Applications

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 21, 2026

Anthropic has opened applications for the Life Sciences Verification Program, a beta that gives vetted life-science teams access to Claude Mythos, Opus 5 and Sonnet 5 under classifiers deliberately loosened for biology — drug discovery, research biology, clinical development and manufacturing, much of which the generally available models currently refuse. Applicants are checked on research credentials, security standards and ethical research oversight. There are two grants. Standard Use covers most life-science work, extends across a team and renews annually. High-risk Use is a project-scoped add-on for work that Standard blocks, and renews every six months. The company names three threat models it built against — account compromise, insider threat, and agents acting badly over long horizons — keeps its cyber classifiers switched on, retains flagged data for thirty days, and moves monitoring from the individual prompt to the whole session.

Take the case for it at full strength, because it is a good one. A classifier that blocks a vaccine researcher is not a safeguard, it is friction wearing a safeguard’s coat, and the people it costs are the ones working on diseases that nobody will fund. Anthropic could have widened the filter quietly, the way this industry usually adjusts a refusal rate, and instead it built a gate with named tiers, published renewal terms and a written threat model. Session-level monitoring is a genuine advance on prompt-level blocking. This is the most legible piece of access policy any laboratory has published.

Now read the tiers. High-risk grants are live today for Opus 5 and Sonnet 5. For Mythos — the model Anthropic itself treated as too capable to let loose on this domain — high-risk access stays limited to a small set of entities with extra vetting, and the company says it is working with the United States government to widen it. Which part of the government, it does not say. How many teams are enrolled, what the vetting asks, who signs, when it opens: none of that is published either. The original worry has not been withdrawn. It has been converted into an eligibility question, and on the one tier where it still bites, the company that spent Friday buying itself an evaluator did not keep the pen.

The safeguard has not been removed. It has been turned into a queue, and somebody we cannot name is standing at the front of it.

“Life science professionals can use our models — including, for the first time, Mythos — with a new set of safeguards designed to enable the full range of biology-related work.” — Anthropic, announcing the Life Sciences Verification Program

Who gets which model, and at which tier

The gap between the two tiers is the programme. The one endpoint that isn’t filled in belongs to the model the whole safety case was written about.

Safety · Evaluation

The Question We Asked Yesterday Was Answered on Friday

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 21, 2026

This paper closed its lead yesterday by writing that nobody had said whether Accenture may publish a finding Anthropic dislikes, and that until somebody did, it was the only question worth asking. Somebody had. Writing about the arrangement, Dario Amodei set out terms: “We will have the narrow ability to redact security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information, but we can’t redact findings just because they are unfavourable. The reviewers can say publicly if a redaction removed something important to their conclusions.” That is a better answer than we expected, and it was available before we printed the question.

What is still unwritten is the half that bites. Nothing published says whether the embedded team may halt a deployment, what obliges Anthropic to act on a severe finding, or whether the evaluator can walk out without a penalty. A redaction regime is a real constraint and this one is tighter than the industry norm, which is none. It governs what may be said afterwards. It says nothing about what may be stopped at the time.

Published terms on what an embedded evaluator may say

The horizontal axis is not time — it is which of the two documents you read. We have not established which came first. Counting clauses is a liberty; the point is that the widely quoted line was the left-hand one, including by us.

Research · OpenAI

OpenAI Asked for an Advisory Board. Nine Mathematicians Wrote Their Own Terms.

By The Editor, Research Desk · September 21, 2026

Announced today: the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, with nine initial members — François Charles, Camillo De Lellis, Timothy Gowers, Martin Hairer, Nikhil Srivastava, Ulrike Tillmann, Ravi Vakil, Edward Witten and Melanie Matchett Wood. It arrives after OpenAI said an internal model had resolved more than a hundred long-standing open problems across most areas of mathematics, and after the abrupt publication of a claimed solution to Navier–Stokes, one of the Millennium Prize problems. Terence Tao, who is not a member, carried the announcement on his blog.

The procedural detail is the story, and almost nobody has reported it as one. OpenAI approached some of these people about setting up an external advisory board. What exists instead is a body they constituted themselves and then invited others to join, by agreement with OpenAI, at an institution OpenAI does not own. Its remit covers advising on the review and communication of results and on the professional standards of mathematical research. It also claims the freedom to offer advice OpenAI has not requested, to comment on OpenAI’s effect on mathematics, and to publish what it thinks.

For a hundred and thirty-odd issues this desk has written a version of the same sentence about every outside body in this industry: the people were independent, the questions were not theirs. This is the first time the invitees read the terms and returned different ones. Say plainly what it is not, though. It is not a referee. A hundred claimed results have not been through a journal, and an advisory group is not a journal — mathematics has had the institution for this since the seventeenth century, and its problem was never judgement, it was latency. OpenAI is producing claims faster than refereeing can absorb them, and what has been built is a body to manage the interval. The difference from everything else in today’s paper is that the nine people managing it can say so out loud without asking.

The group claims the freedom to offer advice OpenAI has not requested, to comment on OpenAI’s impact on mathematics, and to make its advice public. — The Advisory Group’s stated remit, announced 21 September 2026
Policy · Washington

A Crisis Line for the Hoax

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 21, 2026

After talks with Vice Premier He Lifeng in New York on Sunday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the two governments had discussed a US–China AI dialogue carrying a notification mechanism — a channel for telling each other about AI incidents that rise to the level of national security. Trump hosts Xi Jinping at the White House on Thursday. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said export controls on advanced chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment are not part of the mechanism. Xinhua confirmed that AI was discussed and did not say whether Beijing had accepted anything. The two countries have done this before, for other technologies: a direct secure telephone link agreed in 1997, a military hotline since 2008.

Two days before his Treasury Secretary proposed a nuclear-vintage crisis channel for artificial intelligence, the President called fears about it a hoax. Both are now the position of the same government and nobody has been asked to reconcile them. It is worth being precise about what a hotline is: an instrument for telling the other side about something you have already failed to prevent. Building one is the correct instinct, and it concedes in procedure the argument the administration is refusing in public. Meanwhile the only AI lever either government actually pulls on the other was carried out of the room before anybody sat down.

The Glossary

Open-weight

The official definition

A model whose parameters are published, so that anyone may download, inspect, fine-tune and run it on hardware they control.

The real definition

A release schedule. StepFun announced Step 5 Preview this weekend — 600 billion parameters total, 27 billion active, a million tokens of context, a dollar per million tokens of input — and it was described as open-weight throughout. The weights are due on 15 October. Until then it is a hosted endpoint with a promise attached, which is to say it is the same object every closed model is, plus a date. This is not a prediction about StepFun, which may well ship on the fifteenth. It is an observation about a word that has quietly stopped describing a property of the model and started describing an intention of the company — and intentions, unlike parameters, have a way of slipping by a quarter.

Editor’s Note

In a hundred and thirty-two previous issues this desk has never once been able to print the following sentence: an outside body was offered terms, and wrote different ones. Nine mathematicians did it today. OpenAI went looking for an advisory board; what it has instead is a group at Princeton that constituted itself, reserved the right to comment on OpenAI’s effect on mathematics, and can publish advice nobody asked it for.

Set that beside the other outsider let in this week. Anthropic built a permission system for biology, kept most of it, and handed the one tier that still worries the company to a federal agency it will not name. Both are outsiders with real power over something that matters. One was appointed; the other appointed itself. The difference is not goodwill, and it is not that mathematicians are braver than biosecurity people. It is that mathematics is old enough to have a body capable of saying no, and frontier biosecurity has no discipline standing behind it at all. That is why its scope went to a government — not because a government was the right author, but because there was nobody else in the room to write it.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
