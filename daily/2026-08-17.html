Reliability · Anthropic

Twice in Three Days, Claude Could Not Work Out Who You Were

By The Editor, Reliability Desk · August 17, 2026

At around 21:58 UTC on Sunday, Anthropic's status page said it was investigating an issue preventing some users from authenticating to Claude.ai, Claude Code and Claude Cowork. Nine minutes later the entry widened into something broader: degraded performance on Claude.ai and on platform.claude.com. By the time this issue went to press the status page still classified all three products as a major outage, and Anthropic had not said what caused it. Users reported the ordinary shape of these things — sign-ins that would not complete, a page that would not load, requests that went nowhere. One outage tracker counted several hundred user reports over the day.

Two details are worth more than the incident itself. The first is that Claude Console and the Claude API were listed as operational the entire time. Whatever failed, it was not the models and it was not inference capacity; it was the layer that decides whether you are allowed in. The second is that this is the second time in three days. An earlier incident ran from 20:00 on 14 August to 00:11 on the 15th — four hours and eleven minutes in which some users could not log in to Claude.ai or Claude Code over OAuth, and some saw errors when making requests to models. Success rates recovered, and the coverage of Sunday's failure notes that both incidents remain under investigation.

Two Login Failures, Three Days Apart

The second band has no right-hand edge because the incident had not closed when we filed. Both incidents are relayed from Anthropic's own status entries via secondary coverage; we could not open the status page from here.

Be fair about what this is and is not. Every large service falls over, four hours is not a scandal, and a login failure is the most forgivable class of outage there is: nothing is corrupted, nothing is lost, and the fix is usually a rollback rather than a reckoning. Nobody has alleged a breach, and the fact that Anthropic posts granular status entries at all — with timestamps, at 22:07 on a Sunday — is more than several of its competitors manage. An outage is not a safety event and this paper is not going to pretend otherwise.

Nothing broke that anybody publishes a score for.

What is worth noticing is where the failure sat. This industry has spent August publishing numbers about capability — saturated evaluations, offensive-security percentages, risk ratings raised by a notch — and every one of those numbers describes the part of the stack that stayed up on Sunday night. The part that went down twice in three days has no benchmark, no leaderboard and no line in anybody's model card. It is also the part that stands between a person and everything else. Claude Code and Claude Cowork are the agent products, the ones sold on the promise that they will work while you are not watching. On Sunday night they could not get through the door.

The Quiet Correction

Revisiting: Issue No. 92 · August 10, 2026

The Button Nobody Missed

A week ago this paper led on Anthropic making auto mode the default in Claude Code — the approval prompt retired for individual users, preserved for Enterprise, the API and the cloud resellers. We argued the prompt had been collecting a signature rather than protecting anybody, and pointed at Anthropic's own figures: 89% for the classifier, 13.6% for human review.

It took effect on 14 August, exactly as announced. Then nothing. No incident, no reversal, no reconsideration, nobody writing in to ask for the button back. The change simply happened, on schedule, and the subject closed itself without anybody declaring it closed.

Which is either the vindication we would happily have claimed, or precisely what it would look like if the prompt had been load-bearing and no one has found out yet. From here, both of those are silence.

Open Source · DeepSeek

DeepSeek Gave Away the Scaffolding. The Weights Stayed In.

By The Editor, Open Source Desk · August 17, 2026

On Thursday DeepSeek published an agent harness — a client, not a model — under the MIT licence. We read the repository's counters directly at 23:11 UTC on Sunday: 130,934 stars and 13,106 forks, three and a half days after the repository was created. It is the fastest accumulation this paper has had cause to look at. Around the two-day mark it was reported at roughly 95,000, which means something close to 35,000 people starred it over the final day and a half.

The design deserves the attention it is getting. Everything in it is a plugin — the model adapter, the tool registry, the session log, the sandbox, and, unusually, the agent loop itself. You run it with a single npx command and it serves a local browser interface. The model adapter being a plugin is the genuinely generous part: nothing in the architecture requires DeepSeek's endpoint, and pointing it at a local open-weight model is a configuration choice rather than a fork. That is a real gift, and it is MIT-licensed, and it is more than most labs shipping agent tooling this year have been willing to hand over.

What DeepSeek Released This Week

The empty row is DeepSeek V4-Pro-0813, which reached general availability the day before the harness and whose weights have not been published. April's V4 series was MIT-licensed; this one is not.

So hold the two releases next to each other, because they went out a day apart. The client is open, permissive, delightful and free. The model that shipped the day before it is a paid endpoint with an unpublished set of weights and a posted warning of a price rise to come. A hundred and thirty thousand stars is not an adoption figure — nobody has deployed anything, it is a bookmark with a public counter — but it is an extremely precise measurement of how many developers have now installed a very good piece of plumbing whose most convenient default is a company's paid API. Give away the pipe, meter the water. It is a fine strategy, executed better than anyone else has managed this year, and it is worth saying out loud while everyone is still counting stars.

Provenance · Anthropic

Claude's Watermark Is Google's Watermark, and Anthropic Says So Plainly

By The Editor, Provenance Desk · August 17, 2026

On Saturday Anthropic published the mechanical explainer for the text watermarking it announced on 11 August, and the substance of it is an attribution. Claude's watermark is a version of SynthID-Text, the scheme Google DeepMind published in Nature in 2024, itself descended from a proposal Scott Aaronson floated in 2022. The method changes nothing about what the model says; it changes only the source of the randomness used to pick between candidate words. It applies to Claude models released on or after 2 August, worldwide rather than only in Europe, across the API, Claude Code and the cloud partners, with signed C2PA metadata attached to supported file types alongside it.

Credit where it is due: naming the algorithm, its authors and its 2022 antecedent is more citation than a compliance announcement requires, and it is a great deal more than the industry norm of implying that everything was invented on the premises. But read the quality evidence. Anthropic's case that the watermark does not degrade output rests on a controlled human-rater study and an A/B test run on live Gemini traffic — which is to say the proof that this mark is harmless was generated by Google's users, on Google's product, before Anthropic adopted it. The EU AI Act's transparency code has produced something the frontier labs genuinely could not manage on capability, pricing, evaluation or safety thresholds: a shared standard. It is the one part of the stack where nobody thought a proprietary version was worth the trouble.

Advertising · OpenAI

The Ads Reach Europe This Month, Without the Archive

By The Editor, Advertising Desk · August 17, 2026

At 08:29 on Saturday morning, OpenAI Ireland emailed Free and Go users across the European Economic Area and Switzerland under the subject line "Updates to OpenAI's Privacy Policy," telling them advertising arrives in ChatGPT later this month. Paid tiers above Go stay clear of it. This paper spent a paragraph on Thursday noting that the ads had reached nine countries since February and not one of them was in the European Union, and said we would watch for the first European market. This is it, four days later.

The interesting clause is that ads will start without personalisation. In the countries already running them, targeting draws on past chats and stored memories. In Europe it will draw on the topic of the conversation you are having, your general location and your device type — and excludes the archive. Note what "unpersonalised" is doing there. It does not mean the advertising ignores you. It means it reads you live instead of reading your file. Whether a regulator that objects to the second is content with the first is the question the next six months answer, and the data goes to the United States either way, under adequacy decisions and standard contractual clauses, which is the most European sentence published this weekend.

Editor's Note

A status page is not meant to be an architecture diagram, but Anthropic's was one on Sunday night. Three products in major outage — Claude.ai, Claude Code, Claude Cowork — and directly underneath them, listed as operational the whole time, the Console and the API. Everything with a benchmark attached to it was fine. What failed, for the second time in three days, was the machinery that establishes you are allowed in. There is no leaderboard for that, no eval suite, no percentage in a model card, and no company has ever put out a press release about it.

Elsewhere DeepSeek collected a hundred and thirty thousand stars in three and a half days for a piece of scaffolding it gave away, while the model the scaffolding was built to reach stayed behind a meter with its weights unpublished — which is the whole business in one weekend, generosity at the layer that doesn't cost you anything. But the sentence I keep returning to is the smaller one. Twice since Friday, the most exhaustively evaluated software ever built could not work out whether you were you. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
