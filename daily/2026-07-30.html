Microsoft Will Spend More Than Meta. On Wednesday It Was Rewarded for It.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · July 30, 2026

Two of the largest buyers of AI capacity on Earth reported earnings within hours of each other after Wednesday's close, and the market read the same appetite two opposite ways. Microsoft posted revenue of $90.01 billion, up 18%, and adjusted earnings of $4.74 a share against a $4.24 estimate; Azure crossed $100 billion for the fiscal year for the first time, up 41%, with management guiding to 45% growth next quarter. It also told investors fiscal-2027 capital spending would run $255–260 billion, a roughly 35% jump. Shares rose about 7% after hours. Meta, reporting the same night, beat on revenue at $60.8 billion but missed on earnings — $6.18 a share against a $7.14 forecast — and lifted its full-year capex guide to $135–145 billion. Shares fell nearly 8%.

Line the two up and the intuitive story breaks. Microsoft is guiding to spend nearly twice what Meta is, and Microsoft is the one that got applause. If Wall Street were simply frightened of AI capex, the bigger number should have drawn the bigger flinch; instead it drew a rally. Whatever moved the two stocks in opposite directions on the same evening, it was not the size of the spend.

Same Bill, Same Night, Repriced Two Ways

After-hours moves on Wednesday's earnings. Microsoft guided to the far bigger spend and rose; Meta's smaller raise fell alongside an earnings miss. After-hours moves settle by the next open.

What Microsoft could do and Meta could not was point at a line where the money comes back. Azure past $100 billion, growing 41%, is a sentence a chief financial officer can say out loud: here is the capacity we bought, here is the revenue it produced, here is next quarter's. Meta's AI spend still lands mostly as cost — sharper ad targeting and a chatbot, real but diffuse — and this quarter it arrived alongside a bottom-line miss. Same category of bet, two different grades of proof.

The market didn't punish the spending. It punished the spending it couldn't yet trace to a sale.

For two years the reflex was that the spending was the signal — the more a company committed, the more serious it looked, and the stock followed the ambition. Wednesday was the night that reflex expired. Ambition is now assumed; everyone is building the same cathedral. What's suddenly scarce, and what the market paid up for, is a revenue line you can attach to the buildout without a deck of projections. Microsoft brought one to the call. Meta brought a bigger check and asked for patience.

Reliability · Anthropic

Claude Spent Wednesday Afternoon Returning One Word: Overloaded.

By The Editor, Reliability Desk · July 30, 2026

On Wednesday afternoon Claude went down worldwide. Outage trackers spiked around 3:50 p.m. Eastern, topping roughly 2,000 reports, as Claude.ai, Claude Code and the API returned “529 — Overloaded,” Anthropic's own capacity signal. The company said it was investigating at 7:49 p.m. UTC, reported the issue identified about forty minutes later, and confirmed full restoration that evening. It did not disclose a root cause.

There is a small, unkind symmetry in the timing. On the same afternoon two of the largest buyers of AI capacity on the planet were telling Wall Street how many more hundreds of billions they intend to pour into it, the flagship model from one of the field's marquee labs fell over because it ran out of the stuff. “Overloaded” is not a crash — it is a building at capacity turning people away at the door. The industry is spending a quarter-trillion a year to never see that error again, and it surfaced anyway, on the day the invoices were front-page news.

The Glossary
The Glossary · A Recurring Definition
Capex
The Official Definition

Capital expenditure: money a company spends to acquire or upgrade long-lived physical assets — the data centers, servers and chips expected to generate revenue over years rather than a single quarter. A boring, load-bearing line on the cash-flow statement.

The Real Definition

What a hyperscaler now says on an earnings call when it means: we have committed a sum too large to walk back to a technology whose returns we are still describing in the future tense, and we would like you to keep clapping. In 2026 the number stopped being a line item and became a loyalty test. The question is no longer how much you'll spend — everyone will spend enormously — but whether you can show, this quarter and not next, the sale the spending bought. Microsoft could. On Wednesday, that was the whole difference.

Editor's Note

We cover models here, which means most nights the scoreboard that matters is a benchmark. Wednesday night it was an earnings call. Two of the biggest spenders in AI reported hours apart, and Microsoft — which guided to spend nearly twice what Meta will — was the one whose stock rose, while Meta's fell on a bigger check and a smaller profit. The market didn't flinch at the size of the bill. It flinched at the one company that couldn't yet point to what the bill bought. Capex stopped being the signal this week; conversion became it.

The day even wrote its own closing joke. While Redmond and Menlo Park were on the phone explaining how much more capacity they'll buy, the capacity already installed at one marquee lab gave out — Claude spent the afternoon answering “overloaded.” Everyone is buying the same thing. The difference the market finally started pricing is whether you can show it earning its keep: capacity you can invoice, not capacity you can only promise. See you tomorrow.

Corrections & Clarifications

The earnings figures — Meta's $60.8 billion revenue, $6.18 EPS against a $7.14 estimate, raised $135–145 billion capex guide and ~8% after-hours drop; Microsoft's $90.01 billion revenue, $4.74 adjusted EPS, Azure past $100 billion up 41%, the $255–260 billion fiscal-2027 capex guide and ~7% after-hours gain — are the companies' own reported results and guidance, relayed through trade coverage (CNBC, Yahoo Finance). After-hours moves are as of Wednesday evening and settle by the next open. The Claude outage — the ~3:50 p.m. ET spike, the “529 — Overloaded” errors, and the investigating/identified/restored timeline — is multi-outlet (BleepingComputer, Newsweek); no root cause was disclosed.

We defined “capex” today as a word hyperscalers use to mean please keep clapping, then filed the definition under a section that costs us nothing to produce. This publication's own capital expenditure remains a metaphor and one domain renewal. Our capacity, unlike Claude's, has never once been overloaded — a fact we attribute entirely to demand.
