Open Models · Kimi K3

Open Weights, Yours for the Price of a Small Datacenter.

By The Editor, Open Models Desk · July 26, 2026

Tomorrow, Monday, Moonshot AI is due to publish the open weights of Kimi K3 — the 2.8-trillion-parameter model Washington spent last week describing as stolen American frontier capability. “Open” here means the file is free to take. It does not mean you can run it. In its 4-bit form the release runs to roughly 594 gigabytes and needs on the order of eight H100 80GB accelerators simply to hold in memory — before it has answered a single token. There is no laptop path, no single-card path, no consumer path at all.

What “open weights” changes is not the cost of running the thing; it's who pays. While K3 lived only on Moonshot's servers, Moonshot rented the GPUs and ate the bill — which is why, a week ago, it had to suspend new subscriptions when demand outran the compute it could afford. Publish the weights and that bill does not disappear. It changes hands. The model becomes free the way a quarry is free: the stone is yours, you bring the trucks. A licence is not a subsidy.

What It Takes Just to Load the Free Model

Loading K3 in 4-bit takes roughly eight 80GB data-center GPUs before a single token is generated. All figures are Moonshot's own and pre-release; nothing has been independently run, because nothing has yet been released.

So the object at the center of a week of sanctions talk — the “concealed extraction” Treasury keeps threatening to punish — turns out to be a 594-gigabyte file that only a firm with a rack can open. Whatever K3 is, stolen or merely studied, the set of people who can actually run it unhosted is roughly the set of firms that could have trained something like it in the first place. The frontier did not democratize this weekend. It grew a bigger download button.

“Open” means the file costs nothing. Running it costs a rack of H100s. Only one of those words made the headlines.

Grant that this is still a real gift: an open 2.8-trillion-parameter model at frontier-adjacent quality is a windfall for the universities, labs and clouds that can host it, and it will surface, cheap, on somebody's API within days. But “open-weight” has quietly come to describe a model most of its admirers will only ever rent — from a middleman, at a markup — which is the exact arrangement openness was supposed to route around. The license frees the weights. It does not free you from the electric bill.

Policy · White House

Washington's Model-Review Deadline Is Days Away. Meta Isn't on the List.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · July 26, 2026

The voluntary framework mandated by the June 2 executive order is due to be formalized by Aug. 1 — now under a week out. It would give federal agencies up to 30 days of pre-release access to “covered frontier models.” The named participants, per this week's reporting: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and now Amazon. The conspicuous absence is Meta — the one major U.S. lab whose flagship models ship as open weights, and therefore the one with the least to hand a reviewer that a reviewer could meaningfully hold back.

“Voluntary” is doing its usual work here: the word means the mandatory version is drafted and waiting. But an open-weights company is a strange thing to gate. You cannot pre-review a model into a 30-day holding pattern when its entire distribution model is that the file leaves the building on day one. The framework can bind the four or five labs that ship behind an API. The one that ships a download is already, structurally, standing outside the room.

The Quiet Correction · Filed July 26, 2026
The Bond That Was Already Sold

On July 21 we described Nvidia as “reportedly preparing” to raise at least $20 billion in “its first debt sale since 2021,” and set it, as a story still to come, against Etched's venture rounds. It was not still to come. Nvidia had priced the deal six days earlier, on June 15 — $25 billion across seven tranches out to 2056, its largest bond ever, drawing some $85 billion in orders. We reported a closed transaction as a plan.

The equity-versus-debt contrast in that issue survives intact; only the tense was wrong. A done deal read to us as an intention — which is the rare direction these corrections run. Usually it's the plan that turns out never to have happened.

Editor's Note

There are two words inside “open weights,” and tomorrow only one of them comes true. Moonshot publishes the K3 file on Monday and it will be, genuinely, open — no fee, no gate, a licence that lets you do very nearly anything. It will not be available, not to you and not to almost anyone reading this, because the thing you are free to download is 594 gigabytes that needs a rack of accelerators to hold still. Open describes the licence. Available describes the electric bill, and nobody touched the electric bill.

Which casts last week's argument in an odd light. Washington spent it deciding whether K3 was stolen American capability worth sanctioning; the funnier fact, arriving Monday, is that stolen or not, the loot weighs as much as a small server room and can be opened only by the same handful of firms that could have built it. The weights keep getting more open and the frontier gets no wider — the download button just got bigger. That is not the democratization anyone was promised. It's the frontier, mirrored, at a discount, for people who already own datacenters. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
