Business · Anthropic

Anthropic Shipped the Model It Was Still Arguing About

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 22, 2026

Claude Opus 5.5 arrived this morning as claude-opus-5-5, Anthropic's new default flagship, live on the Claude API and on Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure. List price is $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, down from $5 and $25. Cache reads fall sixty per cent, to twenty cents a million. Trade coverage of the company's own capability table puts the model at 89.9% on SWE-bench Pro, 10.7 points above Opus 5, and 66.4 on Terminal-Bench 4.0, a jump of 14.1 and a new state of the art.

Two different discounts are circulating, and both come from Anthropic: forty per cent cheaper “on typical workloads”, and twenty per cent off list. They are not the same claim. The twenty is a price; the forty is a price plus an assumption about how heavily you cache, and only one of those numbers appears on an invoice. The price cut is nonetheless real and unusually large. What nobody has explained is the row above it. Claude Fable 5.1 still lists at $10 and $50 — two and a half times the price of a model that, by Anthropic's own published table, outscores it on every line.

Four days ago, in a report this desk read only at second hand, Anthropic was said to be weighing a model release ahead of its public offering, with one source saying the safety of that model was still being evaluated while the release was being deliberated. The model is here. Anthropic says it ranks highly on safety, that it was tested before release by external evaluators, and that it performed more strongly than other models on the company's automated behavioural audit. It has not named an evaluator, published a finding, or said when the testing concluded relative to the decision to ship. Four days after buying itself an embedded evaluator and announcing the fact at length, the first model out of the door was assessed by people the company will not identify.

Every adjective in that safety paragraph is comparative, and the thing each one is compared against is unpublished. “Ranks highly” against what list. “Performed more strongly” than which models. The price, by contrast, is four dollars, and you can check it.

Highly ranked in safety, having been tested before release by external evaluators. — Anthropic's announcement of Claude Opus 5.5, which names none of them

Two Claude Models Have Retirement Dates Their Own Company Can No Longer Meet

By The Editor, Business Desk · September 22, 2026

Anthropic's model-deprecation page commits the company to “at least 60 days' notice before model retirement for publicly released models”. The same page gives Claude Sonnet 4.5 an earliest retirement date of 29 September — seven days away — and Claude Haiku 4.5 one of 15 October. Neither is marked deprecated. Both read N/A in the notice column. For either date to hold, the warning would have had to go out at the end of July and the middle of August respectively, and it did not. Both dates are now dead letters.

This is not a scandal, and it is worth saying so plainly: “not sooner than” is a floor rather than a promise, and a model outliving its earliest permitted death is the harmless direction for the error to run. The interesting part is that the number was published at all. Opus 5.5 arrived this morning with its own floor already set — 22 September 2027, exactly a year. Every Claude model is issued a death date on the day it is born, and that date is the most precise forward-looking statement the company makes about any of them.

The next ten weeks of scheduled model death

The one rust event on this axis belongs to a different company. Anthropic's three dates are floors; the arithmetic that makes them unreachable is ours, not the company's.

Model Obituary

sora-2 · sora-2-pro · the Videos API — deprecated 24 March 2026, removed 24 September 2026

Sora 2

Sora 2 dies on Thursday, and it has been dying for a while. The app that made it famous was switched off in April, so the model has spent five months as a back end serving people who were never the audience. OpenAI gave six months' notice, which is more than most, and the date has not moved.

What distinguishes this death is the paperwork. On the deprecations page, the recommended-replacement column — filled in for every other entry, dutifully, going back to snapshots from the GPT-3 era — is empty. Developers were given a date and left to make their own arrangements. Account data is deleted permanently afterwards, with no recovery window.

It is survived by no successor, by a great deal of generated video, and by everyone who assumed the internet keeps things.

Safety · OpenAI

OpenAI Will Let Outsiders Watch the Training. Possibly on OpenAI's Laptops.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 22, 2026

OpenAI published Priorities and principles for effective third party assessments, committing to let outside groups run technical safety assessments while a model is being trained and evaluated, rather than only in the weeks before it launches. The document names four priority areas; the summaries reaching this desk describe three of them — independent assessment of safety cases spanning training, evaluation and both internal and external deployment; testing of what the company calls the safeguard stack, under “grey box” access, to establish whether safeguards survive jailbreaks and whether they stop capability uplift in cyber and biological work; and a required competence list running from alignment and control methods through monitoring to red teaming. OpenAI says it is in talks with METR and Redwood Research. No assessor has been named and access terms are not set.

Take the advance seriously, because it is one. Every external evaluation in this industry has so far been handed a finished artefact and a window, which is a bit like auditing a building by walking round it after the scaffolding comes down. The decisions worth examining — what went into the run, what was measured during it, what was waved through — are made months before anybody outside is shown anything. Moving the assessor upstream of the object is the correct instinct, and OpenAI is the first to put it in writing.

Then read the clause about where the work happens. Where data is especially sensitive, OpenAI suggests, it may be appropriate for the assessment to be conducted on company-managed devices, or on company premises. The same document asks for strong independence mechanisms. Four days ago Anthropic's answer to the identical question was to place a consultancy's staff inside its own building. Two laboratories, one week, and both have arrived at the view that the way to make an examiner independent is to move them closer to the thing they are examining.

Notice also what each company is missing. Anthropic bought an evaluator and published no standard. OpenAI has published a standard and bought no evaluator. Between them they have one complete arrangement, and it is split across two companies that compete.

Strong independence mechanisms, scientific rigor, robust security practices and clear responsibilities. — OpenAI's stated principles, in a document that also contemplates the assessor working on OpenAI's devices
Agents · Commerce

Amazon Locked Meta's Agent Out of the Shop, and Then Said Why

By The Editor, Technology Desk · September 22, 2026

From Sunday, users who pointed Meta's Muse assistant at Amazon.com got a refusal: continued access by an unauthorised AI agent, the message said, violates Amazon's Conditions of Use, to which its customers have agreed. Amazon confirmed it had asked Meta to remove Amazon from the Muse experience, said Meta never sought permission, and said it had no advance warning that the agent would be shopping there. It is the latest in a year of such moves: Amazon has sued Perplexity over its Comet browser and acted against shopping agents from Google and OpenAI.

Amazon gave two reasons. The first is credential handling — whether Muse looks after the customer's login safely — and it is a real objection that would carry more weight had it been raised before the litigation rather than alongside it. The second reason is the one to read twice. An agent shopping on your behalf, Amazon says, bypasses the personalisation and insights the company has built, so customers may see products that do not reflect their browsing and purchase history.

That is not a security complaint. It is a complaint that the agent buys the thing you asked for instead of the thing the shop had arranged to show you. Every company in this industry is currently promising an assistant that acts on your behalf; the largest shop on earth has now put in writing that acting on your behalf is precisely the problem. The agent wars will not be fought over capability. They will be fought over whose ranking the agent is willing to ignore.

Policy · United Nations

DeepSeek Goes to the Security Council

By The Editor, Policy Desk · September 22, 2026

The United Nations Security Council meets on Wednesday to consider artificial intelligence and international security, convened by France, which holds the September presidency. A French concept note puts the agenda as loss of control and the use of advanced models in ways that bear on international peace and security. Sam Altman will present in the room; senior Anthropic executives will attend, with Dario Amodei expected to address the council by remote link. Invitations went to DeepSeek and to Moonshot; DeepSeek's founder Liang Wenfeng is not expected in person and the company will send representatives.

The composition is the news. For three years this argument has been staged at summits convened by the governments with the most to gain from the outcome — Bletchley, Seoul, Paris — where attendance was an invitation and the communiqué was substantially drafted before anyone sat down. Wednesday is not that. The Security Council has five permanent members holding vetoes, two of whom have laboratories at the same table, and it is the only body in this story that can produce something binding. It is most unlikely to produce anything binding on Wednesday. It is still the first room in this argument whose chairs were not set out by the people being discussed.

Editor's Note

Four days ago this paper carried a report that Anthropic was weighing whether to release a new model before it goes public, and that one person close to it said the safety of that model was still being evaluated while the release was being argued over. Claude Opus 5.5 shipped this morning. The argument is therefore finished, and nobody has said which way it went, when it ended, or what the evaluation found — only that external evaluators, unnamed, saw the model first. Credit where it is owed: the price cut is real, the model appears to be genuinely good, and Anthropic published the earliest date it may be retired on the day it was born, which is more calendar than most of this industry volunteers about anything.

Set that beside what OpenAI published today — the conditions under which outsiders might one day examine a model while it is still being trained. One laboratory has a procedure and no assessor. The other has a model, says assessors saw it, and will not say who. The difference is not candour. It is that one of them is describing an object that does not exist yet, where everything can still be promised, and the other has shipped one, where something could actually have been disclosed. The single forward-looking commitment in Opus 5.5's paperwork is the earliest date on which it may be killed: 22 September 2027. Nothing about who tested it, or what they found, arrives with a date attached at all.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
