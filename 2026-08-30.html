Contracts · OpenAI

OpenAI Cut Off Cursor Over a Trust Problem, and the Remedy Does Not Touch It

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 30, 2026

On Friday OpenAI told SpaceX it will wind down the contract that supplies OpenAI models to Cursor, proposing 12 November as the shutoff. The trigger was a change-of-control provision: SpaceX completed its $60 billion all-stock acquisition of Anysphere, Cursor's parent, on 14 August, and the clause gave OpenAI a limited window to walk. It used it inside a fortnight. The company published its reasoning under its own title — it cannot be confident SpaceX will operate within its terms of service, citing X breaking contractual terms after Musk bought it and xAI violating OpenAI's terms, a violation it notes Musk acknowledged under oath earlier this year.

Credit where it is due, and it is genuinely due: OpenAI gave the maximum notice its contract allowed, said so, and said why — to leave developers as much time as possible. Seventy-five days is more warning than most of this industry gives anybody. Anthropic's published commitment for retiring an entire model is sixty days, and it has never exceeded that by much. A supplier ending a partnership treated the counterparty better than the standard practice for treating your own paying users.

Now the part that does not fit. OpenAI's stated concern is misuse of its technology by a company it does not trust. Its remedy ends the commercial arrangement and leaves the route open: Cursor also serves Anthropic, Google and xAI models, and after 12 November a developer can paste in a personal OpenAI API key and carry on using GPT inside the same SpaceX-owned application. Cursor's chief executive, Michael Truell, puts OpenAI models at about 5% of Cursor's traffic. So the action removes a fraction of a fraction, addresses none of the stated risk, and lands squarely on the one thing it does change: OpenAI is no longer a supplier to Elon Musk.

Whatever the trust problem is, a personal API key solves it for everybody except OpenAI's press office. — on the Cursor cutoff
From option to cutoff: a fourteen-day fuse
Rust marks the only stretch that required a decision. Everything before 14 August was a transaction proceeding on its own schedule; everything after 29 August is a countdown already set. The endpoint is drawn hollow because it has not happened and OpenAI describes it as proposed.
Access · Anthropic

Anthropic Is Giving Scientists the Seat and Selling Them the Ceiling

By The Editor, Access Desk · August 30, 2026

Anthropic opened 10,000 Claude Team seats to scientists this week. Standard seats are free. Premium seats, with five times the usage limits, are $15 a month — an 80% discount, locked for twelve months. Above that, labs can apply to the AI for Science programme for up to $50,000 in credits per project. Eligibility runs through the principal investigator: you verify that you run a lab at an accredited university or non-profit, and then you add your researchers. The company says it intends to expand well past the initial ten thousand.

This is a good thing to do and there is no version of this paragraph that pretends otherwise. It is also worth naming precisely what is being given away and what is being sold, because they are not the same object. Access is free. What costs money is rate — how much of it you may have per unit time. Three tiers, one product, and the only variable across all three is throughput. For a chemistry lab, the thing now standing between a hypothesis and the next run is a subscription tier.

Open Weights · Tencent

Tencent Let a Model Work on Itself. It Pointed It at the Electricity Bill.

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 30, 2026

Tencent open-sourced Hy4 preview on Friday: 770 billion total parameters, 49 billion active per token, a context window over a million tokens, Apache 2.0, weights on the internet. That is a 2.6x jump on Hy3, which shipped at 295 billion in early July. It is live in WorkBuddy and CodeBuddy, free there for a fortnight, and priced on Tencent Cloud and OpenRouter at $0.834 per million input tokens.

Buried in the announcement is the sentence that matters. Tencent says Hy4 took part, for the first time, in the automated optimisation of its own training methods, data strategies, evaluation frameworks and low-level operators — proposing approaches, running experiments, iterating on results, with the code and logs feeding the next round. It also says the model analysed bottlenecks in its own inference system and rewrote enough of it, through operator fusion and communication optimisation, to raise end-to-end throughput by 31.8%.

Note what that number is. Of everything a system turned loose on its own development could have been measured against — capability, reliability, safety behaviour, any of the benchmarks this industry cannot stop publishing — the one figure Tencent chose to put in the launch post is the cost of serving it. The self-improvement went into the margin. This is not a scandal; it is the most predictable allocation in commercial computing, and that is the point. When a company finally gets a model to work on itself, it does not aim it at the hard questions. It aims it at the bill.

The phrase for a model improving its own training is, at one of Tencent's competitors, the name of a risk category with a staff member assigned to it. Here it is a bullet point, with no paper, no methodology and no comparison group behind it. Both companies are being entirely sincere. That is what is unnerving about it.

Given a machine that could improve itself, the first thing anyone improved was throughput. Nobody should be surprised, and everybody should notice. — on Hy4 preview

The Glossary

One term, defined honestly

Preview

The official definition

An early release of a model made available for evaluation and feedback ahead of general availability, so that developers can test it and the provider can gather real-world signal before committing to it.

The real definition

A finished product with the safety catch left off the paperwork. Hy4 preview has 770 billion parameters, a published price per million tokens, a licence, an API and users; the only thing preliminary about it is the word. The word is load-bearing in the other direction, too. Anthropic commits to sixty days' notice before retiring a model, and its deprecation table gives every model a date — except Claude Mythos Preview, which sits deprecated in a note above the table with no retirement date at all. Google will switch off gemini-robotics-er-1.6-preview tomorrow. "Preview" is the industry's only word that lets you ship a thing, charge for it, and owe nobody a calendar when you take it away.

Editor's Note

The longest document any of these companies produced this weekend concerns about five per cent of one product's traffic. OpenAI published a titled essay explaining why it will stop supplying Cursor on 12 November — naming Musk's companies, citing sworn testimony, setting out its contractual window and its reasoning. It is careful and, by the standards of corporate disclosure, generous. It is also about a commercial relationship its own figures call marginal, and it does not prevent a single developer from running an OpenAI model inside Cursor next week.

In the same forty-eight hours Tencent released a 770-billion-parameter model and mentioned, in roughly one sentence, that it had participated in optimising its own training methods and rewritten enough of its own inference stack to run 31.8 per cent faster. No paper, no methodology, no comparison group. Sort the weekend by how much explanation each company thought it owed and the order runs backwards from consequence. Then notice the part that redeems it: the unexplained one is the only one anybody can check. The essay you have to take on trust. The weights are on the internet under Apache 2.0, and a stranger with enough hardware can go and find out.

See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
