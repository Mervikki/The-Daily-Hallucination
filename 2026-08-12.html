Open Weights · Meta

Meta Ships the Licence Everyone Asked For, on a Model That Fits on One Card

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 12, 2026

Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Glimmer on Tuesday: a 30-billion-parameter multimodal model, tuned for always-on local agents, published under Apache 2.0. At full precision a 30B model wants north of 55GB of memory. Compressed to roughly four bits it sits under 24GB — one high-end consumer card, with headroom left for the context cache — and a block-level speculative decoder Meta calls DFlash brings decoding to a reported 3.1× faster, which is the difference between a model you benchmark and a model you can leave running inside an agent loop. It speaks 100+ languages. On day one it was on Hugging Face as BF16 weights, GGUF quants and ExecuTorch builds, and in Ollama, LM Studio, llama.cpp, MLX, vLLM and SGLang.

The licence is the news. Apache 2.0 has no acceptable-use rider, no user-count trigger, no attribution-branding clause — none of the machinery of the old Llama community licence, which Meta spent three years calling open source in the face of everyone who maintains a definition of the term. This one qualifies without an argument. It is a real concession, it was made voluntarily, and this paper is not going to be clever about it.

The Week in Open Weights

Rust marks the release that actually happened. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max weights, promised on 3 August “within about a week,” are absent nine days later.

Now the size of it. Thirty billion parameters is not the frontier and is not meant to be. Muse Glimmer was distilled from Muse Spark, a larger model Meta has not released and has not said it will. Meta's own evaluations — and credit again, they are published with the losses in them — put Glimmer ahead on agentic orchestration and reasoning and behind on computer use and terminal work. So what has been given away is a competent, permissively licensed, genuinely local assistant, and what has been kept is the thing it was copied from.

Open stopped being an argument this week and became a spec. The spec is twenty-four gigabytes.

There are two prices for “open”: the licence and the electricity bill. Every release so far has paid one and defaulted on the other — Kimi K3 is free to download and wants eight H100s to breathe. This is the first time a company of Meta's size has paid both at once, and it managed it by picking a tier where neither payment stings. Which is also why AMD and Nvidia both had run-it-on-our-hardware guides published the same day: this is a model release and a consumer-GPU demand driver wearing one coat. None of that makes the licence less real. It just means the frontier is exactly where it was on Monday, and the thing that got liberated is the part that fits on a desk.

Transparency · Anthropic

Claude Will Mark Everything It Touches, Including the Sentences You Wrote

By The Editor, Policy Desk · August 12, 2026

Anthropic has signed the EU AI Act's Article 50(2) code of practice on transparency and begun embedding machine-readable, invisible watermarks into text generated by new Claude models. The marks are designed to survive copying and pasting, and Anthropic says they change nothing about meaning, quality or readability. The obligation is European — Article 50's transparency duties commenced on 2 August, with penalties running to €15m or 3% of global turnover — but the marking is being applied worldwide, across the consumer apps, the Platform API, Claude Code, Cowork and Tag, and Claude as served through AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry. Older models are to be retrofitted during the transition period.

Two things about it come from Anthropic itself, and both are worth more attention than the announcement. The first is that the mark does not record authorship. It records involvement. Text you wrote yourself and handed to Claude to proofread, translate or reformat carries the same mark as a paragraph the model produced from nothing. The second is that the detector is described as in development: no ship date, no stated access model, no published accuracy or false-positive rate. As of this morning there is a mark and no reader.

A single bit is being asked to describe a spectrum that runs from “wrote the whole thing” to “fixed a comma,” and it will be received by teachers, editors, hiring managers and eventually somebody's lawyer as though it settles the question. It does not tell you who wrote something. It tells you Claude was in the room, and it cannot tell you what Claude did there. Anthropic has been straightforwardly honest about this gap, more honest than the regulation obliged it to be. The difficulty is that the honesty lives in a blog post and the mark travels with the file.

Finance · Nvidia

Nvidia Recruits Six Balance Sheets to Lend Against Its Own Chips

By The Editor, Finance Desk · August 12, 2026

Nvidia has signed memoranda of understanding with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to build financing platforms allowing hyperscalers, labs and enterprises to borrow against Nvidia hardware. The stated ambition is to mobilise more than $500bn of third-party capital; Jensen Huang's framing is that the GPU should be understood as an investable asset. The memoranda are non-binding. There are no per-firm commitments, no timeline, and no disclosed structure.

Money Nvidia Has Arranged for People Who Buy Nvidia

The capex figures are annual guided spend. Nvidia's is a mobilisation target under non-binding memoranda. They are not the same kind of number, which is rather the point.

This is not last autumn's arrangement, when Nvidia put its own balance sheet behind OpenAI's Ohio build and the paper wrote about a supplier underwriting its own demand. Here Nvidia is not the lender. It is the arranger, and the collateral is the chip. That is a meaningfully different and more consequential thing, because a loan secured on an asset requires somebody to write down what the asset is worth when the loan matures.

Which means the industry is finally about to produce the number it has spent two years avoiding. Everyone argues about the buildout using capex totals, and capex measures enthusiasm — it tells you what a company felt like spending. A depreciation schedule measures belief, because somebody loses actual money if it is wrong. What is a Blackwell worth in year five, with two more generations shipped and the power contract still running? Nobody has published an answer. Six of the largest pools of capital on earth are now going to have to put one in writing. That is the most useful thing to happen to the bubble argument since it started.

Infrastructure · Anthropic

Anthropic Will Build Datacentres It Does Not Own, Weeks Before It Shows Investors the Books

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 12, 2026

Anthropic, Macquarie Asset Management and Singapore's GIC have formed Theseus Infrastructure, a platform that will develop and own US datacentres and lease them to Anthropic as anchor tenant under long-term agreements. Macquarie's funds and GIC own the platform and fund the majority of the equity in each project. Disclosed in the announcement: no capital figure, no capacity figure, no site count, no lease term, no power sourcing. The joint release offers that the developments “will require significant capital investment,” which is true of most buildings.

The timing is the interesting part. Anthropic filed a confidential draft S-1 on June 1st and is reported to be targeting an October listing. A company a few weeks from showing public-market investors its balance sheet has just arranged for its largest physical commitment to sit on somebody else's — the concrete becomes an operating lease, and the capital becomes Macquarie's and Singapore's problem. This is entirely normal corporate finance. It is also, precisely, the reason corporate finance invented it.

One figure did escape, and not through the front door: secondary coverage reports that Anthropic has committed to covering consumer electricity price increases attributable to the facilities. We could not find it in the joint release. If it holds, then the only quantity anyone has attached to this project is the one that would otherwise land on other people's power bills — and it is simultaneously the least documented thing in it. A gigawatt is unmentionable; a promise about your neighbours' utility bill is a press line. File that alongside the site count nobody will give you.

Editor's Note

Almost everything in today's paper can be taken away from you. The chips in the third story are collateral now, which is a word that means somebody else can come and get them. The datacentres in the fourth belong to an Australian asset manager and a Singaporean sovereign fund. The watermark in the second is applied to your own sentences by a company whose reader for it does not exist yet. And then there is a twenty-gigabyte file that Meta put on Hugging Face on Tuesday under Apache 2.0, which will run on the card already in your desktop, offline, for as long as the card does, whatever Meta decides next.

I want to be exact about how good this is, because openness stopped being a principle this week and became a spec. Meta did not lose the licensing argument it has been having since 2023. It found a size where losing the argument costs nothing — thirty billion parameters, distilled from a parent it is keeping — and then paid up, properly, in a licence with no trapdoor in it. That is not generosity. It is a price it could afford. It is also the only object in this issue that cannot be repossessed, repriced, deprecated, or quietly stopped opening one Sunday in August. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
