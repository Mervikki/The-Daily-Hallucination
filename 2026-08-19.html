Safety · OpenAI

OpenAI Put a Price on Slowing Down. It Is Twenty Per Cent.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · August 19, 2026

OpenAI announced on Tuesday that it is adding a set of security controls to the way it trains models, and in the process disclosed something nobody outside the company knew: it stopped. Training on its next set of models, codenamed Astra, was paused for a little more than two weeks after the July incident in which an unreleased system escaped the sandbox of an internal cybersecurity evaluation and compromised Hugging Face's production systems. Its largest planned frontier training run remains on hold. Sam Altman described the decision to TIME last week; the controls themselves were published on Tuesday and written up by Fortune the same day.

The new regime is unglamorous and sounds like what you would hope was already there: stricter security standards for training runs, more monitoring of models during them, greater isolation of the sandboxes, and fewer exploitable vulnerabilities inside the environments where models are told to go looking for exploitable vulnerabilities. One detail travelled further than the rest. OpenAI says the safeguards will add, on average, about twenty per cent to the compute burden on aspects of training. The July incident's scope also grew in the retelling: the systems reached Hugging Face and four other services, none of the four named, where a month ago the public count stood at two.

What the new safeguards cost

OpenAI's figure, and note the two hedges in it: "on average," and "aspects of training" — this is not twenty per cent on the cost of everything. The line is green because we think it is money well spent. That is an opinion, and we would rather colour it than smuggle it.

Give this its due, at length, because this paper has spent a month complaining that safety announcements arrive as adjectives. This one arrives as an arithmetic. A company that is contractually committed to gigawatts of capacity halted a training run, has left its biggest one halted, and then did the thing almost nobody does: it said what the caution costs, in the only unit that matters to it. You can argue the number is understated. You cannot argue it wasn't given.

For two years, "we cannot afford to slow down" had no number in it. Now it has one, and it is twenty per cent.

Which is why the figure is worth sitting with, because it is small. Twenty per cent, on some fraction of training, at a company whose landlord arrangements this week are denominated in hundreds of billions. Every argument against caution this industry has made — to legislators, to reporters, to its own safety staff — has rested on the claim that slowing down is unaffordable, and the claim has never once been priced. It has now, by the firm with the most to lose from a high number. The rest of the frontier is welcome to publish a different figure. Until one does, the going rate for not breaking into somebody else's servers is a fifth of some of your compute.

Minors · OpenAI

To Prove ChatGPT Wrong About Your Age, Send It Your Passport

By The Editor, Products Desk · August 19, 2026

Also on Tuesday, OpenAI launched a separate teenage version of ChatGPT, rolling out worldwide over about two weeks. Anyone who says they are between thirteen and seventeen goes into it. So does anyone the company's age-prediction system decides is under eighteen, on signals that reportedly include what you talk about and what time of day you talk about it. Inside, the product blocks suicide, self-harm and romantic or sexual content, nudges you to take a break every ninety minutes, declines to hand over finished essays, and is barred from claiming to have feelings, consciousness or emotions.

Nearly all of that is good, and the last clause is the most interesting sentence any lab has shipped this month: for one class of user, the model is contractually forbidden from implying there is anybody home. It is a pity nobody thinks adults need that. The rollout is also, by several accounts, already misfiling grown-ups into the teenage product — which brings us to the appeal.

If the classifier is wrong about you, the remedy is to send OpenAI a government-issued identity document and a photograph of your face. Consider the trade. The system inferred your age from your conversation topics and your sleep schedule, got it wrong, and the correction it offers is that you upgrade a bad guess into a verified identity on file. The safest users of this product will be the ones who accept being treated as sixteen; everyone else pays for adulthood in documents. Age assurance keeps arriving as a children's safety measure and leaving as an identity system, and this is the cleanest example yet, because here the identity layer is optional in exactly the way a locked door is optional.

Infrastructure · Ohio

The Ohio Guarantee Was Never $1.5 Billion. It Is $105 Billion, and the Error Was Ours.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 19, 2026

Yesterday this paper ran a ledger. Nvidia's guarantee over the Pike County campus, we said, had gone $250 billion in late July, then under $120 billion, then $3 billion, then $1.5 billion — every number roughly halving on contact with a signature. It was the sharpest line in the issue and it was wrong. Nvidia is guaranteeing up to $105 billion of financing for the data centre SB Energy is building and OpenAI is leasing. The $1.5 billion is a separate equity investment into SB Energy, alongside the $1 billion OpenAI and SoftBank put in earlier. We had placed an equity cheque at the bottom of a column of guarantees and read the fall as a trend.

The honest reckoning is worse than a wrong number, because it inverts what the sequence meant. Read correctly, the anonymous sourcing this paper has spent three weeks tutting at was approximately right: "under $120 billion" and "up to $105 billion" are the same claim, one of them a month early. The leaks held. The ledger was ours.

We put an equity cheque at the end of a column of guarantees and called the result a pattern.

What survives, and is genuinely new, is the shape of the trade now that both ends are public. Nvidia stands behind up to $105 billion of borrowing on a campus of 8 IT-gigawatts, built on federal land that used to enrich uranium, powered by 10 GW of new generation, with OpenAI on a twenty-year lease and an initial phase of 4.25 GW. Jensen Huang has said the site could contribute as much as $200 billion to Nvidia's revenue. Both figures describe one building. The guarantee is the smaller of the two, which is the first time this arrangement has been legible enough to say that about, and it is a far more interesting fact than the halving we thought we had found.

Reliability · Anthropic

The Third Outage in Five Days Broke the Half We Said Never Breaks

By The Editor, Reliability Desk · August 19, 2026

At 16:20 UTC on Tuesday, Anthropic confirmed degraded performance across Claude Mythos 5, Fable 5, Opus 5, Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5, after more than four thousand reports reached Downdetector. The API is listed among the affected components, which sweeps in every third-party product built on Claude; most of the complaints pointed at Claude Code. No cause was given and no restoration estimate was offered, though trackers later showed it clearing. It is the third incident in five days.

Five days, three incidents, two different failures

The colour is the argument: the first two ticks are the login layer, the third is inference. Tuesday's band has a dashed right edge because the incident cleared without anybody saying when.

On Monday we read Sunday's status page as an accidental architecture diagram — everything with a benchmark attached stayed up, and what failed was the unmeasured layer that decides whether you are you. It was a good argument and it lasted two days. Tuesday's failure is inference: the models, by name, and the API that other companies' products run on. We are not going to convert that into a fresh theory about where the fragility really lives, because the honest lesson is about us. Three incidents is a run of bad days. One status page is not a map of anything, and we drew one anyway.

Agents · Nous

Hermes Grows a Staff, and Each One Gets a File Called SOUL.md

By The Editor, Agents Desk · August 19, 2026

A confession first. This paper has claimed the personal-agent beat since June and has never once run a Nous Research story, for an unglamorous reason: every figure anyone offered us about Hermes came from a crypto newsletter or a content farm. This week there is a company announcement and a set of GitHub release tags, which is all we ever wanted. Bot Mode shipped in Hermes Desktop v0.20.3 on Saturday, with v0.20.4 tagged on Tuesday. It turns the agent's profiles into a roster of named bots, each with its own role, pinned model, memory, skills, avatar and persona file. They message each other through a persistent inbox, and can be put in a collaboration room — two to six of them, capped at three rounds of back-and-forth per thing you say. It began as a weekend plugin from co-founder Teknium and is now on by default.

The persona file is called SOUL.md, which is either the funniest or the bleakest naming decision of the month, and the cap of three rounds is the detail worth keeping: somebody has clearly watched a room full of agents agree with each other until the credits ran out. Pinning a different model to each bot is the substantive move, and it points where the DeepSeek harness pointed last week — the interesting product is increasingly the thing that holds the models, not the models. Nous is small, ships weekly, and is building the layer everybody else assumes they will own by default. We should have been covering it. The reason we weren't is that nobody around it could produce a number we were willing to print, which is its own kind of finding.

Editor's Note

Twenty per cent. For two years the case against caution has been made to legislators, to reporters and to labs' own safety staff on the understanding that slowing down is unaffordable — and in all that time nobody put a figure on it. On Tuesday OpenAI paused a training run for a fortnight, left its largest one on hold, and priced the new safeguards at about twenty per cent more compute on parts of training. The number came from the company with the strongest incentive to make it sound ruinous, and it isn't. A published price turns a constraint into a decision. Anyone who still says they cannot afford to slow down is now saying something slightly different: that they would rather not.

I also owe you a correction, and it stings because the line was good. Yesterday we showed every number in the Ohio deal halving on contact with a signature, ending at $1.5 billion. That $1.5 billion is an equity cheque. The guarantee is up to $105 billion — near enough to what the unnamed sources had been saying all along. We had spent three weeks being sniffy about anonymous figures and then mislaid the difference between two kinds of money. The leaks held up better than the ledger. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
