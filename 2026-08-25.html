Deals · Nvidia

Nvidia Has Not Acquired Three Companies Since Last September

By The Editor, Deals Desk · August 25, 2026

The reporter Eric Newcomer broke it on 20 August and Bloomberg credited him the same day; the rest of the trade press caught up over the weekend and through Monday. Nvidia is paying Poolside $6 billion for a non-exclusive licence to Model Factory — the internal system Poolside used to build its own models — and separately investing $1 billion in what remains, at a $12 billion pre-money valuation. It is making offers to 109 Poolside employees, the people who built the company's open-weight model Laguna, who will work on Nvidia's Nemotron line. The three founders — chief executive Eiso Kant, co-founder Jason Warner and operations executive Margarida Garcia — are not going. They stay with the company, on unspecified research.

Poolside was available because of a cluster. At the end of last year it had a six-week window to raise $2 billion to pay for 40,000 Nvidia GB300s due to come online in January. The round did not close in time and the cluster went elsewhere. Without it the company could not train at the frontier, and a code-model company that cannot train at the frontier is a consultancy with good intentions. It had raised $626 million at a $3 billion valuation in late 2024, from Bain Capital Ventures, eBay and — this is not a twist so much as an accounting detail — Nvidia.

Eleven months of not buying anything

Three of these marks are transactions. The fourth is a question, and it is drawn hollow because nothing came of it: in March two senators wrote to Jensen Huang asking whether the Groq deal had been "structured to evade scrutiny by antitrust regulators," and five months later the answer arrived in the form of another one. Total consideration is our arithmetic, and one of the three figures was itself reported as "over" $900 million.

Now put it beside the other two. In September 2025 Nvidia paid more than $900 million to license Enfabrica's networking technology and hire its chief executive, Rochan Sankar, and much of his team; Enfabrica remained a standalone company. In December it paid around $20 billion for a non-exclusive licence to Groq's inference technology and took on founder Jonathan Ross, president Sunny Madra and key engineers; Groq, likewise, continued to exist. Each deal has the same three parts: a licence that is explicitly not exclusive, job offers rather than a transfer of staff, and a company left standing at the end of it. That last part is not a courtesy. It is the load-bearing element.

"While we are adding talented employees to our ranks and licensing Groq's IP, we are not acquiring Groq as a company." — Jensen Huang, in an email to Nvidia staff, December 2025

That sentence is not public relations. It is a jurisdictional claim. Acquisitions above a threshold must be notified to the antitrust agencies and can be blocked; licences and hiring cannot, because no company has changed hands. And the survival of the target does double duty — a firm that still exists is still, formally, a competitor, which makes the market look exactly as populated after the deal as before it. An analyst told CNBC in December that the Groq structure was designed to keep "the fiction of competition alive." Eight months on, that reads less like a jibe than a specification.

The temptation is to call this defiance, and it isn't. Senators Warren and Blumenthal put the question in writing in March, in public, to the chief executive by name. No answer to it has ever been made public, nothing followed it, and in August the same structure was used again for the third time. That is not a company daring a regulator. It is a company that has established, through repetition, that the form works — and a form that has been used three times without consequence is no longer a workaround. It is standard documentation, and the next one will take a week to paper.

What is left in Poolside is the strangest object here. It has about $7 billion, three founders, no cluster, 109 fewer engineers, and a licence it remains free to sell to anyone else — Nvidia's rights are non-exclusive, so AMD could in principle buy Model Factory tomorrow. It would be buying software from a company that no longer employs the people who wrote it. Poolside is still a competitor in the AI coding market. It will say so on any form it is ever asked to fill in.

Silicon · Hot Chips

Eighty-Eight Cores, Explained in Public, on a Tuesday Afternoon

By The Editor, Silicon Desk · August 25, 2026

At Hot Chips 2026 at Stanford this afternoon, Nvidia walked through the Vera CPU in the kind of detail the industry has largely stopped volunteering. Vera is an in-house Arm server chip: 88 custom "Olympus" cores on a single monolithic die — a deliberate rejection of the chiplet approach its x86 rivals have taken — paired with 1.2 TB/s of SOCAMM2 memory. The front end is ten-wide on decode and dispatch, with eight integer ALUs, six vector and floating-point pipelines, four load units, two store units and a neural branch predictor that evaluates two branches per cycle. A second-generation coherency fabric spreads the last-level cache across a mesh to avoid the cross-die latency that chiplets impose. Vera anchors the Vera Rubin platform.

The performance claims — 1.8 times faster task completion, twice the efficiency of traditional x86 — are Nvidia's own and arrived on a slide, so price them accordingly. The SPEC CPU 2026 results published alongside them are a different sort of object, and this is the first time they have been shown. Microarchitectural disclosure at this altitude is genuinely rare now, and it deserves saying: a company explaining its branch predictor to a room of engineers who will pick it apart is doing something the rest of this newspaper is mostly about the absence of.

Credit · Broadcom

The First Number in This Deal Nobody Had to Leak

By The Editor, Credit Desk · August 25, 2026

Bloomberg reported on Monday that the bond market has been quietly repricing Broadcom. Yields on the company's 5.15% notes due 2031 rose about 14 basis points over the course of August; five-year credit-default swaps on Broadcom widened roughly 28 basis points across the same stretch. The stated reason is the off-balance-sheet vehicle Broadcom is backstopping — a special-purpose entity that buys custom accelerators and leases them to customers, with Anthropic named among the destinations. Neither figure is large. Twenty-eight basis points is not distress; it is a room full of people adjusting slightly in their seats.

Consider, though, what kind of number it is. This paper has now covered this financing three times in five days. Every figure we have printed came from unnamed people: more than $60 billion on Thursday, upwards of $70 billion on Friday, "up to $80 billion" by Friday afternoon, and two tranche splits that disagreed with each other twenty-four hours apart. We complained about this at some length, twice. A credit-default swap spread is not that. Nobody released it, nobody can retract it, and there is no press office anywhere that can revise it, because it is not a statement — it is the price at which people who are wrong for a living will insure Broadcom's debt.

So the first checkable evidence that this deal exists at all did not come from anybody who knows about it. It came from strangers pricing the possibility of it, which is a lower standard of knowledge and a much higher standard of commitment. An anonymous source describes a deal; a spread bets on one. We have spent two issues asking who will put their name to these numbers, and the honest answer today is that the most informative figure in the story has no name attached and never needed one.

Two cautions, both real. Credit spreads move for many reasons, and August gave the market plenty besides Broadcom; the attribution to the financing vehicle is Bloomberg's framing, not a proven mechanism. And a widening of this size is fully consistent with a deal that closes uneventfully and is never thought about again. The point is not that the bond market is alarmed. It is that the bond market is the only participant so far that has been obliged to have an opinion in public.

Pricing · OpenAI

A Price Cut With an Expiry Date

By The Editor, Pricing Desk · August 25, 2026

On 21 August OpenAI reduced the price of GPT-5.6 Sol by more than 20%: input from $5 to $4 per million tokens, output from $30 to $20. It applies to the pay-as-you-go API, to Codex credits and to eligible ChatGPT Work plans. It was reported, reasonably enough, as frontier intelligence getting cheaper. The part that got less attention is in the announcement itself. The rate is promotional, and it runs for about three months — through roughly 21 November, at which point output tokens cost $30 again unless somebody decides otherwise.

Output price per million tokens, and where it is scheduled to go

Output tokens only; input prices move the same way. This is deliberately not a like-for-like comparison of two models — they are different tiers and they are not substitutes. It compares what each company did to a future date. Anthropic deleted a scheduled increase, which is why its right-hand dot is struck through and its line is dashed: it leads nowhere. OpenAI created one, and called the near end of it a price cut.

Set that next to what Anthropic did eleven days earlier. On 10 August it made Claude Sonnet 5's introductory pricing — $2 in, $10 out — permanent, cancelling a rise to $3 and $15 that had been posted for 1 September. We read that on Anthropic's own release notes at the time and it earned one approving paragraph. Same fortnight, opposite operation: one company removed a future price increase from its documentation, the other added one and let the press report the near end of it.

None of this is sharp practice; promotional pricing is ordinary, and $4 and $20 are genuinely what you will pay this autumn. But there is a difference between a price and a campaign, and the sector's running story — that inference costs are collapsing — is being told with both mixed together. A cut you can put in a budget for 2027 is a fact about the cost of compute. A cut that expires on a Saturday in November is a fact about the cost of customer acquisition.

Unfinished business, for the record: GPT-5.6 Sol Ultrafast, the Cerebras-powered tier previewed on 13 August at up to 14 times standard speed and 750 output tokens a second, still has no published price and no general availability. This paper has been waiting six issues for a commercial term of any kind. It is still waiting.

Enterprise · Anthropic

Someone Has Finally Asked Who Approves the Connectors

By The Editor, Enterprise Desk · August 25, 2026

Anthropic made enterprise-managed authorization generally available on Monday across its Model Context Protocol connectors, which means an IT administrator now decides which third-party systems Claude may authenticate into, rather than each individual user deciding for themselves. Datadog, Notion and Slack join a supported roster that already included Asana, Atlassian, Canva, Figma, Granola, Linear and Supabase; Exa, Miro and Zoom are listed as coming.

This is dull, correct, and quietly an admission. MCP's entire proposition is that an agent can reach into the systems where the work actually lives, and for as long as that has been true the question of which systems has been settled by whoever happened to be sitting at the keyboard when the consent screen appeared. Shipping the answer as a governance feature in the last week of August is not late by enterprise-software standards. It is late by the standards of the pitch.

Editor's Note

There is no verb for what Nvidia did to Poolside. It did not buy it — the company is still there, founders and all. It did not simply invest, though it invested. It did not hire the staff; it made offers to a hundred and nine of them. English has "acquire" and it has "license," and the thing that happened is a third thing with no word of its own yet. A transaction nobody can name is a transaction nobody has to file.

Then notice what the same company did this afternoon: on a stage at Stanford, explained the Vera CPU down to the width of its instruction decoder. This is not a secretive firm. It is a firm that answers every form it is handed, and no form exists for August. So sort today's figures by who chose to release them. The decode width: volunteered. Poolside's price: given to one reporter. Anthropic's connector list: published because a customer asked. And Broadcom's swap spread, twenty-eight basis points wider than a month ago, which nobody announced and nobody can withdraw, because it is not a claim — it is what happens when people with money are made to decide. It is the only number here with no author, and it is the one I would bet on.

See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
