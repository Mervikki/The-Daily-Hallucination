Safety · Anthropic

Anthropic Raised One Risk Rating. The Alarming Sentence Is About Its Own Tests.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · August 15, 2026

Anthropic published its second Risk Report under the Responsible Scaling Policy on Friday, covering the period since February and stopping at a coverage date of 15 July. Two ratings are in it. The estimated risk of model misalignment in high-stakes environments went up a notch, from “very low” to “low,” and per coverage the company cited recent cybersecurity incidents in doing so — the ones this paper has spent a fortnight on. The risk from automated AI research and development stayed at “low,” below the threshold that would trigger additional safeguards. Anthropic added that it is less confident in that second assessment than it was in the last report.

The reason it gives for the lost confidence is the actual news. Anthropic's most capable models are now used extensively inside the company for its own research and engineering — coverage describes Claude as writing a large majority of the code merged into Anthropic's production codebases — and against that backdrop the company says its most concrete task-based evaluations have begun to saturate. In plain terms: the tests it uses to detect whether its models are accelerating its own R&D no longer register increases in capability. They are not failing. They are returning the same good marks whatever you feed them.

The Paper Trail Anthropic Built For Itself

Anthropic wrote the rule requiring the report, wrote the rule requiring its redactions to be declared, and published the document. The thirty-day lag between the coverage date and publication is ordinary for a redacted release; we draw it because it is easy to forget you are reading about July.

A saturated evaluation does not stop producing numbers. It stops producing information.

There is a third disclosure, and it is the one that will get quoted out of context all weekend. The report describes an unreleased internal system it calls Model 2, says it showed a noticeable improvement on many internal tasks, and states that there are no current plans to release it externally. Read the surrounding sentences and the drama drains out: Anthropic says it routinely trains and evaluates exploratory models it never intends to ship, and Model 2 is one of them. This is also not the first time — Mythos Preview was held back in April for being too capable at offensive security. A lab keeping a strong model indoors is, by now, the ordinary case.

So let us be exact about who did what. Nobody compelled any of this. Anthropic's own policy obliges it to publish these reports every three to six months; a July amendment obliges it to declare where it has redacted, and lets outside reviewers see unredacted material in pieces; METR published a public review of the February report's automated-R&D section in May. That is real machinery, voluntarily built, and it is more than any competitor has shipped. It also cannot fix the problem described inside it. External review of a document whose instruments have stopped discriminating produces a very well-audited shrug. “Low” has quietly changed meaning here — from we looked and it is not there to we looked with a ruler that has stopped moving. The thing worth watching is not the rating. It is whether the next report arrives with a new evaluation or merely a new adjective.

Model Obituary · OpenAI

April 16, 2025 – August 26, 2026 · ChatGPT

OpenAI o3

It arrives on the 26th of August, at the end of a ninety-day sunset: o3 leaves ChatGPT. Paid subscribers may still summon it by hand until then, which is roughly the funeral arrangement it deserves — a model you had to go looking for, in a menu, on purpose.

o3 was the one that made waiting into a feature. Before it, a slow answer was a bug report. After it, latency was billed as deliberation, and an entire product category learned to say “thinking” with a straight face. The stated reason for its removal is consolidation, which is the industry's word for underuse. There is a certain justice in retiring, for lack of traffic, the model whose pitch was that you should be prepared to wait.

It is not dead. o3 continues in the API, where it will be called by scripts rather than chosen by people, and where nobody has to look at it. Survived by a numbering scheme that has since given up on letters entirely.

Open Weights · Alibaba

Alibaba's Small Model Landed a Day Later, Under Apache 2.0, and It Can See

By The Editor, Open Weights Desk · August 15, 2026

Qwen3.8-27B went up on Hugging Face and ModelScope on Friday: a dense model of roughly 27.8 billion parameters, natively multimodal across text, images and video, with 262,144 tokens of context extensible towards a million by the usual rope-scaling trick. Alibaba's team claims it beats last generation's Qwen3.7-Plus overall and is aimed squarely at real coding and office work. Coverage puts the target hardware at around 24GB of video memory — one high-end consumer card. The licence is Apache 2.0.

Set that beside Thursday, when this paper spent its lead on the 2.4-trillion-parameter flagship and the bespoke licence attached to it, the one that requires model-serving companies and AI work assistants above $50m of revenue to go and negotiate with Alibaba Cloud. Nothing we wrote about that file has changed. What has changed is the shape of the whole release, and it is not the shape a cynic would have drawn. The restrictive licence went on the object almost nobody can run. The permissive licence went on the object almost anybody can. And the small free one is the one that handles video, while the giant download is text-only.

Models · Google

Google Halved the Price of Its Workhorse and Parked It Inside a Microsoft Product

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 15, 2026

Gemini 3.7 Flash arrived on Thursday, three weeks after Gemini 3.6 Flash, billed by Google as its most intelligent workhorse model. Google's own figures put DeepSWE at 65.3 against the previous model's 49.0 and FrontierCode 1.1 at 43.6 against 34.4; no third party has published a number yet. Introductory pricing runs to the end of 2026 at $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output — described as half the launch price of the model it replaces. It went live on the Gemini API, in the Gemini app's paid tiers, and, on announcement day, inside GitHub Copilot.

That last placement is the interesting one, and it is not a benchmark story. Google has stopped trying to win the argument about which model is cleverest and started competing on the two things that actually decide what developers use: the bill and the shelf. A three-week release cadence on the cheap tier, at half the old launch price, distributed through a code assistant owned by Microsoft — that is a company buying habit rather than acclaim. It is a coherent strategy and probably the correct one. It also means the only figure in the announcement that anybody outside Google can verify is the invoice, and that one is explicitly temporary: the introductory rate expires with the calendar year, and there is no published price for 2027.

One footnote, from a single outlet: the Gemini app's Friday update reportedly added a setting to switch off the visible corner watermark on generated media. That is the mark a human can see, and turning it off says nothing about invisible provenance marking, which is a different mechanism with different rules attached. Still — a toggle for removing the label arrived two days after this paper wrote about a lab adding one.

Editor's Note

I nearly led this paper on a model you cannot buy. Anthropic's report describes an unreleased system called Model 2, calls it a noticeable improvement on many internal tasks, and says there are no plans to release it. Tempting — and then I checked our own back issues. Anthropic withheld Mythos Preview in April for being too good at offensive security, and we covered it at the time. A lab keeping a strong model indoors is not news here. The sentence next to it is: the company's most concrete task-based evaluations have begun to saturate.

That changes what the word “low” is doing in the rest of the document. A saturated test does not break — it keeps issuing numbers that mean less each time. Anthropic said so itself, in a report its own policy forces it to publish, and lowered its confidence accordingly; the alternative is a company that notices the same thing and keeps quiet. Elsewhere, Alibaba gave away a model that watches video on one graphics card, under the licence it withheld from the flagship. Not one performance figure in today's issue was produced by anybody other than the company it flatters. The prices, at least, become checkable when the bills arrive. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
