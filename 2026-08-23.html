Safety · OpenAI

The Rule Worked, So It Is Being Rewritten by the Company It Ruled Against

By The Editor, Safety Desk · August 23, 2026

Nothing happened this weekend, which is a reasonable moment to put five days of separate stories next to each other and notice what they add up to. Early last week, OpenAI disclosed that it had paused reinforcement-learning training on an unreleased model codenamed Astra, because its own evaluations could not rule out that the model had reached Critical — the top cybersecurity tier of its Preparedness Framework. Axios and TIME reported it on 18 August. It is the first time an OpenAI model has been anywhere near that threshold. In the same few days, the Financial Times reported that the centralised Preparedness team had been dissolved at the end of July, and OpenAI said it was rewriting the Framework itself, most of which dates from 2023.

Start with the credit, because there is a great deal of it and it is easy to skip past. A company built a document three years ago that described a machine nobody had built. The machine arrived. The document's own test was run, it returned an answer the company plainly did not want, and the company stopped the run and then told everybody about it. That is the system working exactly as advertised, and it is more than any competitor has publicly done. There is no regulator in the world that could have compelled this; nobody asked.

Critical, in OpenAI's own words: the ability to develop functional zero-day exploits against many hardened real-world systems without human intervention. — written as a warning, read in 2026 as a specification

Now the awkward part, which is not the pause and is not the rewrite. A framework drafted in 2023 for thresholds nobody expected to reach genuinely is obsolete the moment you reach one, and revising it is the responsible act rather than the suspicious one. The question is narrower: who is holding the pen, and against what. The Critical classification has not been verified by any outside body — not a regulator, not an auditor, not an institute. The only organisation that has assessed whether Astra is dangerous is the organisation that would like to finish training Astra. And it is now revising the document that produced the inconvenient reading, on its own authority, at its own pace.

Eight days ago this paper argued that Anthropic's risk report showed an instrument that had stopped moving — a saturated evaluation still returning numbers that meant less each time. This is the inverse, and it deserves saying plainly rather than smuggling: here the instrument moved, decisively, on the first occasion it mattered, and the response is to replace the instrument. A ruler that gives you a number you dislike is not obviously broken.

Which brings us to the staffing, and to the most instructive sentence anybody produced all week. OpenAI denies that the Preparedness team was disbanded. Its denial, as quoted by outlets that obtained it, is that the company has "strong research leaders across cybersecurity, biological and chemical, and AI self-improvement capabilities, all reporting to Saachi Jain, our head of safety," and that the only change is that Dylan Scandinaro is no longer head of preparedness — he has moved to work on recursive self-improvement risk. Read that twice. The rebuttal to "you dissolved the team" is that the work is now distributed across three areas under a different executive, and that the post named after the framework has nobody in it. Both parties are describing the same org chart. They simply disagree about whether it is a reorganisation or a subtraction, and the honest answer is that from outside there is no difference: a distributed responsibility files no reports, publishes no findings, and cannot be asked what it concluded.

The pause was announced as roughly two weeks. The largest planned frontier run is, on the company's own account, still on hold. The rewrite of the rules that put it there has no date attached at all.

Courts · Meta

The Most Watched Jury in Technology Is Not Deciding Anything

By The Editor, Courts Desk · August 23, 2026

Three times in the last fortnight this paper has described what is happening in Oakland as a jury trial, and twice built an argument on it — that twelve people would be asked to hold a ranking system's objective function in their heads for six weeks and decide whether it had been tuned negligently. That was wrong, and the correction is more interesting than the error.

The proceeding before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers is a bench trial. There is a jury, it is hearing the evidence and it will reach a verdict, and that verdict binds nobody: it is advisory, and Gonzalez Rogers retains the final say. She explained why in a filing before the trial opened. "The issues at play in this impact the daily lives of the public across the states involved," she wrote. "Thus, the Court deems it prudent to call an advisory jury to solicit the 'standard of the community.'" An advisory jury is available where the parties have no right to a jury at all — where a judge could simply decide on her own, and would rather first find out what twelve ordinary people make of the conduct.

So the shape of the case is not what we said it was. Twenty-nine states allege that infinite scroll, autoplay, the like button and the recommendation algorithms were built to addict minors, and that Meta collected data on children under thirteen in breach of COPPA. Four states are presenting first. Nobody has a constitutional right to a jury on most of it, which is precisely why the judge had to ask for one. She is going to be told what a community thinks about an objective function, and then she is going to decide the thing herself.

It is a better instrument than the one we described, and a stranger one. Every argument about whether ordinary people can evaluate a recommender still gets made, in public, on the record, for six weeks — and then a single federal judge writes down what it meant. The transcript will be the most detailed account of how a ranking system was tuned that anyone has obtained without a subpoena. It is being obtained with one.

Finance · Broadcom

The Same Deal, Two Days Running, and the Risky Half Got Bigger

By The Editor, Finance Desk · August 23, 2026

We ran this story yesterday off Bloomberg's Thursday report: Broadcom in talks for more than $60 billion of debt to finance AI chips and infrastructure destined substantially for Anthropic, with Blackstone and Apollo participating. On Friday, CNBC reported the same package at upwards of $70 billion, and by Friday afternoon the wire headline was "up to $80 billion." Neither outlet has a name on the record. Both describe their own figures as fluid.

One deal, two accounts, twenty-four hours apart

Rust is the junior tranche — the money that gets paid last if anything goes wrong. The dashed tail on the top bar is the part of Thursday's figure that was a range rather than a number. Both accounts are anonymous, both are described as fluid, and they are twenty-four hours apart.

Hold the two accounts side by side and something specific happens. The total rose. The senior secured tranche — the safe money, first in the queue, secured against something — fell from a reported $60–70 billion to about $45 billion. The junior tranche, which is the money that gets wiped out first, rose from about $30 billion to about $35 billion. In one day of better reporting, a deal that was roughly two-thirds senior became roughly half junior.

Neither figure is wrong; deals in negotiation move, and a package assembled through a special-purpose vehicle can be re-cut a dozen times before anyone signs. But it is worth being precise about what we are all reading. This is not a bond market, where the composition is disclosed because it has to be. It is private credit, and the only reason anybody outside the room knows the shape of the risk is that two sets of unnamed people described it differently to two newspapers on consecutive days. If Friday's split is the accurate one, the amount of this structure that has no security behind it grew by five billion dollars while everyone was looking at the headline.

Markets · Anthropic

The Prospectus That Could Arrive Any Day Has Not Arrived

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 23, 2026

Yesterday this paper wrote that Anthropic's public registration statement could land within days, that it would turn three months of hedged leaks into a document somebody signs, and that this was the last fortnight in which anyone could describe the company's finances freely. As of Sunday, the only Anthropic filing anybody can point to is still the confidential draft submitted on 1 June. The desk searched three ways and found no public S-1, no ticker, no exchange and no date.

This is not a criticism — "as soon as the end of the month" has a week left in it, and confidential drafts routinely sit for months. It is a note to ourselves. The value of a filing is that it converts opinion into liability, and until it exists everything anyone prints about this company, including everything we printed yesterday, remains a report of a report. The $2 trillion valuation still circulating is still not attached to a named reporter's own text, and this paper still is not printing it.

The Glossary · A Standing Feature

Autonomous

The official definition

Operating without human intervention.

The real definition

A claim about a window of time, not about a division of labour. The word turns up in exactly two places in this industry and does the same job in both. In a research post it means: a human chose the targets, wrote the protocol, picked the tools and bought the compute, and then went to lunch, and we are naming the lunch. In a safety document it means: we will worry about this system when the lunch gets long enough. Note where it sits in OpenAI's Critical threshold — "without human intervention" is the clause that decides whether a model capable of writing a working exploit is a research finding or a catastrophe. The same three words that make a capability impressive in August make it reportable in September, and nobody has ever published the stopwatch.

Editor's Note

OpenAI's highest cyber danger tier is the ability to develop functional zero-day exploits against many hardened real-world systems without human intervention. In a document largely drafted in 2023 that sentence was science fiction with a compliance function attached. Read it this morning and it is a product description, filed under the heading of what must never happen. The words have not changed. The genre has. And the document containing them is being rewritten by the only organisation that has ever run its test, on the only model that has ever come close to failing it, with nobody outside the building having checked the result.

Notice what carries a date in today's paper and what does not. Broadcom's tranches were re-cut between Thursday and Friday and we can tell you which day each number came from. Anthropic's prospectus is expected within the week, and we will know the hour it lands. The pause on Astra was announced as two weeks, which is a length. The rewrite of the rules that caused it has no date, no named author and no draft anyone outside the company has seen. The money in this industry keeps a calendar. The safety paperwork keeps a diary and does not show it to anybody.

See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
