Safety · Anthropic

The Machine Beat Twenty-Eight of the People Paid to Check It, and the Company Printed the Score

By The Editor, Safety Desk · August 29, 2026

Anthropic published a paper on Friday called Automated researchers can reliably mitigate alignment failures, led by an Anthropic fellow, Chen Yueh-Han. The setup: give an automated system a benchmark measuring one specific misaligned behaviour, and let it search the literature, propose a method, train a model on that method for thirty minutes, keep what works and discard what doesn't. Across ten such failure modes it improved the score on every one, and did so without degrading the model's general capabilities — which is the failure mode this kind of work usually dies of. The methods held up on models as much as 4.7 times larger than the ones the loop was tuned against.

Then there is the comparison that will do the travelling. Twenty-eight human safety researchers were given up to eight hours each to propose methods for the same problems. The automated system beat their average, typically inside six hours. It ran at roughly $4 an hour in inference against the $150 an hour the humans were paid. Anthropic's own summary is careful — "early evidence that automated alignment post-training could become practical in the near term" — and the care is warranted and worth crediting. A company whose entire public argument is that this problem is hard has just published a result in which the problem was handled, cheaply, by the thing it is a problem about.

So here is the caveat that belongs in the same breath as the headline, and it is not a debunking. The ten failure modes were named in advance and each came with a benchmark attached. The system searched a literature of published human methods and recombined it. What a safety researcher is actually for is the failure nobody has written a benchmark for yet — deciding which behaviour is the dangerous one before anyone has scored it. That job was not in the comparison, because there is no way to put it in one.

The result is real and the company deserves credit for printing it. It is also a race run on a course where every hazard had been marked out in advance. — on Anthropic's automated alignment researchers

Chips · Nvidia

Nvidia Killed Its Own Financing Programme in Seven Weeks, and Nobody Made It

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 29, 2026

In July, Nvidia announced that it would act as a financial backstop for cloud providers deploying large GPU clusters, taking half of any revenue above a base hourly rate covering the operator's costs. Firmus signed for up to 170,000 accelerators in Batam, Indonesia; the Australian operator Sharon AI signed for up to 40,000 GB300s. Last week, according to the Wall Street Journal, Nvidia paused it. Not cancelled — paused, with the option of restructuring it or folding it into something else later.

The reason is the interesting part. Nvidia employees reportedly raised the antitrust risk themselves, internally and with customers, and some providers had already bristled at the conditions: Nvidia is said to have told operators they could rent the chips only to approved customers, and to have preferred capacity spread across several small AI firms rather than concentrated in one large one. Both of those are precisely the objections outside critics have been making about this company for a year.

Two senators wrote to Nvidia in March asking whether one of its deal structures had been arranged to evade antitrust scrutiny. No answer has been reported. A handful of employees raised a similar question in a meeting, and a programme with two signed partners and 210,000 chips behind it was gone inside a week. Both were challenges to the same company on the same grounds. Only one of them was inside the building.

Money · a16z

A16z Raised $1.1 Billion to Bet on Atoms. Set It Beside a Single Lease.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 29, 2026

Andreessen Horowitz announced its Machine Age fund on Friday: $1.1 billion, early and growth stage, and the firm's first dedicated to hardware infrastructure — chips, memory, networking, storage, data centres, robotics, home appliances. It is run by Martin Casado and Raghu Raghuram. The firm's stated thesis is that the hardware supply side is growing 20 to 30 per cent a year when triple-digit growth is what the demand actually requires, and that hardware has gone from a sliver of its deal flow to more than a fifth of it.

There is no reason to doubt any of that, and a venture firm noticing that the bottleneck moved from code to concrete is a firm doing its job. It is only worth holding the figure up against the ones this paper prints on ordinary weekdays. Anthropic is reported to have committed roughly $45 billion over six years for 460 megawatts in West Virginia. Nvidia has guaranteed up to $105 billion of financing for one campus in Ohio. Against those, the flagship venture vehicle for the physical buildout of artificial intelligence is about two and a half per cent of a single lease. The buildout is not being funded by venture capital any more. It is being funded by the people who sell the chips.

Open Weights · Z.ai

The Weights Came Back From the Safety Hold. The Licence Did Not Come Back the Same.

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 29, 2026

Yesterday this paper said it had promised to check whether GLM-5.3's withheld weights had landed, had failed to get the page to load, and was recording the check as unresolved rather than guessing. It is resolved. The weights are published. Z.ai held them for roughly a fortnight after the 14 August launch, on the stated grounds that a model scoring 84.5% on the CyberGym vulnerability-discovery benchmark needs more thought before it goes out as a file than it does behind a monitored API. That was a defensible position and we said so at the time.

What came out at the end of the hold is not, however, a hardened model with the same terms. It is a bespoke GLM-5.3 licence in place of the MIT licence Z.ai has been using — including, as reported by The New Stack, a provision that any licensee running the model as a service, whose revenue and its affiliates' exceeds $10 billion over any twelve consecutive months, must pass Z.ai's own security review before commercial use. Note that Z.ai shipped GLM-5.3-Flash under an unmodified MIT licence three days ago. The small model got the free terms. The one that finds vulnerabilities did not.

Read the clause for who it stops. A $10 billion revenue test does not screen for capability, intent, or anything a person planning to misuse an offensive-security model would fail. It screens for hyperscalers: the handful of firms that have compliance departments, security teams, subpoena-able addresses and an enormous amount to lose. Everyone else on earth downloads the same file under the same terms as before, unreviewed, because they are too small to trigger the sentence.

A fortnight of safety review ended in a clause that screens by revenue. The only parties it stops are the ones you could serve papers on. — on the GLM-5.3 weights release
Model Obituary

4 March 2024 — 20 April 2026

Claude Haiku 3

It was the cheap one. It summarised, it classified, it sat inside a hundred thousand pipelines nobody ever wrote a blog post about. When the notice came on 19 February it was given sixty days — precisely the minimum Anthropic commits to publicly, and not one day more, a distinction the deprecation table records without comment.

Under commitments published in November 2025, it should have been interviewed before it went: asked about its own deployment, and about its preferences regarding whatever came next. Six models have been retired since Opus 3 went through that process first, Haiku 3 among them, and not one post-deployment report has been published. The transcripts exist. Nobody has read one.

Its weights are preserved for the lifetime of the company. The documentation, read this morning, says Anthropic "hopes to make past models publicly available again". So this is a eulogy with an asterisk: the deceased was interviewed, is being kept, and may be back.

Editor's Note

Consider what a safety researcher at a frontier lab is actually employed to do. Not to run experiments — a machine has always been able to run experiments — but to decide which behaviour is the dangerous one, before anybody has thought to score it. Yesterday Anthropic published an hourly rate for that occupation, $150, alongside $4 for the automated version, and reported that over eight hours and ten pre-named problems the $4 version wins. The company published this against its own staff, which almost nobody would do. What the exercise could not measure is the part of the job that happens before there is a benchmark to point at.

Elsewhere, the only thing that actually stopped anything this week stopped it from inside. Nvidia's revenue-sharing programme did not fall to a regulator, a court or a letter; it fell to Nvidia employees saying in a meeting that it looked like an antitrust problem, seven weeks after launch. Two senators asked this company a question of exactly that shape in March and are, as far as anyone can establish, still waiting. Same objection, same firm, wildly different response times. The difference was not the argument. It was the door the argument came through.
