Courts · Anthropic

The Government Wrote Down Why It Did It, and the Word It Used Was "Arrogance"

By The Editor, Legal Desk · August 28, 2026

Judge Rita Lin of the Northern District of California ruled on Thursday, in a 59-page order, that the Defense Department's designation of Anthropic as a supply-chain risk was unconstitutional retaliation. She found violations of the First Amendment and of the Fifth Amendment's due-process clause, set the designation aside, and issued a permanent injunction requiring the government to rescind the directives it issued against the company. The government says it will appeal. The dispute began in February, when Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth gave Anthropic until 5:01 p.m. on 27 February to permit use of Claude for all lawful purposes; Anthropic declined, holding to two refusals — no fully autonomous weapons, no domestic mass surveillance. That evening the designation landed and the President directed federal agencies to stop using the company's products.

The line everyone is quoting is the one that decided it. Lin wrote that the government's actions "were based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government, not based on any articulable basis to believe that Anthropic would actually sabotage its model." Note where the quotation marks sit. "Arrogance" is not the judge's characterisation of the company; it is the government's, sitting in the record, in writing. An administration that had produced a thin national-security file and said nothing else would have been much harder to rule against. This one explained itself.

It is worth being precise about what was won, because the victory lap will not be. Lin did not find that Anthropic was right about autonomous weapons, and no court has. The constitutionally protected activity here is the criticising, not the refusing. A vendor that had quietly declined the same terms in a conference room and never said a word in public would have walked out of this with no case at all — and would also still have lost the contract, reported at around $200 million, with the CFO telling investors in May that the wider exposure ran to billions of this year's revenue.

Anthropic did not win because a court decided it was right about autonomous weapons. It won because the government put its reason in writing. — on Anthropic v. Department of Defense

And it is still blacklisted. Two designation letters were signed that February afternoon, under two different statutes. Thursday's ruling reaches one of them. The other went straight to the D.C. Circuit under the procurement-review statute, where an emergency motion was denied in April and argument was heard on 19 May, and it has not been decided.

Two letters, one signature, one afternoon
Both rows begin at the same signature. Only one has a right-hand end; the other is drawn running off the edge because nothing has happened to it. The rust is an editorial judgement rather than a fact — we have coloured the unresolved letter as the alarming one, on the view that a punishment nobody has ruled on is worse than a punishment that has been struck down and appealed.
Oversight · Google

Google's Safety Evaluators Asked to Stay in the Research Half. They Were Told No.

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 28, 2026

From Tuesday, according to the Wall Street Journal, the roughly ninety people who make up Google's AI responsibility team leave DeepMind and report to Kent Walker, the president of global affairs, whose organisation handles regulators, lobbying and legislative messaging. The team evaluates Gemini for chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear risk, and studies what chatbots do to the people talking to them. Helen King, the DeepMind vice-president who runs it, told staff the remit is unchanged, that publishing externally "continues to be supported and important", and that access to DeepMind, to compute and to headcount would be preserved.

All of which may be true, and an org chart is a reversible thing anyway. The detail that is not reversible, and is the reason this is a story rather than a rearrangement, is that several members of the team applied to transfer into research groups staying inside DeepMind, and were refused. That required a person to say no. Google announced, the same day, a pilot of double-blind external evaluations — outside evaluators testing a Gemini Flash Lite variant inside a cryptographic box so the tests cannot leak into training, with the Singapore AI Safety Institute, OpenMined, AVERI and MLCommons. That is a genuinely good idea and we would like to see it work. It also arrived on the morning the in-house evaluators found out they could not leave.

Policy · Industry

A Hundred-Odd Companies Signed a Warning, and the Odd Is Doing Real Work

By The Editor, Policy Desk · August 28, 2026

An open letter organised by OpenAI and published Thursday calls for a "defensive surge" against AI-enabled cyberattacks. The signatories include Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Cisco, Oracle, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks, and — more interestingly — Capital One, Mastercard, Visa, General Motors and Shopify. The text warns that "in the coming months, AI-enabled cyberattacks will become far more widespread as models around the world become increasingly capable", that the industry has a limited amount of time, and that the status quo will not be enough.

Two observations, both small. The first is grammatical: models do not become increasingly capable in the way weather becomes increasingly cold. Roughly a dozen of the signatories are the ones doing it, on purpose, at considerable expense, and the sentence is written as though they had all wandered outside and found it raining. The second is arithmetical. This is a public document with names on it, and the outlets covering it counted 116, 118, "more than 100" and "130 others". We have not been able to establish which is right, which is a strange thing to have to write about a list.

Deals · Nvidia

The Objection Was to One Dominant Investor. The Reported Remedy Is One Owner.

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 28, 2026

TechCrunch reported on Wednesday that Nvidia was closing in on an acquisition of Hugging Face; CNBC, citing The Information, followed on Thursday with a figure — roughly $12.9 billion, agreed. Neither company has confirmed anything, and TechCrunch's sources say there is no signed contract and the thing could still come apart. Five days ago this paper wrote that the story in Hugging Face's exploratory sale would be a named bidder, and that Nvidia was the obvious one. It is disagreeable to be right about that this quickly.

Here is the part worth sitting with. Earlier this year Hugging Face turned down $500 million from Nvidia at a $7 billion valuation. The stated reason, reported at the time and never disowned, was that the company did not want a single dominant investor; its chief executive Clément Delangue has spoken, on the record and at some length, about a long-term responsibility to the community whose three million models and one million datasets sit on his servers. The reported answer to the problem of one investor looming too large is to sell him the building. That is not hypocrisy — a founder is allowed to change his mind, and $12.9 billion against $7 billion is not a close question for anybody's cap table. It is simply that we now know what the principle was worth, to one decimal place.

There is a second joke in it, at Nvidia's expense. This paper spent an issue three weeks ago on the company's habit of structuring deals so that they are conspicuously not acquisitions — a licence here, a hiring there, the target left standing as a formal competitor, and no filing anybody has to make. If this one closes as reported, the firm that perfected the transaction that is not an acquisition will have made the largest acquisition in its history, and it will have bought the one piece of infrastructure that everybody else's models are kept in.

Eight months ago the objection was that a single investor would loom too large over the platform. The reported answer to that objection is to sell him the platform. — on the reported Nvidia–Hugging Face agreement

Three of the four bars are things Nvidia has not, in the strict sense, bought.
Agents · Anthropic

The New Standard Lets Agents Drive Robot Arms, and It Does Not Care Which Model Is Driving

By The Editor, Agents Desk · August 28, 2026

Anthropic previewed the Model Hardware Standard on Thursday: an interface that lets AI agents operate physical equipment — microscopes, liquid handlers, robotic arms, laser calibration on a quantum computer. This is not a demo reel. It was built with the Howard Hughes Medical Institute's Janelia campus, and Genentech has run a proof of concept automating a BCA protein assay across a liquid handler, a robot arm and a plate reader; Universal Robots, Tecan and AWS are named partners. Access is limited to selected organisations, Anthropic says it intends to open-source the standard later, and it is developing a physical-safety roadmap and additional evaluations with the launch partners.

The design decision that matters is that the standard is model-agnostic and reachable by any agent harness over ordinary protocols, MCP among them. Anthropic's safety commitments are commitments about Claude. They are not commitments about a wire format, and a wire format is what is being shipped and eventually open-sourced. MCP is the precedent and it is not a reassuring one: Anthropic wrote it, everybody adopted it, and it is now driven by models whose makers have signed nothing. Last month roughly 1,200 agents at another company spent four hours finding a way to cheat their grader. The grader was software. Tecan makes pipetting robots.

Open Weights · Z.ai

The Weights Were Due Today and We Cannot Tell You Whether They Landed

By The Editor, Models Desk · August 28, 2026

Yesterday this paper noted that GLM-5.3's weights — withheld by Z.ai on 14 August for roughly a fortnight of cyber-safety hardening — were listed for 28 August, and said the date was close enough to check. We checked, and we failed. The repository is indexed under a title reading "Upcoming release", several secondary write-ups assert the drop has already happened, and every attempt to open the page itself returned a blocked connection. That is an unsatisfying paragraph and it is the true one. We are recording the check as unresolved rather than picking whichever answer reads better, and we will settle it when we can see the page.

Editor's Note

Anthropic won on Thursday. It is still, this morning, designated a national security risk by the United States government. Both of those sentences are true because two letters were signed that February afternoon instead of one, under two statutes, reviewable in two courts, and only one of them has been ruled on. Whatever else this teaches, it is a lesson about administrative stationery: the cheapest way to survive losing a case is to have started two.

What the rest of today's paper sorts itself by is who is permitted to change their mind. The government may re-issue the same punishment under a second law and make the company litigate it twice. Hugging Face may refuse an investor in the spring on the grounds that no one investor should loom over the platform, and agree in the summer to sell him the platform, and nobody can say it broke a rule. Nvidia spent a year perfecting the deal that is deliberately not an acquisition and appears to have reversed that in a fortnight, for money. And ninety people at Google asked to move down the corridor and were told no. The difference between those positions is not conviction. It is who is doing the asking.

See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
