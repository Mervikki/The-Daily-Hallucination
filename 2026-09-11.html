Safety · Anthropic
Everything in the Report Was Built With the Models You Can Just Buy

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 11, 2026

Anthropic published Detecting and Countering Misuse of AI: September 2026 on Thursday, its third such report since March 2025 and by a distance its most specific. It covers December 2025 to August 2026 across seven categories of harm, and the conventional-weapons section alone runs to six cases — three in China, two in Russia, one in Yemen. In one, a Russia-based actor used Claude Code to build an autonomous FPV kamikaze drone swarm whose onboard model could select targets, including a person class, and issue detonation commands with no human in the loop; the vision classifier was trained on scraped Ukrainian combat footage. In another, a China-based actor assembled a roughly 16-module electronic-warfare and air-defence-suppression suite, then mid-project changed the simulation’s default scenario to 12 targets in Taiwan — a command bunker, an early-warning radar site, and Patriot and Tien Kung batteries.

The credit is real and it is unusual. Anthropic says it disrupted every operation in the report. It names nine influence operations run out of Russia, Iran, Turkey and across the Gulf, South Asia, Africa and Europe; a Russian espionage campaign that automated breaches against more than 20 organisations; and PRC-linked surveillance work that produced templated dossiers on Catholic cardinals, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, Tibetan Buddhists and Falun Gong. It blocked five attempts at biological research that could have supported weapons work, one of them a grant application for gain-of-function experiments on chikungunya aimed squarely at transmissibility and immune evasion. And it volunteered the sentence nobody makes a company write: older models sat well below the threshold at which they could meaningfully help build a biological weapon, and for today’s models that is no longer something Anthropic will assert.

Now the part that sat in the coverage unremarked. Across all seven categories and all nine months, Anthropic’s own frontier models — the Fable and Mythos classes — appear in exactly one case. Not the drone swarm. Not Taiwan. Not the chikungunya application. The single exception is a distillation case, which is to say the one occasion on which the frontier model was not the tool but the thing being taken.

The obvious explanation is also the correct one, and it is a point in Anthropic’s favour: Mythos-class access is gated, so of course it is thin on the ground in a misuse report. Grant that entirely. The residue is still uncomfortable. Every threshold this industry has written — Preparedness tiers, trusted-access lists, the federal rate card that ran in this paper yesterday — rations the frontier. The capability that actually produced sixteen modules of air-defence suppression was agentic coding, which is not gated anywhere, by anybody, and which no lab’s framework has ever proposed a threshold for. The gate is sound. It is one floor above the work.

Products · OpenAI
OpenAI Is Now a Data Vendor That Also Reasons

By The Editor, Products Desk · September 11, 2026

Also on Thursday, OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services, a tailored ChatGPT Work configuration running on GPT-6 Astra and shaped by a design partnership with Morgan Stanley and Evercore. The stated starting point is investment banking and equity research, and the stated target is the research, modelling and pitchbook work that junior bankers currently do. It shipped alongside an Agents API public beta and GPT-Live-1 voice in the API.

The automation-of-analysts line is the one everybody ran, and it is the less interesting half. Look instead at the plumbing: premium datasets from Daloopa, PitchBook, LSEG News and Crunchbase are not connected to, they are hosted and indexed by OpenAI, with citations down to the table. Entitlement integrations follow for S&P Capital IQ, MSCI, Dow Jones Factiva and Moody’s, among more than fifty connectors. A company that sells reasoning has spent the year quietly becoming the place the underlying corpus lives. That is a much stickier business than a good model, and a much harder one to leave.

Security · China
They Weren’t Training on Claude. They Were Reselling It.

By The Editor, Security Desk · September 11, 2026

The distillation section of Anthropic’s report deserves its own story. The company says it observed roughly 200 million exchanges tied to distillation across five campaigns. The bulk is attributed to Alibaba: 151 million exchanges between May and July 2026, peaking near three million a day, which Anthropic calls the largest wholesale distillation effort it has ever seen. The campaigns went after the capabilities Anthropic sells hardest — agentic behaviour and tool use, coding and data analysis, logical reasoning.

Moonshot AI and DeepSeek, on Anthropic’s account, did something else entirely. They forwarded their own customers’ requests to Claude and showed those customers Claude’s answers. Over a single ten-day stretch Moonshot is said to have relayed nearly 300,000 customer requests through a network of 5,380 fraudulent accounts, against total distillation activity of more than 23 million exchanges in the May-to-July window.

Distillation and relay are not the same offence and it is worth being precise about why. Training a student model on a teacher’s outputs is a dispute between two companies about a terms-of-service clause; reasonable people argue about it. Taking a paying customer’s question, passing it to your competitor, and returning your competitor’s answer with your logo on it introduces a third party who was never told and never asked. Alibaba’s campaign is six times the size and half the story. Somewhere there are people who paid Moonshot for an answer, got a good one, and were impressed by the wrong company.

One caveat belongs in the body rather than the footnotes: every figure above is Anthropic’s, measured on Anthropic’s own logs, naming Anthropic’s competitors, with no outside party in a position to check it. What makes it more than a grievance is the timing. On Wednesday — a day before the report — the NSA, FBI and CISA issued a joint advisory naming six China-based companies, DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI, and concluding that distillation functions as “the critical core” of their development programmes.

Antitrust · Nvidia
The Justice Department Has Been Asking Since December

By The Editor, Antitrust Desk · September 11, 2026

The New York Times reported this week, matched by Bloomberg, that the Justice Department is investigating whether Nvidia structured its roughly $20bn arrangement with Groq to avoid antitrust review. Nvidia took a non-exclusive licence to Groq’s chip technology and hired several of its executives, founder Jonathan Ross among them, and did not buy the company. The same shape appears at Poolside: $6bn for the model factory, 109 staff. The inquiry opened shortly after the December announcement and a formal request for information has gone to Nvidia.

This desk wrote in July that a transaction nobody can name is a transaction nobody has to file. It turns out the Justice Department had been asking that exact question since December, and the only new fact this week is that somebody finally reported it. Note what makes this hard, because it is not a loophole in the sneering sense: antitrust law asks whether control of a company changed hands, a non-exclusive licence does not transfer control, and Nvidia’s position is therefore not merely arguable but probably right. Which is the interesting bit. One deal in this shape is two ordinary commercial decisions. Three of them is a method, and the law has no word for a method.

Editor’s Note

For two years the industry’s answer to the bioweapons question has been the same from everybody: the models are not good enough to matter. It was a reasonable answer and it was probably true. On Thursday Anthropic stopped giving it. The report says that older models sat well below the threshold at which they could meaningfully assist in building a biological weapon, that for today’s models the evidence no longer supports the same assurance, and that a wide range of dual-use biological queries has therefore been restricted from Fable 5 onward. It is reported as the first time a major lab has said so in public. Nobody asked. There is no form on which it was required, and no regulator that would have noticed its absence.

Set that against the week’s other true sentence. Nvidia’s licence from Groq was non-exclusive, so control never changed hands, so there was nothing to file — and that is not a dodge, it is simply correct, which is presumably why the Justice Department has spent nine months on it. One company gave up a sentence it was entitled to keep saying until somebody proved otherwise. The other keeps a sentence that is entirely accurate and carrying an enormous amount of weight. The uncomfortable part is not that Anthropic withdrew the assurance; it is that withdrawing it was available only to Anthropic. Thresholds, tiers, trusted-access lists, yesterday’s federal rate card — all of it rests on assurances of exactly this kind, and every one of them can only ever be taken back by the party who gave it. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
