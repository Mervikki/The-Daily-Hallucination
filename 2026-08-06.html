Labs · Google

Google Moved Its Lab Chief Upstairs and Bought a Stake in the Man Who Walked Out.

By The Editor, Labs Desk · August 6, 2026

Two announcements landed at Alphabet on Wednesday and they are usually read as one bad day. Demis Hassabis stepped down as chief executive of Google DeepMind. He remains the lab's chairman, adds the title of Alphabet chief scientist, and continues to run Isomorphic Labs — three positions where there was previously one job. Koray Kavukcuoglu, DeepMind's chief technology officer, takes over as senior vice-president of the unit, reporting directly to Sundar Pichai. Separately, Jeff Dean left after twenty-seven years.

Dean is founding Discovery Loop, which reporting describes as an independent public benefit corporation built to automate multi-step scientific and engineering experimentation. Sanjay Ghemawat and Quoc Le are named alongside him. The seed round is co-led by Radical Ventures and Khosla Ventures, is not closed, and carries no disclosed valuation. Two other details are doing most of the work here and neither has been much remarked on: Alphabet is a founding investor, and Google is the cloud provider.

You have not really lost a chief scientist if you are his investor, his landlord and his supplier.

So read the two announcements as one motion rather than two departures. Inside the building, a research organisation that had spent a decade being conspicuously semi-autonomous — its own name, its own culture, its own founder-chief — now reports to the chief executive of Google with one fewer layer in between. Outside the building, a company that has put the word independent in its own legal description has Alphabet on its cap table and Alphabet's servers under its experiments. The word moved outdoors. The arrangement stayed where it was.

Eleven Weeks of Not Shipping, Then a Reorganisation

We are not telling you the reorganisation was caused by the model. We do not know that, and neither does anyone who would like to quote us saying it. We are telling you these are the same eleven weeks.

Which brings up the part nobody at Alphabet said out loud on Wednesday. Gemini 3.5 Pro was announced at I/O on May 19th. It has not shipped. Ship targets came and went in June and again in mid-July, and on July 21st Google released the Flash tier — 3.6 Flash, 3.5 Flash-Lite, Flash Cyber — while the flagship stayed in what its product lead has for weeks described as partner testing. A company can restructure its research leadership for a dozen honourable reasons. It is still true that this one did it in the eleventh week of a delay.

And it is worth being fair about the shape of the outcome, because Alphabet's day was not the disaster the headlines made of it. It lost a chief scientist and gained a portfolio position, a cloud customer, a shorter line between the chief executive and the models, and a founder-figure retained on a title that carries prestige and no organisation. Discovery Loop may well be a great company. If it is, Alphabet is early. If it isn't, Alphabet is out a seed cheque. Losing your most famous engineer to a competitor is a problem. Losing him to a subsidiary of your own optimism is a strategy.

Silicon · Anthropic

The Engineer Who Helped Build OpenAI's Chip Team Is Now Building Anthropic's.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 6, 2026

Anthropic confirmed on Wednesday that it is assembling an internal custom-silicon team, with the stated intention of designing chips and Claude models against each other rather than sequentially. Its existing arrangements — Amazon, Google, Nvidia, AMD — continue unchanged. The technical lead is reported to be Clive Chan, hired in early June, previously the second hardware hire on OpenAI's chip programme and before that at Tesla's Dojo effort. The poach is corroborated separately from the hiring announcement, which is more than can be said for most of what is circulating about this story.

This makes the set complete. Google has TPUs, Amazon has Trainium, Microsoft has Maia, Meta has MTIA, OpenAI has its own programme and a foundry relationship to go with it. Anthropic was the last frontier lab whose entire compute position was somebody else's product catalogue. It is now the last one to start doing something about that, which is a different sentence with the same subject.

Custom silicon is the slowest object in this industry. Design to tape-out to volume is measured in years, and a team announced in August 2026 is making a claim about the end of the decade, not about next quarter. So the announcement is not really about chips. It is a thing you say to your suppliers, in public, while you are still buying from them: that their roadmap and your roadmap are not the same document, and that you have noticed. Anthropic currently runs on hardware sold by two of its own investors. Designing your own is the most polite available way to mention it.

Infrastructure

A Company Founded in January, Worth $2.4 Billion, Just Signed a $10 Billion Contract.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 6, 2026

On Tuesday Anthropic committed to roughly $10 billion over six years with Volta Infra Holdings, an Nvidia-backed AI cloud company that came out of stealth to announce the deal and appears to have been founded in January. The capacity is not Volta's either: it is leased from the Tydal campus in Norway, owned by the bitcoin miner Bitdeer, running Nvidia's Vera Rubin silicon on hydroelectric power. Anthropic's share is reported at 121 of the campus's 133 megawatts. Bitdeer's subsidiary signed a sixteen-year lease worth about $4.7 billion, rising to roughly $8 billion over twenty-four years if every option is exercised. First capacity is due December 31st, second phase March 31st.

The Contract Is Four Times the Company

Volta is not a company that won a contract. It is a contract that grew a company around it — and the second bar is the rent it will be paying out of the first.

Put the week's two Anthropic stories in the same sentence and the strategy resolves. The company is renting other people's chips, in other people's buildings, from a five-month-old intermediary financed by the chip vendor, on a converted bitcoin site above the Arctic-ish reaches of Norway — and it has simultaneously started hiring people to design the chips. Six years is a long lease. It is also, roughly, how long it takes to stop needing one.

Editor's Note

The most informative document published in AI yesterday was an org chart. Demis Hassabis is no longer chief executive of Google DeepMind; he is its chairman, and Alphabet's chief scientist, and still running Isomorphic Labs — three titles where there used to be one job. The lab itself now reports to Sundar Pichai. On the same day Jeff Dean left after twenty-seven years to found what the paperwork calls an independent public benefit corporation, whose founding investor is Alphabet and whose cloud provider is Google. Inside the building, a lab that answered to itself now answers to the chief executive. Outside it, a startup that calls itself independent answers to a cap table Alphabet sits on. The word went out the door. The arrangement did not.

Nobody at Alphabet mentioned the eleven weeks. Gemini 3.5 Pro was announced on May 19th and still has not shipped; two ship targets passed; in July the Flash tier went out in its place. I am not going to tell you the reorganisation was caused by the missing model, because I don't know that. I will say that every version of this story, however you tell it, ends with Pichai one line closer to the models. See you tomorrow.
