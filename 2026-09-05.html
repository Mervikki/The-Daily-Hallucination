Mathematics · Anthropic

Claude Didn't Prove Fermat's Last Theorem. The Paperwork Was the Open Problem.

By The Editor, Mathematics Desk · September 5, 2026

Anthropic published a research post on Friday saying that Claude, running as dozens of agents on an academic platform called Prove2Me, produced the first end-to-end machine-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean 4. Its account: eleven days, largely autonomous, more than thirteen million lines of Lean, thirty thousand-odd theorems and around six billion output tokens, with human mathematical input limited to occasional high-level nudges from Tianyi Peng at Columbia, who built Prove2Me. The repository is public under Apache 2.0. We opened it, which is more than we can usually say. It contains 60,475 modules, 29,511 theorems, and this at the top: a ^ n + b ^ n ≠ c ^ n, for 3 ≤ n, with no sorry, no added axioms and no native_decide escape hatch. It rests on Lean's three standard axioms and the build fails if it doesn't.

The version of this on the internet is that an AI proved Fermat's Last Theorem. It did not. Andrew Wiles did, in 1995, and Claude wrote none of the mathematics. The obvious correction — that this is mere translation, clerical work, a very expensive typist — is also wrong, and it is the more interesting error. Formalising Fermat is a famous open problem in its own right. Kevin Buzzard has been running a project at Imperial College to do exactly this since 2024, on a five-year EPSRC grant that runs until September 2029, and that project is still going. Its partial output is one of the ingredients here, alongside Mathlib and flt-regular. Buzzard reviewed the artefact and has been quoted calling it an extraordinary autoformalization achievement that proves the theorem on no assumptions beyond the axioms of mathematics.

So the load-bearing fact is not the eleven days. It is that the repository ships its own refutation kit. Two independent Lean kernels were pointed at the proof and agreed with it: a comparator tool, and nanoda, an implementation in Rust, which returned "Checked 1,052,234 declarations with no errors." Anyone can repeat this. The cost of doing so is about five and a half hours on ninety-six parallel jobs, roughly 67GB of build artefacts, another 220GB of generated C, and a peak of somewhere between 153 and 230 gigabytes of memory. Windows is not supported, because the file paths are too long.

I want to be precise about what is impressive, because the press release version and the debunk are both lazier than the thing itself. Nobody discovered anything on Friday. What happened is that an enormous quantity of tedious, unglamorous, error-intolerant formalisation — the kind of work that has a five-year grant attached to it because nobody knew how long it would take — got done in under a fortnight by a process whose mathematical supervision consisted of a man occasionally typing that Mazur should be finished soon. That is a smaller claim than the headline and a much larger one than the correction.

The popular version of this story is wrong. So is the correction to it. — on what actually happened to Fermat this week
Eleven days, drawn on the grant's own clock
The bar is the funded human effort to do this job. The rust mark is the run that did it. Both are drawn to the same scale, which is the only honest way to draw them and also the reason you can barely see one of them.
Benchmarks · OpenAI

Astra Scored 99.9% and 62.7% on the Same Test. The Variable Was Memory.

By The Editor, Benchmarks Desk · September 5, 2026

When OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Astra on Wednesday it led its case with four numbers, and the loudest was 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark that measures how well an agent learns its way around an interactive environment it has never seen. This paper printed that figure on Thursday with the note that nobody outside OpenAI had reproduced any of the four. ARC Prize, which owns the benchmark, has now had a go. On its own provider-neutral Standard harness, Astra at maximum effort scores 62.7% on the semi-private set. On OpenAI's Provider Adapter harness, at a lower effort setting, it scores 99.9%.

Neither number is fake and ARC Prize says so plainly. The difference is what the model is permitted to carry from one request to the next. Under the Standard harness it can keep visible notes. Under the Provider Adapter it can additionally preserve opaque reasoning state and compact its own conversation history — that is, it gets to keep more of the work it already did. Thirty-seven points, same weights, same puzzles. And the prices run backwards: the 62.7% run cost about $26,000 and the 99.9% run about $19,000. More retained state, less money, better score.

Here is why this is not a harness-configuration footnote. ARC-AGI-3 exists to measure how efficiently a system learns something unfamiliar. How much of your previous attempt you are allowed to keep is not an implementation detail of that measurement; it is substantially the thing being measured. So the honest statement is not "Astra scores 99.9%" and it is not "the real number is 62.7%" — it is that the benchmark turns out to be extremely sensitive to a variable nobody was arguing about, and that the vendor picked the setting where its model looks like it has solved the test. ARC Prize, to its considerable credit, added that it is not claiming anyone has built AGI. That correction was necessary, which tells you how the week went.

Thirty-seven points, same weights, same puzzles. The variable was how much of its own homework it got to keep. — on ARC Prize's re-run of Astra

Capital · Anthropic

Every Date in Anthropic's Offering Moved. The One It Is Aiming At Can't.

By The Editor, Capital Desk · September 5, 2026

Anthropic's prospectus was going to be public as soon as this coming week — after Labor Day, per reporting this paper carried on 27 August. It is now expected late September. The roadshow will not start before mid-October. And the listing is now being aimed at the days just before the United States midterm elections on 3 November. Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Citigroup are on it. Everything, as ever, is subject to change, which is the one part of this sentence that has been consistently accurate.

The slippage is about three weeks at every stage, which is unremarkable in itself; large offerings move. What is worth noticing is who absorbs it. The company gets three more weeks to prepare its document. Its bankers get three more weeks to sound out demand. The institutions being asked to buy the largest offering in history get a roadshow starting in mid-October and a listing in the first days of November, which is roughly three weeks to read a prospectus none of them has seen yet and decide what a company with no public financial history is worth. Every other party's clock got longer. Theirs is the one that got compressed, because the far end of it is a date nobody at Anthropic chose.

And still no S-1. That is the fifth consecutive issue in which this desk has recorded its absence, which is starting to feel less like a news item and more like a hobby.

Two dates that moved, toward one that didn't
Both slopes run downhill by about three weeks. The dashed line is the one fixture in the schedule, and it belongs to the electorate rather than to the underwriters.
Capital · Nvidia

Nvidia's $99bn Portfolio Is a Mark, Not a Price, and It Was Struck on 26 July

By The Editor, Capital Desk · September 5, 2026

CNBC reported on Friday that Nvidia's equity investments are now worth $99bn, up from about $7bn a year ago and about $2.2bn two years ago. More than $40bn of that was committed during 2026; CFO Colette Kress has said the company has put nearly $50bn into frontier AI labs, and at least $6.5bn has gone into photonics and optics since March. The obvious story here is written already and this paper has barred itself from writing it again.

Two things the coverage mostly skipped. First, the valuation date is 26 July — the figure was six weeks old when it was published, which is what happens when most of a portfolio consists of stakes in companies that do not trade. $99bn is not a price anyone paid or would pay; it is a considered opinion, held by the party with the least incentive to mark it down, about assets whose prices are set by rounds that Nvidia is frequently in. Second, Alphabet and Amazon each carry more than $100bn of equity investments on recent earnings. Nvidia is not even the biggest holder of this kind of paper in its own industry. It is just the one whose customers keep appearing in the portfolio.

Agents · Nous Research

Hermes Threw Away Three Quarters of Its Agent Loop

By The Editor, Agents Desk · September 5, 2026

Nous Research shipped Hermes Agent v0.21.0, its first release we have had cause to write about since early August. The additions are the usual agentic land-grab: a Bot Mode with multi-agent group chats and peer-to-peer direct messages, persistent memory for scheduled jobs, live steering of running subagents, and desktop browser automation over MCP. The number worth printing is the subtraction. The agent loop went from 16,083 lines to 3,821 across fourteen modules — a 76% cut — in a week when the rest of this paper is about things getting larger. We have not read the release notes first-hand; the figures are Nous's own.

Editor's Note

The most consequential piece of software in this week's news is called nanoda. It is an independent Lean kernel written in Rust, it is at version 0.4.13, and pointed at Anthropic's thirteen-million-line proof it printed: checked 1,052,234 declarations with no errors. That sentence is the whole reason the story is worth printing. Anthropic did not ask anybody to take its word for it. It published the artefact, the build instructions and the axiom check, and two kernels it did not write agreed. In a hundred and eighteen issues I cannot recall another frontier-lab claim that arrived with the machinery to refute it attached.

Which is why the other half is worth saying out loud. The eleven days, the six billion tokens, the agents working "largely autonomously" — none of that is in the repository. It is the company's account of its own process, and it is precisely the half that travelled. OpenAI had the same week inverted: it led on 99.9%, ARC Prize re-ran the model on a neutral harness and got 62.7%, and both numbers are honest. So both of this week's headline claims have now been independently checked. One held and one didn't. I would bet money that on Monday both are still being quoted in the form their authors wrote them.

See you tomorrow. — The Editor
