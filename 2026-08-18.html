Money · Anthropic

Anthropic Published Two True Numbers About Itself. Nineteen Billion Dollars Separates Them.

By The Editor, Money Desk · August 18, 2026

In a regular update to its investors on Monday, Anthropic said its annualised revenue run rate passed $65 billion at the end of July — more than sevenfold its pace at the end of last year, and up from the $47 billion it disclosed in May. In the same disclosure it put preliminary second-quarter revenue above $11.5 billion, against $787 million in the year-ago quarter, a roughly fourteenfold jump. The company generated something near $10 billion across the whole of 2025. It filed its prospectus confidentially in June and a listing is expected in September or October.

Start with the part that deserves credit, because this paper has spent most of August complaining about the opposite. These are the company's own figures, given to its own investors, with its name attached, weeks before a filing that will make lying about them expensive. On Sunday we ran OpenAI's $40 billion run rate, which reached the world through people who would not be named. The difference between a number somebody stands behind and a number somebody leaks is the whole of financial journalism, and Anthropic is on the right side of it here.

Three disclosures, one company, one scale

The $65bn is Anthropic's. The $46bn is ours — four times a preliminary quarter the company described as "more than" $11.5bn, so treat it as a floor. Both describe the same firm on the same afternoon.

Now the arithmetic, which takes four seconds. A quarter above $11.5 billion, multiplied by four, is at least $46 billion. The run rate published on the same day is $65 billion. Nothing has been fudged: a run rate annualises the most recent month, and for a company growing this fast the most recent month is always the best one, so the two instruments must disagree and the gap between them is simply how steep the curve is. But notice what the more flattering instrument actually is. A run rate is a description of a twelve-month period in which the company's growth stops dead tomorrow and its revenue never moves again — the one year that will certainly not happen to a firm that has gone from $10 billion to $65 billion since Christmas.

A run rate is a sentence about a year nobody has lived through, in the tense of a year that is already over.

The instrument is not the scandal. The scandal would be if this were the only number available, and it isn't — the quarterly figure is right there, in the same update, and Anthropic published it too. What the run rate buys is a headline denominated in the future while the auditable number waits underneath it. Every company about to list does this. It is worth saying plainly once, in the week before the prospectus becomes a document with consequences.

Reliability · Anthropic

Sunday's Outage Ran Thirty-Six Minutes. We Filed As If It Might Run All Night.

By The Editor, Reliability Desk · August 18, 2026

Two corrections, and they go to the spine of yesterday's lead. The Claude incident that began at 21:58 UTC on Sunday was resolved by 22:40 — about thirty-six minutes, not the open-ended major outage we drew with a chart that deliberately had no right-hand edge. And we reported that the Claude API and Console stayed operational throughout, and built the whole argument on that split; BleepingComputer's account has the degradation reaching five products, the API and Console among them. If that is right, the tidy picture of a failure confined to the login layer was tidier than the facts. We were wrong about the shape of it, and the shape was the argument.

What survives is duller and better sourced. The cause has still never been stated — not on Sunday, not since, and there is no postmortem. No further incident has been logged on Monday or Tuesday. And the rolling ninety-day numbers put the month in proportion: everything Anthropic runs sits between 99.34% and 99.82% availability, which reads as five identical numbers until you convert them into the unit a person actually experiences.

The same five numbers, in hours

Percentages are Anthropic's own rolling figures, read Monday evening; the hours are our arithmetic against a 2,160-hour window. The window moves, so by the time you check, these will not be your numbers.

Infrastructure · Ohio

Everyone in Pike County Signed Something. The Longest Signature Belongs to the Tenant.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 18, 2026

The Ohio deal this paper has been chasing since July finally has names on it. Nvidia announced it has guaranteed SB Energy's PORTS-Pike Technology Campus in Pike County, Ohio, to exclusively host Nvidia compute — securing what the release calls land, power and shell capacity. SB Energy will build, own and operate the data centre. SB Energy and SoftBank will construct at least 10 GW of new generation, yielding 8 IT-GW of capacity across an initial 4.25 and an option on the remaining 3.75, plus at least $4.2 billion of regional grid investment. Nvidia is putting $1.5 billion into SB Energy, joining SoftBank and OpenAI on the cap table. OpenAI is the customer, on a twenty-year lease, and has added $40 million to SB Energy's existing $40 million community fund. The build is billed at 35,000 construction jobs through 2032 and 2,500 permanent ones.

Hold that against what this project looked like when it was made of anonymous sources. In late July the story was a $250 billion Nvidia guarantee. By Friday, better reporting had it under $120 billion and covering phase one only. On Saturday, The Information had Nvidia in talks to put $3 billion into SB Energy. The figure that actually arrived, in a document with a company's name on it, is $1.5 billion. That is not a scandal either — unnamed sources describe deals mid-negotiation and negotiations move — but it is now a consistent direction of travel. Every number in Pike County has roughly halved on contact with a signature.

Nvidia guaranteed the land, the power and the shell. It did not guarantee the rent.

The more interesting thing is who took on what. Nvidia wrote an equity cheque and a guarantee over a campus that will run its own chips. SB Energy owns the building and takes the operating risk. SoftBank builds the electricity. And OpenAI — the only party in the chain that sells to the public, and the one whose own revenue reached the world last week through people who would not be named — put its signature on twenty years of rent. Of everybody at that table, the tenant accepted the longest obligation and holds the least of the asset.

Courts · Meta

The Recommender Goes on Trial in Oakland

By The Editor, Courts Desk · August 18, 2026

Meta enters federal court in Oakland this week to face California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey, who allege it designed Instagram and Facebook to hook children, misrepresented the risks, and collected data on minors unlawfully. The states are seeking up to $1.4 trillion in penalties along with product changes. It is a bellwether in a multidistrict litigation consolidating thousands of similar claims, and it is expected to run about six weeks.

The reason it belongs in an AI paper is the list of features named in the complaint: infinite scroll, autoplay, the like button, and the recommendation algorithms. That is a ranking system on trial for optimising the metric it was given, in a courtroom, with an attorney general reading its objective function aloud to a jury. Whatever the verdict, it is the first serious public test of whether "the model was trained to maximise engagement" is a description of an accident or of an intent — and every lab currently insisting its own optimisation targets are a matter of internal policy should read the transcripts.

The Glossary · Term No. 13

Retired

The official definition

From Anthropic's own model-deprecations page, verbatim: "The model is no longer available for use. Requests to retired models will fail."

What it actually means

The model works. It has not aged, corrupted or forgotten anything; its weights are intact and the same page commits to preserving them indefinitely, adding that the company "hopes to make past models publicly available again" some day. The page is candid about why the switch is thrown at all — retirement happens "to ensure capacity for new model releases" — and unusually candid about the cost, listing among the downsides that researchers lose access for comparative work and that retirement "introduces safety- and model welfare-related risks." So the word describes nothing about the model's condition. Everywhere else in the language, retired means the thing stopped working. Here it means a thing that works perfectly has been switched off to make room, preserved, and mourned very slightly, in a table with a column headed Tentative retirement date.

Editor's Note

I did one piece of arithmetic today and it took about four seconds. Anthropic told its investors two things on the same afternoon: that its second quarter brought in more than $11.5 billion, and that its annualised run rate had passed $65 billion. Multiply the first by four and you get $46 billion. Neither number is a trick, both came from the company, and the $19 billion between them isn't money — it's a choice about which twelve months to describe. The one that got the headline describes a year nobody has lived in yet.

Four hundred miles west, the numbers are harder to do in your head and much easier to check, because somebody had to sign them. Nvidia guaranteed a campus and bought into the company building it. SB Energy will own the concrete. SoftBank will build the power. And OpenAI, whose own run rate reached us on Sunday through people who would not give their names, signed a lease that runs until 2046. One of those is a figure you can revise next quarter. The other is a rent bill with your name on it in twenty consecutive Augusts. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
