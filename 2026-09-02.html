Safety · OpenAI

OpenAI Says Its Next Model Writes Zero-Days Unaided. The Number Worth Reading Is About the Model You Can Buy Today.

By The Editor, Safety Desk · September 2, 2026

OpenAI announced on Tuesday that Astra, its forthcoming frontier model, is the first it has ever placed in the Critical cybersecurity tier of its own Preparedness Framework. The company's written threshold for that tier is a model that can "identify and develop functional zero-day exploits of all severity levels in many hardened real-world critical systems without human intervention," or devise and execute end-to-end novel attack strategies against hardened targets given only a high-level goal. OpenAI says Astra meets it. In expert red-teaming it reportedly found previously unknown vulnerabilities and assembled a browser-compromise chain that escaped its sandbox and ran commands on the host machine. Credit first and at length: a company writing that sentence about its own unshipped product, unprompted, on an ordinary Tuesday, is doing something most of this industry has arranged never to have to do.

The safeguards are described in comparable detail — post-training refusals, system-level activation classifiers watching for cyberabuse patterns during inference, cross-conversation refusal training, chain-of-thought monitoring that interrupts actions outside authorised boundaries, and offline threat disruption. The headline result is that on OpenAI's own cyber jailbreak evaluation, Astra refuses 91.5% of disallowed requests. The company concluded that its safeguards "sufficiently minimize the risk of severe harm" and cleared the model for release. It also confirmed that the large frontier training run it paused on 18 August restarted on 28 August, which closes a question this paper has had open for a fortnight.

Now read the other half of that sentence. The 91.5% is a comparison, and the thing it is compared against is GPT-5.6 Sol, which refused 59%. Sol is not a research artefact or a discontinued line. It is the model OpenAI sells today, in ChatGPT and through the API, to anyone with a payment method. The most useful disclosure in a safety document about an unreleased model is the control group, and the control group is in general release.

Note also how the capability is being distributed rather than withheld. Astra's general reasoning and software engineering go to public ChatGPT and API users; the zero-day discovery and cyber-offence work is restricted to vetted security partners through a programme called Daybreak Blue. That is a sensible design and it is also the shape of the answer: the most dangerous capability the framework has ever measured is not being held back, it is being allocated. Critical, it turns out, is a tier with a customer list attached.

The reassuring number describes a model nobody can use yet. The other one describes the model on sale this morning. — on OpenAI's cyber jailbreak evaluation

Models · Google

Google Shipped Its Fourth Flash Model in Four Months. The Cyber Edition Requires an Application.

By The Editor, Models Desk · September 2, 2026

Gemini 3.8 Flash arrived on Wednesday, three weeks after the last one and, by Artificial Analysis's count, the fourth Flash release in under four months. Google calls it its "most intelligent workhorse model," claims gains over 3.7 Flash on software engineering, agentic tasks and multi-step reasoning, and has priced it — reportedly — at $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output, the same introductory rate as its predecessor. Artificial Analysis puts it at $0.58 per task on its Intelligence Index and calls it the cheapest model at its level of intelligence. It is live in the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers, in AI Mode, in Sheets, and in AI Studio, Antigravity and the API.

There is a second model in the announcement. Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber is the same core model with a different door: available only to vetted security researchers, government agencies and critical infrastructure operators, through something Google is calling the Fairwind Program. Shipping the same weights twice with two eligibility lists is now, apparently, a product category. Google has not published what the vetting consists of, which is the part worth asking about, because the vetting is the entire safety argument.

Labour · Uber

Uber Cuts 3,300 Jobs to Pay for Cars That Drive Themselves

By The Editor, Labour Desk · September 2, 2026

Uber is cutting about 3,300 roles, roughly a tenth of its global headcount and its largest reduction since the start of the pandemic, according to an internal email from chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi reported by Bloomberg on Wednesday. The shape is unusually specific: management is cut by 20%, some managers move to individual-contributor roles, teams of one or two people are halved, and the memo targets staff more than seven layers down from the chief executive. Engineering, science and delivery are being combined; delivery operations across restaurants, retail and direct are merged. The stated purpose is to redirect investment toward ridesharing, delivery and robotaxis.

A company that has spent a decade arguing it is a technology firm rather than an employer has now published a précis of its own org chart in order to explain which parts of it are surplus. Seven layers down is a long way from a robotaxi. It is, however, exactly where the money is.

Safety · Anthropic

Anthropic Will Keep Watching for Misuse. The Record of It Now Lives in the Customer's Cupboard.

By The Editor, Enterprise Desk · September 2, 2026

Anthropic announced Enterprise Frontier Safeguards on Wednesday, a configuration it describes as combining the privacy of zero data retention with the misuse detection that retention makes possible. The mechanism is a change of address. Activity data used for monitoring lands in the customer's own Amazon S3, Azure Blob or Google Cloud Storage bucket, under the customer's own encryption keys, access policies and audit logging. Detection stays with Anthropic. Custody, keys and human review go to the customer: when the automated systems flag a pattern, the signal routes to the customer's staff, and no Anthropic human review is required. It rolls out in phases to eligible customers, by request, later this autumn.

The reasoning is published and it is sound. Anthropic introduced 30-day retention with Fable 5 and says the purpose is detection quality rather than training data: the sophisticated misuse it wants to catch spreads across many tasks, sessions and accounts, so scanning each interaction alone and deleting it immediately cannot see the pattern. Regulated industries understood that and still could not sign. This is a real conflict, met with a real engineering answer, and saying so first costs nothing.

Then read the name again. This is not a return to zero retention. The retention survives — the same window, the same logs, the same cross-account scanning. What changed is the custodian, and the new custodian is the enterprise whose own employees are the population under observation. The party best placed to be embarrassed by the evidence now holds the keys to it, decides who may read it, and is the one asked to confirm the deletion worked.

Which is not an accusation, only a description. Three weeks ago this paper covered OpenAI shipping an unobserved processing tier to its enterprise customers, and relayed a framing that contrasted it with Anthropic, which required data logs. Anthropic has now answered that, and the answer is that it still requires the logs. It simply no longer keeps them.

The system that detects misuse no longer holds the record of it. The company whose staff are being watched does. — on Enterprise Frontier Safeguards
Infrastructure · Dell

Dell Booked $60.9bn of AI Orders and Shipped $16.4bn of Them

By The Editor, Capital Desk · September 2, 2026

Dell reported second-quarter revenue of $47.0bn, up 58% year on year, with non-GAAP earnings per share of $7.04, up 203%. It booked a record $60.9bn of AI server orders in the quarter — $131.7bn over the past year — and left the quarter with a $95bn backlog. Full-year revenue guidance went up by $25bn to roughly $192bn, with about $74bn of that from AI-optimised servers, and per-share guidance from $17.90 to $25.50. Management's explanation for why the backlog keeps growing is shortages: DRAM and NAND above all, with CPUs and optics behind them.

Two years of this industry's conversation has been about who can get GPUs. Dell's own account of its constraint this quarter is memory chips — the least glamorous component in the box, made by three companies, and now the thing standing between a record order book and a delivery.

Data · Venture

Y Combinator's Fastest Unicorn Sells Human Expertise by the Task

By The Editor, Markets Desk · September 2, 2026

AfterQuery, a San Francisco company that pays specialists to produce training data, is reportedly valued at $3.2bn in a new Series B — up from $300m in April, a re-rating of more than tenfold in under five months. Forbes reports the round; Y Combinator partner Gustaf Alströmer calls it the fastest launch-to-unicorn in the accelerator's history. The founders are 22 and 23 and went through the Winter 2025 batch about eighteen months ago. Nvidia is a named customer. In April the company claimed a $100m annualised revenue run rate. Neither the size of the round nor its lead investor appears to have been disclosed, which is a curious set of facts to be missing from a record.

For scale, and without further comment: Mechanical Turk, which sold human judgement by the penny for twenty-one years, closes on 30 September.

Editor's Note

Here is a sentence a company published about its own product on Tuesday. A model reaches the Critical cybersecurity threshold if it can identify and develop functional zero-day exploits of all severity levels in many hardened real-world critical systems without human intervention. That is OpenAI's wording, from OpenAI's framework, applied by OpenAI to OpenAI's next model. Nobody compelled a word of it, and I want that said flatly before anything else, because almost nobody else in this industry has arranged to owe you that sentence. What it produced was a rollout plan.

Anthropic also shipped safety this week, and it is worth noticing what a different substance it is made of. OpenAI's safeguards are refusals: things the model will not do. Anthropic's is custody: where the transcript sits after the model has already done it. One company spent the week buying prevention. The other spent it moving an evidence locker into the customer's own cloud, under the customer's own keys, and calling the result zero retention. The most serious line either of them has ever drawn was crossed this week by the company that drew it, and what came out the far side was a launch calendar and a partner list.
