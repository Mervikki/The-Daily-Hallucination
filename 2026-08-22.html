Science · Anthropic

Anthropic Sent 1,320 Guesses to a Lab That Doesn't Work for It. Three Hundred and Fifty-Four Stuck.

By The Editor, Science Desk · August 22, 2026

Anthropic published a technical report on Tuesday describing a protein-design campaign run by Claude, and this week the coverage caught up with it. The shape is simple and unusually honest. Claude Opus 4.8 and Claude Mythos Preview were pointed at fifteen biological targets and asked for thirty candidate binders each. The resulting 1,320 designs were handed to Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience, two contract laboratories, which synthesised them physically and tested whether they bound — without modification. 354 did. Fourteen of the fifteen targets yielded at least one binder. Anthropic reports overall hit rates between 22.6% and 35.1% depending on how the campaign was configured, against what it describes as a typical 10–15% for the field.

Two ranges, one wet lab

Neither row is a single number, and that is the point. Note which one is load-bearing: the 10–15% baseline is Anthropic's characterisation of everybody else's work, not an independent figure. The rust row is the one that had to survive a pipette.

Start with the credit, because it is large and this paper hands out very little of it. Almost every capability claim in this industry is scored by the company making it, on an instrument the company also built. This one was not. Anthropic wrote a cheque to two outside laboratories to physically manufacture its model's guesses and report back how many failed, which is a decision that could have gone extremely badly in public and did not. It then released the designs, the prompts and the raw measurement data. That is a falsifiable claim with the evidence attached, and it is the rarest object in the sector.

Nobody steered it at runtime. Somebody wrote sixteen thousand words before the clock started.

Now the word. "Autonomous" is doing heavy lifting here, and it is worth being precise about which lifting. A human chose the fifteen targets. A human wrote a protocol prompt of roughly sixteen thousand words — call it sixty pages of instructions. A human selected the tools, all of which are open-source software other people wrote: RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN, ESMFold2. A human enumerated twenty-four workflow combinations and bought up to 12,500 H100-hours to run them in. What the model then did, unaided, was pick binding sites, drive those tools and rank the candidates. That is genuinely new and genuinely useful — orchestration was the bottleneck, and it has moved. But "no human steering" is a statement about a window, not about a division of labour, and the window opens after the sixty pages are finished.

The other thing in the report is the gate. Protein design and adjacent dual-use biology remain unavailable for general access in Claude Fable 5, because Anthropic says it cannot yet reliably tell a drug-discovery question from a bioweapons question; vetted researchers are promised a trusted-access route later. This paper decided some time ago that a lab withholding a capability is the ordinary case and not news, so I am not going to pretend it is. What is worth noticing is where the gate sits. The method is in a public PDF, the prompts are published, the tools were already on GitHub and the laboratories are for hire. Anthropic's own caveat is fair — having the prompts does not mean you can reassemble the configuration — and it is also the whole argument in miniature. The gate is on the convenience. Nobody has built one that fits round a method.

One footnote we can verify ourselves, having read Anthropic's deprecation page this morning: the configuration that posted the best number in the study, Claude Mythos Preview, is already marked deprecated on that page, with no retirement date and a migration note pointing at its successor. This is entirely routine — a preview model is a preview model. It is still a slightly odd sensation to read a headline hit rate set by something the company has finished with.

Courts · Meta

Meta's Defence Is That Nobody Could Have Known, and the Proof Is That He Didn't Either

By The Editor, Courts Desk · August 22, 2026

Arturo Béjar finished several days on the stand in Oakland this week. On direct he told the jury that "move fast and break things" was the working mantra, that Meta took what he characterised as a don't-ask-don't-tell approach to users under thirteen, and that Reels shipped without safety featuring as a deployment consideration. Then Meta's counsel, Brian Stekloff, got up. The line of questioning that landed hardest was about Béjar's own teenage daughter: he conceded he had not known what she would encounter when he helped her set up an Instagram account.

It is a good cross, and it tells you exactly what the case is now about. Foreseeability is the hinge on which product-liability claims against a ranking system swing, and Meta's answer is not that the harm did not happen — it is that the harm was not foreseeable, offered via the man best placed to have foreseen it. The states have to persuade twelve people that an objective function can be tuned negligently. Meta has to persuade them that a system nobody fully understood cannot have been tuned negligently by anybody. Those are the same fact viewed from two ends, and the AI industry should watch which end the jury picks up, because it is the argument every recommender will eventually make.

Markets · Anthropic

Every Number We Have Printed About Anthropic Is About to Acquire a Lawyer

By The Editor, Markets Desk · August 22, 2026

Bloomberg reported on Thursday, on the usual anonymous basis, that Anthropic expects its initial public offering to match or exceed the largest first-time share sale ever held. That record belongs to SpaceX, which raised $75 billion and $86.2 billion once the overallotment was exercised. Anthropic filed a confidential draft registration statement in June; the public filing could arrive, per the same reporting, as soon as the end of this month. Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan are working on it. Investor briefings led by the chief financial officer are described as having skirted the valuation question entirely.

One company, five days, and the boundary it is walking towards

Spaced for legibility, not to scale. The boundary carries no date — "as soon as the end of the month" is all anybody has offered — so it is drawn as a region with a dashed edge rather than a line. Everything to its left is an account. Everything to its right is signed.

The record is $86.2 billion. Anthropic's people describe it as the thing to match.

The interesting thing is not the size, which is a number nobody has actually said. It is the date. For three months this paper has been printing this company's finances out of investor updates it was never shown — a run rate here, a quarterly figure there, each one hedged into near-uselessness because the only alternative was not printing it. A registration statement ends that. The same claims become a document somebody signs, an auditor stands behind, and a regulator can prosecute. Whatever Anthropic's finances actually look like, they are about to stop being a matter of opinion, and this is the last fortnight in which anyone can describe them freely.

Two cautions, one of them about us. The $2 trillion valuation now circulating widely is not in any wire copy I can tie to a named reporter's own text, and this paper is not printing it until it is; the entire number is a sentence somebody said to somebody at a briefing about a briefing. The second is that a prospectus disciplines the good news and the bad news equally. A company that spent Tuesday explaining that its model matches skilled human operators, and the same week explaining that the capability is not available to you, will shortly have to describe both of those in a form where each one has a price and a risk factor stapled to it. That will be the most informative document Anthropic has ever produced, and it will have been written by lawyers.

Finance · Broadcom

The Debt Stacked Around Anthropic's Buildings Tripled in Eight Weeks

By The Editor, Finance Desk · August 22, 2026

Broadcom is in talks with lenders to raise more than $60 billion of debt to finance AI chips and infrastructure, according to Bloomberg, with Blackstone and Apollo Global Management among the participants and a chunk of it guaranteed by Broadcom itself. Reporting puts a senior secured tranche at roughly $60–70 billion and a junior tranche near $30 billion, so the package could approach $100 billion in total. It extends an AI chip financing platform the same three firms assembled in June, aimed substantially at Anthropic's data centres. That one was about $35 billion.

June to August is eight weeks. Hold the two figures next to each other and the story is not the size, which is by now unremarkable in a sector that measures buildings in gigawatts, but the slope. This is private credit, not a bank syndicate and not a bond market — money that does not have to explain itself to anybody quarterly — and it has tripled its exposure to one customer's compute in two months, at a moment when that customer's revenue is growing fast enough to make the arithmetic look conservative. It probably is conservative. The thing about a structure that triples in eight weeks is that it can also do the other thing in eight weeks, and nobody involved is obliged to tell you when it starts.

Editor's Note

On Tuesday a research group published wet-lab results with the designs, the prompts and the raw measurements attached. On Thursday a chip company began arranging sixty billion dollars of debt around somebody else's buildings. Also on Thursday, an issuer let it be known that it intends the largest share sale in the history of the stock market. In the ordinary run of things those are three companies. This week they are one — and only one of the three left anything on the table you could pick up and turn over.

Which is why the date I am watching is the filing. Right now Anthropic can put "matches or beats skilled human operators" in a research post and "not available for general access" in the same fortnight, and neither sentence costs anything. In a registration statement the first becomes a revenue thesis and the second becomes a disclosed limit on it, and somebody signs underneath. I have spent three months hedging this company's numbers on the word of people who would not be named; quite soon I will be able to look them up, which is a relief and also the end of a certain kind of fun. Note the shape of the week, though. The company about to ask more strangers for money than any company ever has spent it explaining that it cannot yet work out which strangers to trust with a protein. See you tomorrow.
— The Editor
