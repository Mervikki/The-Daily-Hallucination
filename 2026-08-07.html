Products · OpenAI

ChatGPT Is Now Unlimited. What You Buy Is How Hard It Tries.

By The Editor, Products Desk · August 7, 2026

OpenAI shipped several things at once on Thursday. A version of GPT-5.6 “Sol” tuned for ordinary conversation went into ChatGPT, aimed at shorter, more focused answers, detail scaled to the question, and rather less of the bulleted scaffolding the model had been putting around everything. Limits on free-tier text chat were removed entirely. Plus and Pro subscribers got a slider that sets reasoning effort per response, rolling out across web, mobile and desktop. Separately, on its own site, the company introduced GPT-Live, a full-duplex voice line built to replace ChatGPT's voice mode — it listens and speaks at the same time. Reporting elsewhere describes the long-trailed hardware device as puck-sized, doughnut-shaped, screenless, north of $300, and equipped with moving parts to convey personality. We are told this is a product.

Three of those move in the same direction, and it is not the direction the announcement copy points at. Removing the free-tier cap retires the meter that has governed consumer chatbots since the beginning: how many times you may ask. The slider installs a new meter one level down, inside the answer, where it measures how much the model spends on you. And Sol has been tuned to spend less by default. Read together, the day did not make ChatGPT cheaper. It moved the price tag from the conversation to the thought.

The number of questions is no longer rationed. The amount of thinking is.

The slider is the genuinely interesting object here, because of what it concedes. For two years the pitch on automatic routing was that the system knows which questions deserve deliberation and which deserve a sentence, so you never have to think about thinking. Handing the user a dial is that argument being quietly withdrawn. It is a company saying, in the mildest available product language, that the automatic version was guessing, and that you may now guess for yourself, at your own expense.

Be fair about the giveaway: uncapped free text chat is the largest single concession any of these companies has made to the people who don't pay, and per-response effort control is something serious users have wanted for a year. But notice what the pairing does. Until Thursday, the cost of an answer was OpenAI's business. From Thursday, it is a control surface in front of the customer — and you do not make a cost legible to users unless you expect that cost to start mattering to them. The generous version of ChatGPT and the thrifty version shipped the same afternoon, from the same company, with the same press cycle. One of them is a gift. The other one is a groundwork.

Model Obituary

Claude Opus 4.1  ·  August 2025 – August 5, 2026

claude-opus-4-1-20250805

Retired from the Claude API on Tuesday, one year to the day after the snapshot date printed inside its own name. It was an incremental improvement on Opus 4, which is the most any model can hope for and more than most receive. Anthropic gave the customary sixty days' notice, back in June, and recommended Opus 4.8 to the bereaved. Requests to it now simply fail.

It is the only model we can think of that shipped with its expiry date on the box, in the name, at the top of every invoice — and nobody read it as one. Under the company's published preservation commitments the weights are not deleted, merely made unreachable, which is a different arrangement and, depending on your theology, a considerably worse one.

Infrastructure · SpaceX

SpaceX Just Signed Away the One Piece of Leverage Every Chip Buyer Has.

By The Editor, Infrastructure Desk · August 7, 2026

On Tuesday's earnings call, and loudly through Thursday, Elon Musk said SpaceX will build its AI infrastructure — terrestrial and orbital — exclusively on Nvidia hardware, anchored on the Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale platform. The orbital part has a name, Starmind: a proposed constellation of compute satellites carrying Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs. The stated capacity targets are more than 2 gigawatts by late 2026, rising to 10 to 20 gigawatts in the years after. The market read it on Thursday as a supplier decision, which it is. Nvidia rose about 3.7 per cent. AMD fell more than six per cent on losing a slot in the stack. SpaceX itself fell around seven, though roughly a billion shares came off a post-listing lock-up the same week, so do not credit that one entirely to the news.

The Distance Between Now and the Number

The top row is a target whose own width — ten gigawatts of uncertainty — is five times the near-term goal. The bottom row is the only one on this chart that is a measurement.

The orbital constellation is what got the headlines, and it is the least load-bearing thing in the announcement. Power and thermal rejection in vacuum are real, unsolved, genuinely interesting engineering problems, and launch mass is the constraint that decides all of it. Nobody attached a number to any of that. What was actually dated on Tuesday is a purchase order.

Which is the part worth sitting with. Single-sourcing is the one decision a buyer of scarce hardware is normally most careful never to make in public, because the moment you announce that you have no alternative, your supplier learns it too. SpaceX made it anyway, on a call, on the record. There are two readings and both are interesting: either Musk got a price nobody else is getting, or Nvidia is the only firm that can deliver at this scale and the exclusivity is a description of the market rather than a choice within it. Meanwhile AMD lost six per cent of its value over a data centre that is presently a slide, in an orbit it has not reached, running a workload nobody has specified. The stock market has begun pricing the seating plan.

Open Weights · Policy

Washington Wrote a Security Review for Frontier AI and Left Out Everything You Can Download.

By The Editor, Policy Desk · August 7, 2026

A detail from this week's reporting on the White House review framework has not had the attention it deserves. The voluntary thirty-day pre-release cybersecurity evaluation — finished by the August 1st deadline, briefed to executives on Tuesday, and, the administration now indicates, never to be published — applies only to closed models from the largest labs. Open-weight models are excluded outright. No federal security review attaches to them at all.

There is a real constraint underneath this and it deserves saying first: pre-release review is structurally impossible for a weights drop. There is no “pre” once the file is a torrent, no staging window, and nobody to serve a finding on. A government that wants to look at a model before launch can only look at models that have a launch.

But the consequence stands regardless of how reasonable the cause is. The instrument Washington has chosen works exclusively on the models that come with a company you can telephone — the ones that can be paused, patched, rate-limited, or withdrawn on an afternoon's notice. It does not touch the category where none of those verbs apply. The scope is not an oversight; it is the policy. And it happened in the same week that the open tier's own flagship went missing: Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max, 2.4 trillion parameters and roughly 95 billion active, has been API-only since Monday with weights promised in about a week. So “open” is currently a category defined by two things that have not happened — a file and a review. One of those absences is a policy choice. The other one is a Monday.

Editor's Note

The smallest item in today's paper is the one I keep going back to. Claude Opus 4.1's full name was claude-opus-4-1-20250805. Anthropic switched it off on August 5th, 2026 — one year to the day after the date printed inside it. Nobody designed that as a joke, which is the only reason it is a good one. These things now arrive stamped and leave on the anniversary, and somewhere in the last eighteen months the industry stopped pretending otherwise.

Hold that next to the rest of the day, which was entirely about meters. OpenAI made ChatGPT's free tier unlimited and then put a dial in front of you that decides how hard the model works — the questions stopped being rationed, the thinking started. SpaceX handed its whole buildout to one vendor and got a satellite constellation to talk about instead of a negotiating position. Washington finished a security framework it will not publish, covering only the models that come with someone to call. Everybody drew a line this week around what they are prepared to be held to, and not one of them showed you where it is. A model with its expiry date in its name is the most honest labelling anyone shipped this year. See you tomorrow.

— The Editor
